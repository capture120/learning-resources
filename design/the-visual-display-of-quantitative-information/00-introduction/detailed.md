# Introduction (and Introduction to the Second Edition) — Detailed Reference

Source: Edward R. Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, 2nd edition (Graphics Press, Cheshire, Connecticut, 2001). This file covers the front matter that frames the entire book: the "Introduction to the Second Edition" (pp. 8–9), the original "Introduction" (pp. 10–11), and the Part I divider page (p. 12). These pages contain no figures, charts, or footnotes — they are pure text, set in the book's characteristic layout of a main text column with a wide, here-empty, right-hand sidenote margin. Because the chapter is short and every sentence is programmatic, this file reproduces or closely paraphrases essentially all of it, with Tufte's exact wording quoted wherever the phrasing carries weight.

## Position in the book and what these pages do

The book opens with two separate introductions written nineteen years apart. The "Introduction to the Second Edition" (dated January 2001, Cheshire, Connecticut) is autobiographical and historical: it tells how the book came to exist, why Tufte self-published it, and what the second edition changes. The original "Introduction" (1983) is programmatic: it defines the subject (data graphics), states the book's central thesis (graphics as instruments for reasoning), lays out the two-part structure of the book, claims universality for its principles, quantifies the stakes, and announces the book's spirit (a celebration). Page 12 is a divider page reading only "PART I / Graphical Practice," marking the transition into Chapter 1.

The two introductions reinforce each other: the 2001 preface shows the book *practicing* what the 1983 introduction *preaches* — the physical book is offered as evidence for its own design principles (Tufte's term: "self-exemplifying").

## Introduction to the Second Edition (pp. 8–9)

### What the second edition changes (p. 8)

The opening paragraph is a complete changelog in one sentence: "This new edition provides high-resolution color reproductions of the many graphics of William Playfair, adds color to other images where appropriate, and includes all the changes and corrections accumulated during the 17 printings of the first edition." (p. 8)

Three distinct changes, then:

1. High-resolution **color** reproductions of William Playfair's many graphics (the first edition reproduced them less well and without color).
2. Color added to **other images** "where appropriate" — color is added selectively, not globally.
3. All changes and corrections accumulated across **17 printings** of the first edition — a quiet testament both to the book's longevity (1983–2001) and to Tufte's practice of continuous revision, which the original Introduction names as a core design method ("the sequential improvement of graphics through revision and editing," p. 11).

### Origins: Princeton, 1975 (p. 8)

"This book began in 1975 when Dean Donald Stokes of Princeton's Woodrow Wilson School asked me to teach statistics to a dozen journalists who were visiting that year to learn some economics." (p. 8)

The concrete particulars: the year is **1975**; the commissioner is **Dean Donald Stokes**; the institution is **Princeton's Woodrow Wilson School**; the students are **a dozen journalists** visiting to learn economics. For the course, Tufte "annotated a collection of readings, with a long section on statistical graphics." (p. 8)

Then comes the book's founding grievance — Tufte's assessment of the field's literature as he found it in 1975: "The literature here was thin, too often grimly devoted to explaining use of the ruling pen and to promulgating 'graphic standards' indifferent to the nature of visual evidence and quantitative reasoning." (p. 8)

Every clause of that sentence carries a claim worth unpacking:

- **"thin"** — there was simply little serious writing on statistical graphics.
- **"grimly devoted to explaining use of the ruling pen"** — what existed was drafting instruction, technique manuals about the physical tools of chart-making, not thought about what charts are for.
- **"promulgating 'graphic standards'"** — the scare quotes are Tufte's. The existing prescriptive rules were standards in name only.
- **"indifferent to the nature of visual evidence and quantitative reasoning"** — the decisive charge, and the implicit definition of what design guidance *should* be grounded in. This phrase introduces two of the book's master concepts — *visual evidence* and *quantitative reasoning* — as the criteria any graphical standard must answer to.

"Soon I wrote up some ideas." (p. 8)

### John Tukey and the field's legitimacy (p. 8)

"Then John Tukey, the phenomenal Princeton statistician, suggested that we give a series of joint seminars." (p. 8) Tufte then credits Tukey with founding the modern field: "Since the mid-1960s, Tukey had opened up the field, as his brilliant technical contributions made it clear that the study of statistical graphics was intellectually respectable and not just about pie charts and ruling pens." (p. 8)

The claims embedded here: (1) the modern study of statistical graphics dates from the **mid-1960s** and from Tukey specifically; (2) what changed the field's status was **technical contribution**, not advocacy; (3) the prior reputation of the field — "pie charts and ruling pens" — pairs a graphical form Tufte holds in low regard with the drafting-tool obsession he has just criticized. (Outside this chapter's pages, the second edition's dedication page reads "To the memory of / John W. Tukey (1915–2000)," alongside the dedication "For my parents / Edward E. Tufte and Virginia James Tufte.")

### The manuscript and the publishing impasse (p. 8)

"After moving to Yale University, I finished the manuscript in 1982." (p. 8) Then the conflict with conventional publishing, in two parts.

First, economics: "A publisher was interested but planned to print only 2,000 copies and to charge a very high price, contrary to my hopes for a wide readership." (p. 8) The numbers: a print run of **2,000 copies** at "a very high price," against Tufte's explicit goal of wide readership.

Second, design control. Tufte states the design ambition that became one of the book's signature ideas: "I also sought to design the book so as to make it *self-exemplifying* — that is, the physical object itself would reflect the intellectual principles advanced in the book." (p. 8) The italics on *self-exemplifying* are Tufte's; the sentence after the dash is his own gloss, i.e., his definition of the term. The publishers' reaction: "Publishers seemed appalled at the prospect that an author might govern design." (p. 8)

### Self-publishing: Gralla, the garage, the mortgage, the elephant (pp. 8–9)

"Consequently I investigated self-publishing. This required a first-rate book designer, a lot of money (at least for a young professor), and a large garage." (p. 8) Note the three requirements, the third deadpan: a garage large enough to warehouse the print run.

The designer: "I found Howard Gralla who had designed many museum catalogs with great care and craft. He was willing to work closely with this difficult author who was filled with all sorts of opinions about design and typography." (p. 8) Tufte's self-description — "this difficult author" — is a rare joke at his own expense, and also a signal: the author held strong, specific opinions about design and typography and insisted on them.

The working method: "We spent the summer in his studio laying out the book, page by page." (pp. 8–9) The result of that page-by-page control is stated as a direct consequence: "We were able to integrate graphics right into the text, sometimes into the middle of a sentence, eliminating the usual separation of text and image — one of the ideas *Visual Display* advocated." (p. 9) This is the self-exemplification made concrete: a layout practice (no segregated figures; image and prose interwoven, even mid-sentence) that is simultaneously one of the book's own advocated principles.

The financing, with the book's best anecdote: "To finance the book I took out another mortgage on my home. The bank officer said this was the second most unusual loan that she had ever made; first place belonged to a loan to a circus to buy an elephant!" (p. 9)

### Tufte's standard, and the payoff (p. 9)

Two short closing paragraphs, both quotable in full.

On standards of work: "My view on self-publishing was to go all out, to make the best and most elegant and wonderful book possible, without compromise. Otherwise, why do it?" (p. 9)

On what the project gave him: "Most of all, the book, as a thing in itself, gave to me fresh new eyes for the intellectual and aesthetic joy of visual evidence, visual reasoning, and visual understanding." (p. 9)

That last sentence supplies the triad that frames the book's whole subject — **visual evidence, visual reasoning, visual understanding** — and yokes "intellectual" to "aesthetic": the joy of graphics is both at once. The phrase "the book, as a thing in itself" again insists on the physical object as carrier of the argument.

The preface is signed with a dateline: "*January 2001 / Cheshire, Connecticut*" (p. 9) — Cheshire being both Tufte's home and the seat of his Graphics Press.

## Introduction (pp. 10–11)

### The opening definition of data graphics (p. 10)

The book's first sentence is its foundational definition: "Data graphics visually display measured quantities by means of the combined use of points, lines, a coordinate system, numbers, symbols, words, shading, and color." (p. 10)

The definition enumerates the complete toolkit — eight elements: **points, lines, a coordinate system, numbers, symbols, words, shading, and color** — and stresses their **combined** use. Two restrictions are built in: the quantities displayed are *measured* (data graphics are empirical), and the display is *visual*. Note that *words* and *numbers* appear inside the definition itself: text is a component of data graphics, not a competitor to them, anticipating the book's later insistence on integrating words, numbers, and pictures.

### The late invention of statistical graphics (p. 10)

The second paragraph makes a historical claim with an explanatory conjecture attached: "The use of abstract, non-representational pictures to show numbers is a surprisingly recent invention, perhaps because of the diversity of skills required — the visual-artistic, empirical-statistical, and mathematical." (p. 10)

The claim: showing numbers with abstract, non-representational pictures is recent. The hedged explanation ("perhaps"): the invention required three different competencies at once — **visual-artistic, empirical-statistical, and mathematical** — and that combination is rare. Tufte offers this as conjecture, not settled history.

He then dates the invention and sharpens the surprise by contrast with mathematics: "It was not until 1750–1800 that statistical graphics — length and area to show quantity, time-series, scatterplots, and multivariate displays — were invented, long after such triumphs of mathematical ingenuity as logarithms, Cartesian coordinates, the calculus, and the basics of probability theory." (p. 10)

Two enumerations to keep exact:

- The fundamental graphical inventions of **1750–1800**: (1) length and area to show quantity, (2) time-series, (3) scatterplots, (4) multivariate displays.
- The earlier mathematical triumphs they trailed: (1) logarithms, (2) Cartesian coordinates, (3) the calculus, (4) the basics of probability theory.

The rhetorical point of the contrast: humanity had calculus before it had the time-series chart. Abstractly picturing numbers is genuinely hard — a real intellectual achievement, not a clerical afterthought to analysis.

### William Playfair and "linear arithmetic" (p. 10)

The paragraph closes by naming the field's founding figure: "The remarkable William Playfair (1759–1823) developed or improved upon nearly all the fundamental graphical designs, seeking to replace conventional tables of numbers with the systematic visual representations of his 'linear arithmetic.'" (p. 10)

The particulars: **William Playfair, 1759–1823**; his achievement covers "nearly all the fundamental graphical designs," and Tufte's verb pair "developed or improved upon" is careful — Playfair did not invent everything from nothing. His program: replace conventional **tables of numbers** with systematic visual representations. His own name for the method, which Tufte quotes: **"linear arithmetic."** Playfair thus personifies the table-versus-graphic opposition that runs through the book, and he is the figure whose graphics the second edition newly reproduces in high-resolution color (p. 8).

### The thesis: graphics as instruments for reasoning (p. 10)

The third paragraph is the book's thesis statement, building in four steps:

1. The negative framing — graphics exceed their humblest use: "Modern data graphics can do much more than simply substitute for small statistical tables." (p. 10) The implied baseline is that many people treat graphics as exactly that — a prettier table.
2. The positive thesis, the most-quoted sentence of the chapter: "At their best, graphics are instruments for reasoning about quantitative information." (p. 10) Graphics are *instruments* — working tools of thought — not illustrations or decoration. The qualifier "at their best" concedes that not all graphics achieve this; the book exists to close that gap.
3. The practical corollary: "Often the most effective way to describe, explore, and summarize a set of numbers — even a very large set — is to look at pictures of those numbers." (p. 10) Three named tasks — **describe, explore, summarize** — and an explicit extension to very large data sets, where one might assume pictures break down.
4. The comparative superlative: "Furthermore, of all methods for analyzing and communicating statistical information, well-designed data graphics are usually the simplest and at the same time the most powerful." (p. 10) The claim ranges over *all* methods of analysis and communication; the condition is "well-designed"; the hedge is "usually"; and the punchline is the conjunction — simplest **and at the same time** most powerful. Simplicity and power are not a trade-off; the best graphics maximize both at once.

### Plan of Part I: Graphical Practice (p. 10)

A visibly separate section of the Introduction (set off by extra vertical space on the page) turns to the book's structure.

"The first part of this book reviews the graphical practice of the two centuries since Playfair." (p. 10) Then the emotional itinerary Tufte plans for the reader, chapter by chapter: "The reader will, I hope, rejoice in the graphical glories shown in Chapter 1 and then condemn the lapses and lost opportunities exhibited in Chapter 2. Chapter 3, on graphical integrity and sophistication, seeks to account for these differences in quality of graphical design." (p. 10)

So Part I has a three-beat dramatic structure: **rejoice** (Chapter 1, graphical glories / excellence), **condemn** (Chapter 2, lapses and lost opportunities — the integrity failures), then **explain** (Chapter 3, graphical integrity and sophistication, which accounts for *why* quality varies). Note the verbs: Tufte scripts the reader's responses, and Chapter 3 is framed as causal explanation, not just more examples.

### Plan of Part II: a language and a practical theory (p. 11)

"The second part of the book provides a language for discussing graphics and a practical theory of data graphics." (p. 11) Two deliverables: a **language** (vocabulary for talking about graphics at all) and a **practical theory** (not merely descriptive).

What the theory does, in Tufte's enumeration: "Applying to most visual displays of quantitative information, the theory leads to changes and improvements in design, suggests why some graphics might be better than others, and generates new types of graphics." (p. 11) Three outputs: (1) design improvements, (2) explanations of comparative quality, (3) generation of *new* graphic types. A theory that can generate novel forms, not just judge existing ones, is doing real work.

The method behind the theory: "The emphasis is on maximizing principles, empirical measures of graphical performance, and the sequential improvement of graphics through revision and editing." (p. 11) Three methodological commitments:

- **Maximizing principles** — the theory will be stated as quantities to maximize (foreshadowing the data-ink ratio and similar formulations in Part II), not as checklists.
- **Empirical measures of graphical performance** — graphics can be measured, and design judged against measurement.
- **Sequential improvement through revision and editing** — graphics are improved iteratively, the way prose is edited; design is a process, not a one-shot act.

Then the sources of insight, reaching outside statistics entirely: "Insights into graphical design are to be gained, I believe, from theories of what makes for excellence in art, architecture, and prose." (p. 11) The triad — **art, architecture, prose** — declares the book's interdisciplinary footing and signals (with "I believe") that this is Tufte's distinctive bet.

### What the book is about; universality of the principles (p. 11)

Another spaced-off section steps back to characterize the whole book.

"This is a book about the design of statistical graphics and, as such, it is concerned both with design and with statistics. But it is also about how to communicate information through the simultaneous presentation of words, numbers, and pictures." (p. 11) The book sits at the design–statistics intersection, but its broader subject is the **simultaneous presentation of words, numbers, and pictures** — the integration principle again, here elevated to a description of the book's whole topic.

The universality claim, with its memorable analogy: "The design of statistical graphics is a universal matter — like mathematics — and is not tied to the unique features of a particular language." (p. 11) Graphical design transcends natural languages the way mathematics does; the principles are not English-specific, genre-specific, or field-specific.

Scope of the apparatus: "The descriptive concepts (a vocabulary for graphics) and the principles advanced apply to most designs." (p. 11) — "most," not all; Tufte consistently hedges his scope claims.

And the evidence he offers for that scope: "I have at times provided evidence about the scope of these ideas, by showing how frequently a principle applies to (a random sample of) news and scientific graphics." (p. 11) The parenthetical "(a random sample of)" matters: Tufte claims a sampling methodology, not cherry-picked examples — frequency counts over randomly sampled news and scientific graphics, which Part II indeed delivers.

### The worldwide volume of statistical graphics: the stakes (p. 11)

Tufte quantifies why any of this matters: "Each year, the world over, somewhere between 900 billion (9 × 10¹¹) and 2 trillion (2 × 10¹²) images of statistical graphics are printed." (p. 11) The estimate is given in both words and scientific notation — **9 × 10¹¹ to 2 × 10¹²** printed images of statistical graphics per year, worldwide.

The consequence: "The principles of this book apply to most of those graphics. Some of the suggested changes are small, but others are substantial, with consequences for hundreds of billions of printed pages." (p. 11) The arithmetic of the stakes: if principles apply to most of ~10¹² annual images, even small changes propagate to "hundreds of billions of printed pages."

### The closing: never the same way again; a celebration (p. 11)

The Introduction ends by stating its ambition for the reader and then its spirit.

The ambition: "But I hope also that the book has consequences for the viewers and makers of those images — that they will never view or create statistical graphics the same way again." (p. 11) Note the dual audience named throughout: **viewers and makers** — the book aims to change both consumption and production of graphics.

The mechanism for that change is exposure to masterworks: "That is in part because we are about to see, collected here, so many wonderful drawings, those of Playfair, of Minard, of Marey, and, nowadays, of the computer." (p. 11) The honor roll: **Playfair, Minard, Marey** — the three historical masters whose work anchors Chapter 1 — plus "the computer" as the contemporary fourth, treated grammatically as a peer draftsman. The implicit pedagogy: studying exemplary graphics is itself a way to learn design, alongside theory ("in part because").

The final sentence, a single-sentence paragraph: "Most of all, then, this book is a celebration of data graphics." (p. 11) After the definitions, the theory-promises, and the stakes, the last word reframes the whole project as celebratory rather than censorious — a counterweight to the condemnation scripted for Chapter 2.

## Part I divider (p. 12)

Page 12 carries only the part title, centered on an otherwise blank page: "PART I" in small capitals above "Graphical Practice" in large upright (roman) serif type. No other content. It marks the boundary between the front matter and Chapter 1 ("Graphical Excellence").

## Argument structure of the Introduction, in summary

The original Introduction runs a tight five-move argument across two pages:

1. **Define** the object of study (the eight-element definition of data graphics).
2. **Historicize** it: the invention was late (1750–1800, after calculus), hard (three skill families required — offered as conjecture), and personified (Playfair's "linear arithmetic" versus tables).
3. **State the thesis**: graphics are instruments for reasoning — better than table-substitution, often the most effective way to describe/explore/summarize even very large data sets, and usually simultaneously the simplest and most powerful of all methods.
4. **Preview the book**: Part I (rejoice → condemn → explain across Chapters 1–3); Part II (a language plus a practical theory built on maximizing principles, empirical performance measures, and revision/editing, with insight imported from art, architecture, and prose).
5. **Claim scope and stakes, then set the tone**: principles universal like mathematics, tested against random samples of news and scientific graphics; 9 × 10¹¹–2 × 10¹² printed graphics a year; ambition to change viewers and makers permanently; spirit of celebration.

The Second-Edition introduction adds the genetic argument: the book's own history (thin 1975 literature → Tukey's legitimation → 1982 manuscript → publishers' refusal → self-publishing with Gralla) explains and exemplifies its principles, above all *self-exemplification* and the integration of text and image.
