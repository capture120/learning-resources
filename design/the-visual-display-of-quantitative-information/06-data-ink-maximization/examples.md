# Chapter 6 Examples: Data-Ink Maximization and Graphical Design

### Spear's range bar and Tukey's box plot (p. 123)
The chapter's starting material. Mary Eleanor Spear's "range bar" (from *Charting Statistics*, 1952) is a horizontal bar showing the full range of a distribution with a hatched box for the interquartile range and a tick for the median. John W. Tukey's "box plot" (from *Exploratory Data Analysis*, 1977) is the now-familiar vertical box spanning the quartiles with a median line and whiskers running to the minimum and maximum. Tufte's point: both designs encode exactly five numbers (min, lower quartile, median, upper quartile, max) yet spend considerable ink on box outlines and caps. They can be mostly erased without losing any information.

### The quartile plot — redesigned box plot (p. 124)
Tufte's erased replacement for the box plot. A single line with breaks at the quartiles and a dot at the median shows the same five numbers. It needs one placing of the straightedge instead of six, and it can serve as a scatterplot frame, which the box plot cannot. He shows an alternative variant — dots marking the extremes, with a line through the middle of the distribution — and rejects it: it will not work effectively to frame a scatterplot, nor does it look very good. To emphasize the middle half of the distribution he tries heavier line weight for the interquartile segment, then settles on the preferred form: the middle half offset slightly sideways. Lesson: erasing generates several candidate designs, and the final choice rests on statistical and aesthetic criteria — *reasonable* data-ink maximizing.

### The parallel schematic plot (p. 125)
A row of ten conventional box plots (a "parallel schematic plot") comparing ten distributions side by side. Drawing the original required 80 separate placings of the straightedge — 50 horizontals and 30 verticals. The erased version, ten quartile plots drawn as simple broken vertical lines with median dots, needs only 10 verticals to show the same information. Lesson: the drawing-effort reduction matters for informal, exploratory data analysis, where the researcher's time should go to analysis rather than drawing lines.

### Standard bar chart vs. Playfair's Scotland chart (p. 126)
Tufte shows the standard model bar chart endorsed by the style sheets of statistical and scientific publications — boxed frame, full vertical axis, ticks, solid bars — and sets it beside William Playfair's original 1781 chart of the exports and imports of Scotland to and from seventeen places (from the *Commercial and Political Atlas*). The architecture has barely changed in two centuries. This sets up the redesign sequence: the convention is old habit, not optimized design.

### The bar chart erasing sequence and the white grid (p. 127-128)
A step-by-step redesign of the standard bar chart. First the surrounding box is erased; then the vertical axis line, leaving only ticks. Then part of the data measures themselves is erased: thin white lines drawn through the bars at each labeled level create a *white grid*, which shows the coordinate lines more precisely than ticks alone. The white grid in turn eliminates the tick marks, since the numerical labels (5%, 10%, 15%) tie directly to the white lines. Finally the baseline can be erased because the bars themselves define the bottom end-point — though Tufte notes the thick-bar-meets-thin-baseline intersection is attractive (while producing an optical illusion of gray dots) and concedes "a thin baseline looks good." The end state: a bar chart with no frame, no vertical axis, no ticks, and a white grid.

### Variable-width notched box plot, redesigned (p. 129)
A computer-drawn "Variable Width Notched Box Plot" from Robert McGill, John W. Tukey, and Wayne A. Larsen, "Variations of Box Plots," *American Statistician*, 32 (1978), 12-16: a month's telephone bill plotted on a logarithmic y-scale against years lived in Chicago, with box width proportional to the square root of group size and non-overlapping notches indicating a significant difference at a rough 95% level. The original is heavy with frame, axis ink, and all-caps computer lettering. Tufte redrew it using the techniques just developed — no frame, no vertical axis, no ticks, quartile-plot-style data measures with gray bands of varying width replacing the boxes. Lesson: the erasing techniques from the bar chart redesign (no frame, no axis, no ticks, white grid) generalize to other statistical designs.

### The range-frame scatterplot (p. 130-131)
A standard bivariate scatterplot whose frame lines customarily run to arbitrary round numbers. Tufte trims each frame line so it extends only between the measured minimum and maximum of that variable. The result, the *range-frame*, makes the frame itself report data: the max, min, and range of both x and y are now shown explicitly rather than estimated. A labeled diagram marks min X, max X, min Y, max Y at the frame's endpoints. Comparing a conventional scatterplot with its range-frame version, only the tails of the frame differ — yet some non-data-ink has been erased and the rest "has gone over to the side of data-ink." The design needs no decoding instructions and should replace the non-data-bearing frame in many applications.

### The quartile-plot frame scatterplot (p. 132)
A small shift in the range-frame's remaining ink turns each frame line into a quartile plot (offset or broken at the quartiles, gap or dot at the median). The scatterplot frame now displays ten extra numbers: minimum, maximum, both quartiles, and the median for each variable. Tufte judges this design "nearly always better than the conventionally framed scatterplot," useful both for exploratory analysis and for publication when marginal distributions matter.

### One-dimensional range-frame on a time-series (p. 132)
A time-series line chart (a roughly decade-long percentage series, shown with vertical scales on both sides) in which the vertical frame lines span only the historical high and low of the series. The frame thus reports the record extremes at a glance. Tufte calls this "an excellent practice" that "should be used widely in all sorts of displays, both scientific and unscientific."

### The dot-dash-plot (p. 133)
The full conversion of frame into data: the bivariate scatter is framed by the marginal distribution of each variable, drawn as a fringe of short dashes along each axis — one dash per observation, projected onto x and onto y. A point (xi, yi) in the scatter corresponds to one dash at xi on the horizontal fringe and one at yi on the vertical fringe. The name follows tradition, since scatterplots were once called "dot diagrams" (as in R. A. Fisher's *Statistical Methods for Research Workers*, 1925). The dot-dash-plot combines the two fundamental designs of statistical analysis — the marginal frequency distribution and the bivariate distribution — and makes routine what good analysts already do: viewing joint and marginal distributions together.

### Cuthbert Daniel's cumulative distribution of residuals (p. 133)
From Cuthbert Daniel, *Applications of Statistics to Industrial Experimentation* (1976): an empirical cumulative distribution of residuals plotted on a normal-probability grid. The main plot shows the outer 18 terms plus the 30th term, while all 60 points appear in the marginal distribution along the side. Lesson: a real published example of combining a bivariate display with its marginal distribution, in the spirit of the dot-dash-plot.

### Pulsar signal graphic (p. 134)
From Timothy H. Hankins and Barney J. Rickett, "Pulsar Signal Processing," in *Methods in Computational Physics, Volume 14: Radio Astronomy* (1975): a data-rich display of narrowband spectra of individual pulsar subpulses. The center panel shows intensity over time and frequency as stacked traces; the right margin plots, for each moment, the intensity summed across the receiver bandwidth; the top margin plots the spectrum averaged over the pulse. Lesson: a strong scientific example of showing both marginal distributions alongside the joint data.

### The rugplot (p. 135)
A new design built from the dot-dash-plot's fringe of dashes: several bivariate scatterplots are tiled together edge to edge, each pair of adjacent plots sharing a variable, with the fringes of dashes carrying the one-dimensional projections between them. (Named because it resembles a set of fringed rugs — "and covers the statistical ground.") The dashes lead the eye to see how each plot filters and translates the data into the next; each bivariate scatter can be read as the imperfect empirical trace of an underlying curve transforming one variable into another. The sequence of variables can wander off in any direction, and the quantitative history of a single observation can be traced through a series of one- and two-dimensional contexts.
