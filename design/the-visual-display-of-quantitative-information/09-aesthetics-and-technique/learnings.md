# Chapter 9: Aesthetics and Technique in Data Graphical Design

Beauty in data graphics comes from simple design displaying complex data, not from decoration. This chapter gives the practical craft rules — choosing between sentence, table, and graphic; integrating words with pictures; making graphics "friendly"; balancing line weights; and proportioning the overall shape — that lift routine, workaday displays to graphical elegance.

## What Makes Graphical Elegance (p. 177)

The central principle:

> "Graphical elegance is often found in simplicity of design and complexity of data."

Attractive graphics also draw power from content beyond the immediate numbers: "The best graphics are about the useful and important, about life and death, about the universe. Beautiful graphics do not traffic with the trivial." (p. 177)

There are no compositional rules for producing the one spectacular graphic in millions (Barnett Newman: "Aesthetics is for the artist like ornithology is for the birds"). But there are guides for enhancing routine designs. Attractive displays of statistical information (p. 177):

- have a properly chosen format and design
- use words, numbers, and drawing together
- reflect a balance, a proportion, a sense of relevant scale
- display an accessible complexity of detail
- often have a narrative quality, a story to tell about the data
- are drawn in a professional manner, with the technical details of production done with care
- avoid content-free decoration, including chartjunk

## The Choice of Design: Sentences, Text-Tables, Tables, Semi-Graphics, and Graphics (pp. 178–180)

The substantive content, extensiveness of labels, and volume and ordering of data determine which display method to choose. The basic structures are the **sentence**, the **table**, and the **graphic**; often combine two or three. (p. 178)

Rules:

- **Sentences fail beyond two numbers.** A conventional sentence prevents comparisons within the data; its linear flow of words, folded over at arbitrary points by column width, offers less than one effective dimension for organizing data. (p. 178)
- **Use a text-table instead**: arrange the type so the numbers line up and can be compared (e.g. Group A 53%, Group B 46%, Group C 57%). (p. 178)
- **Order by content or data values, not alphabetically.** "There are nearly always better sequences than alphabetical." (p. 178)
- **Tables are the best way to show exact numerical values**, and are preferable to graphics for many small data sets. Entries can also be arranged in semi-graphical form. (p. 178)
- **Never use pie charts.** "A table is nearly always better than a dumb pie chart; the only worse design than a pie chart is several of them," which force the viewer to compare quantities in spatial disarray both within and between pies. "Given their low data-density and failure to order numbers along a visual dimension, pie charts should never be used." (p. 178) (Bertin calls multiple pies "completely useless.")
- **Use a supertable for many localized comparisons.** A large, carefully ruled and ordered table (the *supertable*) attracts and intrigues readers through its organized, sequential detail and reference-like quality. "One supertable is far better than a hundred little bar charts." (p. 179)
- **For sets of highly labeled numbers, a wordy data graphic — coming close to straight text — works well.** (p. 180)

## Making Complexity Accessible: Combining Words, Numbers, and Pictures (pp. 180–182)

Words and pictures belong together; their separation is an artifact of craft-union production conventions, not of how information is best conveyed. (p. 180)

- **Words on graphics are data-ink**, making effective use of space freed by erasing redundant and non-data-ink. (p. 180)
- It is nearly always helpful to **write little messages on the plotting field** to explain the data, **label outliers and interesting data points**, **write equations and sometimes tables on the graphic itself**, and **integrate the caption and legend into the design** so the eye need not dart between text and graphic. (p. 180)
- Type on and around graphics can be quite small, since phrases are short and won't fatigue viewers. (pp. 180–181)

The principle of **data/text integration** (p. 181):

> "Data graphics are paragraphs about data and should be treated as such."

Consequences (p. 181):

- Run tables and graphics into the text whenever possible; avoid the clumsy, diverting segregation of "See Fig. 2" (and "Fig." itself is an ugly abbreviation not worth the two spaces saved).
- If a display is discussed in several places, print it afresh near each reference, perhaps reduced in later showings.
- Use the **same typeface for text and graphic**.
- **Avoid ruled lines separating different types of information.**
- Multiple story-lines or levels of presentation should be a deliberate design judgment, not a by-product of production conventions. Graphics scattered out of sequence are as bad as paragraphs of words would be scattered out of sequence.

Historical note: Biderman observes that illustrations were once well integrated with text (Newton, Leonardo), but printing technology segregated statistical graphics from text and table, contributing to their intellectual marginality and handing design decisions to "cosmeticians and merchandisers" rather than analysts. (p. 181)

**Caveat — match words to purpose** (p. 182): decide whether the graphic is for communication of a settled finding or for exploration of a data set. Words powerfully steer viewer attention. For exploratory data analysis, words should tell the viewer *how* to read the design (if technically complex), not *what* to read in terms of content.

## Accessible Complexity: The Friendly Data Graphic (p. 183)

A *friendly data graphic* shows such care in design that it is particularly accessible and open to the eye, as if the designer had the viewer in mind at every turn. The specific differences:

| Friendly | Unfriendly |
|---|---|
| words are spelled out; mysterious and elaborate encoding avoided | abbreviations abound, requiring the viewer to sort through text to decode them |
| words run from left to right, the usual direction for reading occidental languages | words run vertically (particularly along the Y-axis) or in several directions |
| little messages help explain data | graphic is cryptic, requires repeated references to scattered text |
| elaborately encoded shadings, cross-hatching, and colors are avoided; instead, labels are placed on the graphic itself; no legend is required | obscure codings require going back and forth between legend and graphic |
| graphic attracts viewer, provokes curiosity | graphic is repellent, filled with chartjunk |
| colors, if used, are chosen so that color-deficient and color-blind viewers (5 to 10 percent of viewers) can make sense of the graphic (blue can be distinguished from other colors by most color-deficient people) | design insensitive to color-deficient viewers; red and green used for essential contrasts |
| type is clear, precise, modest; lettering may be done by hand | type is clotted, overbearing |
| type is upper-and-lower case, with serifs | type is all capitals, sans serif |

Typography support (Josef Albers): the more letters are differentiated from each other, the easier the reading; all-capital words are the most difficult to read because of equal height, volume, and width; serif letters read more easily than sans-serif. The fashionable preference for sans-serif in text "shows neither historical nor practical competence." (p. 183)

## Proportion and Scale: Line Weight and Lettering (pp. 184–186)

- **Balance relative proportions.** Graphical elements look better together when their relative proportions are in balance, producing an integrated quality and visual linkage between elements. (p. 184)
- Symptoms of imbalance to avoid: clotted ink, poor lettering style, a puffed-up display of a small data set, coarse texture, and mismatch between drawing and surrounding text. (p. 184)
- **Lines in data graphics should be thin.** Eighteenth- and nineteenth-century graphics look good partly because copper-plate engraving produced a characteristic hair-thin line; twentieth-century drafting pens thickened linework, making it clumsy and unattractive. (p. 185)
- **Use orthogonal intersections of lines of different weights** as an aesthetic device; constant-width lines dilute a design's character. (p. 185)
- **The heavier line should be a data measure.** In a time-series step chart, the data line should receive greater weight than the connecting verticals. (p. 186)

> "The contrast in line weight represents contrast in meaning. The greater meaning is given to the greater line weight." (p. 186)

This is a restatement, in different language, of the principle of data-ink maximization.

## Proportion and Scale: The Shape of Graphics (pp. 186–190)

**Graphics should tend toward the horizontal, greater in length than height.** (p. 186) Five lines of reasoning:

1. **Analogy to the horizon.** The eye is naturally practiced in detecting deviations from the horizon; horizontally stretched time-series are more accessible to the eye. A shaded, high-contrast display may occasionally beat the "floating snake" of an unanchored line — but shading should be calm, without moiré effects. (pp. 186–187)
2. **Ease of labeling.** It is easier to write and to read left-to-right words on a horizontally stretched plotting field. (p. 187)
3. **Emphasis on causal influence.** Many graphics plot effect (vertical) against cause (horizontal); a longer horizontal elaborates the workings of the causal variable in more detail. (p. 187)
4. **Tukey's counsel.** Diagnostic plots — bobbling dependences or point spatters — are usually better wider than tall, making it easier for the eye to follow left to right. "Smoothly-changing curves can stand being taller than wide, but a wiggly curve needs to be wider than tall." (p. 188)
5. **Playfair's example.** 92 percent of William Playfair's 89 published graphics are wider than tall; several exceptions are his skyrocketing government-debt displays, where the data demanded a vertical shape. (p. 188)

### The Golden Section and rectangle proportions (pp. 189–190)

The Golden Section is a venerable (fifth-century B.C.) but **dubious** rule of aesthetic proportion: divide a length so the smaller part is to the greater as the greater is to the whole:

a/b = b/(a + b); with a = 1, b = (√5 + 1)/2 = 1.618…

The Golden Rectangle is therefore 1.0 high by 1.618… long. But its nice geometry is not unique — Birkhoff lists at least five rectangles with simple mathematical properties supporting aesthetic claims: ratios r = 1, 1.414 (√2), 1.618, 1.732 (√3), and 2. (p. 189)

Empirical evidence: Playfair favored proportions between 1.4 and 1.8 in about two-thirds of his graphics, with most exceptions *more horizontal* than the golden prescription. Psychological studies since 1860 show only a mild preference near the Golden Rectangle; preferred height/length ratios range from 1:1.2 to 1:2.2, and viewer responses are highly context-dependent. (pp. 189–190)

### The conclusions on shape (p. 190)

- **If the nature of the data suggests the shape of the graphic, follow that suggestion.**
- **Otherwise, move toward horizontal graphics about 50 percent wider than tall.**

## Epilogue: Designs for the Display of Information (p. 191)

The book closes immediately after this chapter with its epilogue: "Design is choice." The theory consists of principles that generate design options and guide choices among them. They should not be applied rigidly; they are not logically or mathematically certain, and

> "it is better to violate any principle than to place graceless or inelegant marks on paper."

Most principles of design should be greeted with some skepticism, "for word authority can dominate our vision, and we may come to see only through the lenses of word authority rather than with our own eyes." (p. 191)

What is sought is the clear portrayal of complexity — not the complication of the simple, but giving visual access to the subtle and the difficult: **"the revelation of the complex."**
