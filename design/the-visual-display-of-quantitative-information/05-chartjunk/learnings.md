# Chapter 5: Chartjunk: Vibrations, Grids, and Ducks

Decoration on a graphic tells the viewer nothing new. Ink that decorates rather than informs is non-data-ink or redundant data-ink, and it is often **chartjunk**. This chapter catalogs the three most widespread species of chartjunk found in scientific and technical work — unintentional optical art (moiré vibration), the dreaded grid, and the self-promoting graphical duck — and argues that all three should be forgone, because graphics stand or fall on their content, gracefully displayed.

## What Chartjunk Is (p. 107)

- The interior decoration of graphics generates a lot of ink that does not tell the viewer anything new. The purposes of decoration vary: to make the graphic appear more scientific and precise, to enliven the display, or to give the designer an opportunity to exercise artistic skills. Regardless of cause, it is all non-data-ink or redundant data-ink, and it is often chartjunk.
- Graphical decoration "comes cheaper than the hard work required to produce intriguing numbers and secure evidence" (p. 107). It prospers in technical publications as well as in commercial and media graphics.
- Claims that decoration captures "the essential spirit of the data" invoke artistic integrity to defend chartjunk. Tufte's reply: "There are better ways to portray spirits and essences than to get them all tangled up with statistical graphics" (p. 107).
- Most chartjunk involves no artistic considerations at all. It is simply conventional graphical paraphernalia routinely added to every display: over-busy grid lines and excess ticks, redundant representations of the simplest data, the debris of computer plotting, and devices generating design variation.
- Three widespread types found in scientific and technical research work: **unintentional optical art**, **the dreaded grid**, and **the self-promoting graphical duck**. Tufte deliberately draws examples from professional scientific production: "A hundred chartjunky examples from commercial and media graphics have been forgone so as to demonstrate the relevance of the critique to the professional scientific production of data graphics" (p. 107).
- The chapter opens (p. 106) with an epigraph from Jonathan Swift's indictment of 17th-century cartographers, who decorated where they lacked data: "With savage pictures fill their gaps / And o'er unhabitable downs / Place elephants for want of towns."

## Unintentional Optical Art: Moiré Vibration (pp. 107-112)

- Moiré effects arise when a design interacts with the physiological tremor of the eye, producing the distracting appearance of vibration and movement. The effect extends beyond the ink of the design to the whole page (p. 108).
- In the hands of op artists (Bridget Riley, Victor Vasarely) such effects are deliberate and eye-catching. In statistical graphics, moiré vibration is "probably the most common form of graphical clutter" and is "inevitably bad art and bad data graphics" — the noise clouds the flow of information (p. 108).
- Moiré vibration appears to be at a maximum for equally spaced bars (p. 109).
- **Actionable rules (p. 111):**
  - Replace cross-hatching with tint screens of shades of gray.
  - Label specific areas on a graphic with words rather than encoding them with hatching.
- Moiré effects have proliferated with computer graphics (in programs such as Excel). They were once generated by thin plastic transfer sheets; now the computer produces instant chartjunk (p. 111). This form of chartjunk is a twentieth-century innovation, and computer graphics are multiplying it more than ever.
- Empirical evidence of prevalence: in a random sample of issues (1980-1982) of the ten most frequently cited scientific journals, the share of graphics with moiré vibration ranged from 2% (Biochemistry, Journal of Biological Chemistry) up to 15% (Lancet), 17% (Science), and 21% (New England Journal of Medicine) (p. 110).
- Worse, the handbooks and textbooks of statistical graphics, and user's manuals for computer graphics programs, present vibrating graphics as exemplars of design — and the proportion is high and rising with recency: from 12% (Brinton, 1914) and 46% (Spear, 1952) up to 68% in the SAS/GRAPH User's Guide (1980) and 53% in the Tell-A-Graf User's Manual (1981) (p. 112).
- Against Bertin's suggestion that a designer might exploit moiré resonance to "flirt with ambiguity without succumbing to it," Tufte answers that no good examples exist: "moiré vibration is an *undisciplined* ambiguity, with an illusive, eye-straining quality that contaminates the entire graphic. It has no place in data graphical design" (p. 112).

## The Grid (pp. 112-116)

- Core principle: "the grid should usually be muted or completely suppressed so that its presence is only implicit — lest it compete with the data" (p. 112).
- Grids are mostly for the initial plotting of data at home or office, not for putting into print (pp. 112-113).
- "Dark grid lines are chartjunk. They carry no information, clutter up the graphic, and generate graphic activity unrelated to data information" (p. 113). A heavy grid can camouflage the very profile of the data (the France age-sex pyramid example).
- Doubled grid lines waste space (18% of the area in the Tukey multiwindow plot) and create optical white dots that *appear* at line intersections; redrawing without them eliminates the vibration (p. 114).
- Remedies, in increasing order of effectiveness (Marey schedule redesigns, pp. 115-116): thinning the grid lines helps a little; a **gray grid** is the better treatment.
- Exception: "When a graphic serves as a look-up table, then a grid may help in reading and interpolating. But even in this case the grids should be muted relative to the data. A gray grid works well and, with a delicate line, may promote more accurate data reconstruction than a dark grid" (p. 116).
- Practical advice on graph paper: most ready-made graph paper comes with a darkly printed grid. Use the reverse (unprinted) side, so the lines show through faintly and do not clutter the data. "If the paper is heavily gridded on both sides, throw it out" (p. 116).

## Self-Promoting Graphics: The Duck (pp. 116-121)

- Definition: "When a graphic is taken over by decorative forms or computer debris, when the data measures and structures become Design Elements, when the overall design purveys Graphical Style rather than quantitative information, then that graphic may be called a **duck**" (p. 116). The name honors the duck-form store "Big Duck" (Flanders, New York), a building whose whole structure is itself decoration.
- The architecture analogy (Venturi, Scott Brown, and Izenour, *Learning from Las Vegas*) applies to data graphics: modern architects who abandoned ornament unconsciously designed buildings that *were* ornament — distorting the whole building into a duck. The passage ends with Pugin's warning, which Tufte adopts as a design rule: **"It is all right to decorate construction but never construct decoration"** (p. 117).
- Fake perspective (false 3D) added to the data structure clutters many graphics. This variety of chartjunk — "Boutique Data Graphics" — abounds in corporate annual reports, phony statistical studies in advertisements, the mass media, and muddled social science research (p. 118).
- Even superbly produced ducks exist (the California Water Atlas), so production quality does not redeem the form (p. 119).
- **The We-Used-A-Computer-To-Build-A-Duck Syndrome** (p. 120): designers seek credit merely for possessing a new technology rather than using it to make better designs. The technology itself is not the problem — "Computers and their affiliated apparatus can do powerful things graphically, in part by turning out the hundreds of plots necessary for good data analysis" (p. 120). Such graphics evoke "Isn't it remarkable that the computer can be programmed to draw like that?" instead of "My, what interesting data." Symptoms: thin substance; clotted, crinkly all-uppercase sans serif lettering; pointlessly ordered cross-hatching; labels written in computer abbreviations; optical vibration — all by-products of the technology of graphic fabrication.
- Related rules from the same critique (p. 120):
  - Do not put more scale markers and labels on an axis than there are data points; print the observed values of the data instead.
  - If the information consists of a few numbers and a good many words, pass up the graphics capability and tell the story with a table.

## Conclusion (p. 121)

- "Chartjunk does not achieve the goals of its propagators. The overwhelming fact of data graphics is that they stand or fall on their content, gracefully displayed."
- Graphics do not become attractive and interesting through the addition of ornamental hatching and false perspective. "Chartjunk can turn bores into disasters, but it can never rescue a thin data set."
- The best designs (Minard on Napoleon in Russia, Marey's graphical train schedule, the cancer maps, the *Times* weather history of New York City, the chronicle of the Japanese beetle, the new view of the galaxies) are *intriguing and curiosity-provoking*, drawing the viewer into the wonder of the data — by narrative power, immense detail, or elegant presentation of simple but interesting data. No information, no sense of discovery, no wonder, no substance is generated by chartjunk.
- Final injunction: **"Forgo chartjunk, including moiré vibration, the grid, and the duck."**

The chapter closes (p. 122) with an epigraph from painter Ad Reinhardt (1955) celebrating "Clarity, completeness, quintessence, quiet. No noise, no schmutz... No humbugging, no button-holing, no exploitation, no mixing things up" — an aesthetic Tufte holds up for data graphics.
