# Examples — Sources of Graphical Integrity and Sophistication

### Quotes documenting "statistics are boring" (pp. 79-80)

Tufte quotes three sources to show the doctrine is widespread among graphic designers, not a straw man:

- *Time* magazine's first full-time chart specialist, an art-school graduate, described his work: "The challenge is to present statistics as a visual idea rather than a tedious parade of numbers" (*Time*, February 11, 1980).
- Jan V. White's *Graphic Idea Notebook* (1980) opens its statistical charts chapter with "Why are statistics so boring?" and promises "Dry statistics turned into symbolic graphics" and "Plain statistics embellished or humanized with pictures."
- The publisher of Walter Herdeg's *Graphis/Diagrams* (Zurich, 1976) — a book Tufte calls fine — still markets it as proving "that statistical and diagrammatic graphics do not necessarily have to be dull."

Lesson: the boredom doctrine leads artists to decorate and exaggerate, and it serves bureaucratic ends by justifying an Art Department's control over data graphics. Tufte's counter: "If the statistics are boring, then you've got the wrong numbers."

### Quotes documenting "graphics are for the unsophisticated" (pp. 80-81)

- *Consumer Reports* (July 1980), on designing its new children's magazine: the professional staff first produced a sugar article longer on graphics than information because "We had feared children might be overwhelmed by too many facts."
- An art director responsible for about 3,000 data graphics a year (yielding 2.5 billion printed images) said graphics are intended more to lure the reader's attention away from the advertising than to explain the news in any detail: "Unlike the advertisements, at least we don't put naked women in our graphics" (Louis Silverstein of the *New York Times*, 1978 conference presentation).
- A news director at a national television network: graphics must be instantly understandable — "If you have to explain it, don't use it" (interview with Tufte, July 1980).

Lesson: contempt for the audience produces content-free graphics. Tufte invokes E. B. White: no one communicates decently while distrusting the reader's intelligence.

### The company cafeteria "unnecessary chart" (p. 81)

A chart titled "The Company Cafeteria was used by 9 Out of 10 Employees during the Fiscal Year 1949" shows a decorated drawing of a cafeteria line over a 0-to-100% scale, all to convey a single number already stated in its own title. Reproduced from Mary Eleanor Spear's *Charting Statistics* (1952), where Spear herself appropriately labels it an "unnecessary chart." Tufte presents it as the natural product of designing for supposedly unsophisticated readers: a graphic carrying no information beyond one sentence.

### Bivariate data forced into univariate designs (p. 81)

Two convoluted newspaper graphics show what happens when artists avoid the scatterplot:

- A *New York Times* chart (June 16, 1980) on "Comparative Tax Loads and Economic Growth Rates in the Fifteen Largest OECD Countries" (data from Allen D. Manvel, *Taxation with Representation Newsletter*, June 1980). It strings two variables — tax burden and growth rate — through an awkward bar arrangement instead of plotting one against the other.
- A *New York Times*/CBS News Poll graphic, "Views on the Economy Influence Carter Support," showing for nine states both the share of Democratic voters calling their financial situation worse and Carter's primary vote share, as paired pseudo-3D bars rather than a scatterplot.

Lesson: lack of quantitative skill leads, like censorship, to "elliptical and eccentric communication." The natural design for two related variables is the relational graphic; forcing bivariate data into univariate bar designs obscures the relationship the data exist to show.

### Examples of relational designs that clear the bar (p. 82)

Tufte shows what counts as "sophisticated" by his deliberately undemanding measure:

- A *New York Times* science story (February 29, 1976), "Pace of City Life Found 2.8 Feet per Second Faster," with a scatterplot of pedestrian walking speed against city population (log scale) for cities from Psychro and Corte to Brooklyn, Athens, Prague, and Munich. A Princeton psychologist and his anthropologist wife measured walking speeds and found people walk faster in bigger cities — about 2.8 feet per second faster in big cities than in small towns (e.g., Brooklyn 5.0 feet per second; Psychro, Greece, population 365, 2.7; Corte, France, population 5,500, 3.3). The graphic shows how one variable affects another — exactly what relational design is for.
- Two scatterplots from a Japanese high school textbook, Isao Sato and Miyohei Shinohara's *New Politics and Economics* (Tokyo, 1974): one relating sector price indices to real productivity per worker across industries, another relating capital investment ratios to economic growth rates across countries (Japan, Holland, West Germany, Canada, France, Britain, and others).

Lesson: relational graphics date from 1765, appear in high school textbooks, and twelve-year-olds understand them. They are not too hard for newspaper readers.

### The world press study and Table 1 (pp. 82-83)

Tufte's own study: randomly selected issues, 1974-1980, of 15 major world news publications; about 4,000 graphics examined; scaled by publication frequency and circulation, the sample stands for 250-300 billion printed graphical images. Each publication is scored by the percentage of its statistical graphics that are relational (more than one variable, but not a time-series or a map).

Results (Table 1, p. 83): the Japanese Communist party paper *Akahata* leads at 9.3% (it appears to have employed a sophisticated and talented graphical designer in 1979), followed by *Asahi Shimbun* 7.6%, *Der Spiegel* 5.7%, *The Economist* 2.0%, *Nihon Keizai Shimbun* 1.7%, *Le Monde* 0.7%, *Business Week* 0.6%, *New York Times* 0.5%. Seven papers scored 0.0%: *Pravda*, *Frankfurter Allgemeine*, *The Times* (London), *Washington Post*, *Time*, *Die Zeit*, and the *Wall Street Journal* (which published 449 sampled graphics without a single relational design). For comparison, a random sample of 220 graphics from *Science* (1978-1980) was 42% relational.

Lesson: news graphics nearly everywhere, and especially in the U.S., avoid the explanatory designs that competent statistical analysis requires; yet Japanese papers and a few European weeklies prove news work can do better.

### Japan's statistical culture and the seven-year-olds' graph (p. 84)

Quoting Andrew H. Malcolm's *New York Times* story "Data-Loving Japanese Rejoice on Statistics Day" (October 28, 1977): no nation matches Japan's collective passion for statistics — statistics has its own holiday, conventions, awards, and a nationwide graph-drawing contest that drew 29,836 entries. First prize went to five seven-year-olds whose graph, "Mom, play with us more often," surveyed 32 classmates on how often mothers played with them and why not. Other children correlated the day's temperature with cicada singing — a relational design, as Tufte pointedly notes.

Lesson: explains Japan's lead in Table 1 and proves that relational graphics are within reach of small children, demolishing the claim that they are too sophisticated for newspaper readers.

### Tables 2 and 3 — textbooks and educational tests (pp. 84-86)

Tufte applies the same relational-share measure to textbooks (Table 2, p. 85) and standardized tests (Table 3, p. 86):

- College texts: medicine and public health articles in Tanur et al., *Statistics: A Guide to the Unknown* 82%; Hilgard's *Introduction to Psychology* 68%; Pauling's *General Chemistry* 66%; Wilson's *Life on Earth* 47%; weather, astronomy, and engineering articles in Tanur 44%; communication, work, education, and economics articles in Tanur 43%; Flanigan and Zingale's *Political Behavior of the American Electorate* 42%; Samuelson's *Economics* 16%; Dahl's *Democracy in America* 8%; Wilson's *American Government* 0%.
- High school texts: Masterton and Slowinski's *Chemical Principles* 77%; Harvard Project Physics 48%; the Japanese Sato and Shinohara text 27%; *Biological Science: An Ecological Approach* 18%; Sampson's *The American Economy* 5%; four sociology, ethics, history, and government texts at 0%.
- Tests: Japanese national university entrance exams (1979-1980) 100% relational; U.S. LSAT review materials (1975) 48%; U.S. standardized tests in science 67%, mathematics 41%, social studies 24%, general ability 21% (graphics from James R. Beniger's 1975 compilation of test items measuring ability with graphics).

Lesson: even high school students see denser relational material than readers of the major U.S. press, so "most news publications outside of Japan operate at a pre-adult level of intelligence in graphical design."

### The double standard in a single New York Times issue (p. 86)

From one issue of the *New York Times*, Tufte quotes four passages of demanding prose — on recycling petrodollars and floating exchange rates, a music review dissecting "square-toed instrumental phrasing" and "intrusively elaborate continuo improvisations," a court ruling on sovereign immunity, and a pharmacologist calling neuroleptics "dysphorogenic." The same paper that publishes this expert-level prose publishes statistically stupid graphics.

Lesson: publications hold a double standard — one level of integrity and sophistication for words, a much lower one for graphics. The audience that reads the prose can handle real graphics (about 80 percent of Sunday *Times* readers attended college).

### Other examples mentioned

- Howard Wainer's observation (p. 79, n. 1) that Playfair's original eighteenth-century work did not contain today's common graphical errors, and that formal training in graphic presentation has markedly declined over recent decades — supporting Tufte's claim that the problem is skills and training, not the medium.
