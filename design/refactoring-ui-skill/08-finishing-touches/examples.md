# Finishing Touches — Examples

Every demonstration in the chapter, paraphrased. "Weak" = the book's ✗ version, "Improved" = the ✓ version.

## Supercharge the defaults

**Feature list bullets → checkmark icons.**
Context: a marketing feature list titled "Everything you need in one place" with three items (create your online presence, manage products and inventory, complete order fulfillment).
Weak: plain small gray dot bullets.
Improved: each bullet replaced with a solid blue circular checkmark icon.
Why it works: the icons add color and weight, making the list feel designed instead of default; checkmarks also reinforce "you get all of this."

**Feature list bullets → content-specific icons.**
Context: a security feature list titled "Protecting your customers and income" (fraud monitoring, no dispute fees).
Weak: plain dot bullets.
Improved: gray circular padlock icons as bullets.
Why it works: the icon choice echoes the content (security), so the decoration also communicates meaning. Checkmarks/arrows are great generic choices for many situations; an icon tied to the content is another option.

**Testimonial quote promotion.**
Context: a customer testimonial card with quote text and attribution ("Daniel Bowen, Founder of Bowen Group").
Weak: the quote is ordinary italic text with quotation marks typed inline.
Improved: the quotation marks are pulled out as oversized, accent-colored opening and closing quote glyphs flanking the text; the inline quote marks are removed.
Why it works: a free decorative element made from punctuation that was already there; it signals "testimonial" instantly and adds color.
Figure detail: the placement is hanging punctuation, not inline characters — the oversized opening glyph is outdented past the text block's left edge, raised beside the first line, and the closing glyph trails after the end of the last line. The two accent-colored glyphs are the only colored elements; the quote itself stays dark italic serif.

**Link styling, two levels.**
Context: body copy "Visit our locations page to find the nearest distributor..." under a "Schedule a consultation" heading.
Weak: the link is default-looking light blue text, blending into the paragraph.
Improved (option 1): the link gets a stronger purple color and bold weight.
Improved (option 2): the link is bold dark text with a thick colorful underline that sits partially behind/overlapping the text.
Why it works: both make the link a deliberate visual element; the custom underline adds flair without imagery.

**Custom form controls.**
Context: a business questionnaire with a checkbox ("Do you own a business?") and radio group (Service / Merchandising / Manufacturing business).
Weak: browser-default blue checkbox and radio buttons.
Improved: custom controls — a purple rounded checkbox and larger custom radio buttons; only the selected states carry the purple brand color, while unselected controls stay neutral gray rings.
Why it works: just swapping the selected-state color to a brand color makes the form feel polished and owned rather than browser-default.

## Add color with accent borders

**Accent border on a card.**
Context: a "Freelancer" pricing card ($12/month, feature list, Get Started button).
Weak: plain white card, entirely grayscale.
Improved: identical card plus a bright blue bar across its top edge.
Why it works: one colored rectangle gives the card brand color and a designed feel with zero added content.

**Accent border under active nav item.**
Context: a top navigation bar (Dashboard, Orders, Products, Discounts) with Products active.
Weak: active item indicated only by blue text color.
Improved: active item also gets a short blue underline bar beneath it.
Why it works: the underline makes the active state unmistakable and adds a crisp accent to an otherwise plain bar.

**Accent border on an alert.**
Context: an informational "Pro user feature" alert with light blue background and a link.
Weak: flat light-blue box.
Improved: same box with a solid blue vertical bar along its left edge.
Why it works: the left border sharpens the alert's identity as a callout and adds saturated color against the pale background.

**Accent under a headline.**
Context: heading "Here to help every step of the way" above a support paragraph.
Weak: heading and gray paragraph, no color.
Improved: a short blue horizontal bar (a few characters wide) sits between the headline and the paragraph.
Why it works: a tiny decoration that gives the section structure and a spot of color — far cheaper than an illustration.

**Accent border across the whole layout.**
Context: a conference landing page (FutureWeb) with logo, headline, date, and description.
Weak: plain white page.
Improved: a dark teal bar runs across the very top of the entire page.
Why it works: the full-width strip frames the page and makes it feel intentionally branded; no graphic-design skill needed.

## Decorate your backgrounds

**Colored panel background.**
Context: a "Premium" pricing card ($9/mo + 2.5% per charge, Start Free Trial footer).
Weak: white card on light gray.
Improved: the card body becomes a dark blue-purple with white/light text; the footer button area stays white.
Why it works: the dark background emphasizes the panel and makes the price data pop, transforming a plain card into a focal point.
Figure detail: the swap is not just the fill — the main figures ("9", "2.5", "$5,000") turn white, while the secondary symbols ($ of $9, +, %, /mo) and the small captions switch from gray to a pale teal, so the price data still reads clearly against the dark panel.

**Alternating section backgrounds.**
Context: a full landing-page wireframe with several stacked sections.
Weak: every section is white — the page reads as one undifferentiated column.
Improved: sections alternate backgrounds (vibrant blue-purple hero, white section, dark section, light gray section).
Why it works: background changes mark where sections begin and end, breaking monotony without changing any layout.

**Gradient background.**
Context: a dark feature section ("Details we're sweating") with icon + text feature blurbs.
Improved (shown only as good): the background is a subtle blue gradient rather than a flat color.
Rule given: keep the gradient's two hues no more than about 30° apart on the color wheel.
Why it works: a tight-hue gradient adds energy while still reading as one cohesive color.

**Full repeating pattern.**
Context: an "About me" personal-site header card (cyan block beside a duotone photo).
Improved (shown only as good): the cyan background carries a subtle tone-on-tone topographic-line pattern (from Hero Patterns).
Why it works: texture adds interest; because the pattern barely contrasts with the background, the white heading stays fully readable.

**Edge-only pattern.**
Context: a dark site footer with link columns and a newsletter signup.
Improved (shown only as good): a fading dot grid pattern runs along just the bottom edge of the footer.
Why it works: a pattern confined to one edge decorates without occupying the content area; low contrast keeps it quiet.

**Geometric shape decoration.**
Context: a blog article header ("Choosing the Perfect Tech Stack") with title and intro text.
Improved (shown only as good): a triangular grid of small purple dots sits in the upper-right corner beside the text.
Why it works: one positioned shape relieves the plainness of a text-only header without any imagery.

**Pattern fragment decoration.**
Context: a "Simple Pricing" section with a savings-comparison panel.
Improved (shown only as good): a small block of wavy gray lines occupies the top-left corner behind the heading area.
Why it works: a chunk of repeatable pattern in one spot reads as deliberate decoration; cheaper and quieter than a full-bleed texture.

**Simplified world map.**
Context: a "Join our newsletter" signup card with an email field and button.
Improved (shown only as good): a faint dotted world map spreads across the card's background behind the heading and form.
Why it works: a more elaborate decoration still works because contrast is kept very low — the map never competes with the form. Same low-contrast rule as patterns.

## Don't overlook empty states

**Contacts list empty state.**
Context: a mobile contacts/messaging screen. The designed mock is full of realistic sample data — avatars, names, message previews, unread badges.
Weak: a real new user opens the feature and sees a bare screen with only the centered text "No contacts found."
Improved: the empty screen shows a friendly purple person illustration, the line "Add a new contact and start messaging.", and a prominent purple "+ Add contact" button.
Why it works: the empty state is the user's actual first experience; an illustration plus an emphasized call-to-action turns a dead end into onboarding.

**Hiding supporting UI when empty.**
Context: an admin "Discounts" page with sidebar nav, tab bar (All / Active / Scheduled / Expired), filter control, search field, and a Create discount button.
Weak: all tabs, filters, and search render even though there are zero discounts — controls that can do nothing — with a modest empty state (a small outline discount-tag graphic, the one-line benefit message, and a Create discount button) squeezed beneath them.
Improved: the tabs, filter, and search are removed entirely — and so are the page title bar and its duplicate Create discount header button, leaving exactly one action on screen. The content area becomes a full-bleed empty state: heading "Create a discount code", a one-line benefit ("Increase sales by offering discounts to your customers."), a Create discount button, and a large faint discount-tag illustration.
Why it works: supporting UI is pointless until content exists; hiding it removes clutter and focuses the user entirely on the one meaningful action. (Editorial note: the figure renders the oversized illustration faintly, consistent with the low-contrast advice from the backgrounds section, though the book doesn't draw that connection here.)

## Use fewer borders

All four figures use the same UI: a "Contacts" invite modal containing a title, a search input, a list of teams/members (one row highlighted), and a footer with Cancel and Invite buttons.

**The border-heavy baseline.**
Weak: borders everywhere — under the title, around the search input, between every list row, and above the footer. The modal feels busy and gridded.
Lesson: borders separate things, but they aren't the only way, and stacking many of them clutters the design.

**Replacement 1 — box shadow.**
Improved: the modal is defined against the page by `box-shadow: 0 5px 15px 0 hsla(0, 0%, 0%, 0.15)` instead of an outline border.
Why it works: a shadow outlines an element like a border but is softer and less distracting. Caveat: it works best when the element's color differs from the background behind it.

**Replacement 2 — different background colors.**
Improved: the search input loses its border and instead gets a slightly darker fill, `background-color: hsl(200, 10%, 94%)`, against the white modal; the footer sits on a band of the same gray — the figure's callout lines run from both the search input and the footer to the single `hsl(200, 10%, 94%)` annotation. One subtle tint does double duty for two separate distinctions in the same modal; no second gray is invented for the footer.
Why it works: a small background-color difference is usually enough distinction on its own. Corollary: if you have both differing backgrounds and a border, try removing the border — you might not need it.

**Replacement 3 — extra spacing.**
Improved: the divider lines between list rows are gone; rows sit farther apart instead (the `margin-bottom: 6px` annotation points at the gap between two rows). The footer keeps its gray band from the previous technique.
Why it works: separation literally is space; increasing it distinguishes groups with no new UI at all.

**The techniques stack — the figures are one cumulative sequence, not three alternatives.**
Each figure keeps the previous fixes and retires a different kind of border. The shadow figure replaces only the modal's outer border — the search input's outline and the row dividers are still visible. The background-color figure keeps that shadow and retires the search outline and footer divider with tinted fills; the row dividers remain. The spacing figure keeps both prior changes and retires the row dividers with the 6px gaps. By the final figure none of the baseline's borders survive — outer border, title divider, search outline, row dividers, footer divider are all gone — yet every grouping still reads clearly. Fully de-bordering the busy baseline comes from combining all three moves, not picking one.

## Think outside the box

**Dropdown menu reimagined.**
Context: a marketing-site nav (Features / Pricing / Company) with Features open.
Weak: the conventional dropdown — a narrow white box with a drop shadow and a plain stacked list of nine text links separated by a divider.
Improved: a wide rounded panel split into two zones: a two-column grid of six features, each with a colorful icon, bold title, and a one-line description (one item carries a yellow "NEW" badge); below, a gray-tinted section lists three resource links (Documentation, Full API Reference, Open Source) with icons and inline descriptions.
Why it works: a dropdown is just a floating box — sections, columns, icons, and supporting text make it informative and visually rich instead of a bare link list.
Figure detail: the improved panel holds exactly the same nine links as the weak menu — the weak version even already splits them six-and-three with a divider. No destination is added or removed; the entire upgrade is presentation (grid, icons, descriptions, badge, tinted zone). You can apply this transformation without inventing new content — it supercharges what's already there.

**Table with merged, hierarchical columns.**
Context: an insurance-policy admin table.
Weak: six flat columns — Name, Role, Policy, Policy Type, Location, Status — every cell a single plain string.
Improved: related columns merge — Name with the role stacked beneath it, Policy amount with the policy type beneath it, both sublines in the same smaller, softer treatment — cutting the table to four columns with clear hierarchy inside each cell.
Why it works: if a column doesn't need independent sorting, combining it with its sibling reduces grid noise and lets size and lightness show what matters (name, amount) versus metadata (role, type).
Figure detail: the sublines aren't a neutral gray — in the figure they render in a muted blue-gray (slate) tint, so the de-emphasis pairs smaller size with a softer tinted color against the dark primary text.

**Table with images and color.**
Context: the same policy table, one step further.
Improved: circular avatar photos are added before each name, and the Status column's plain text becomes colored badge pills — green "Approved", yellow "Awaiting Approval".
Why it works: images humanize rows and badges let status be scanned by color, enriching the data rather than just decorating it.

**Radio buttons → selectable cards.**
Context: a "Change Plan" screen choosing between hosting tiers.
Weak: a vertical stack of four default radio buttons with cramped labels like "Growth - 5 GB ($10/mo)".
Improved: four side-by-side selectable cards, each showing the plan name as a small caps label, the storage amount large and bold ("5 GB"), and the price below; the selected card gets a green border and a checkmark.
Why it works: when the choice is central to the UI, cards give each option room for hierarchy (key number large) and make the selected state obvious — conventions like "radio = little circle" are defaults, not rules.
