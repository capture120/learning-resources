# Chapter 15: Wardley Maps — Examples

## The knowledge-base product map (running example)

The chapter's primer builds a Wardley map for a knowledge-base management product like Confluence or Notion. Two users sit at the top: an author and a reader. Their needs include authoring content, finding content, and giving feedback on which content is helpful; underlying capabilities include document editing, document reading, and search indexing. On the axes, document reading sits far right (commoditized — it's unremarkable that an app lets users read content) and high up (extremely visible to users), document editing sits on the custom/product border (you could buy a vendor editor or build your own), and search indexing sits low on visibility because users depend on it without knowing it exists.
Illustrates: the three foundational concepts — users/needs/capabilities, the genesis→custom→product→commodity x-axis, and the user-visibility y-axis.

## AI-enhanced editing as a future state

A second iteration of the knowledge-base map adds an arrow predicting that the current document-creation experience will be superseded by AI-enhanced editing — and, critically, that the AI version will be *more* commoditized than today's authoring experience, perhaps because it will be driven by commoditized foundation models from providers like Anthropic and OpenAI. That single prediction reshapes the whole strategy: the only place left on the map for meaningful differentiation is search indexing. The company must either accept that it is increasingly a search company or expand the user needs it serves to find a new avenue for differentiation.
Illustrates: how mapping evolution surfaces hard strategic implications that aren't visible in the current state.

## The editing pipeline

Instead of one arrow from current editing to AI editing, a pipeline box expresses the expected sequence of improvements: a typical editing experience, then AI-assisted creation, then AI-led creation where the author only gives high-level direction to a machine-learning-powered agent.
Illustrates: the pipeline notation for expressing staged evolution of a single capability rather than a one-jump change.

## The team-ownership overlay

A fourth version of the map draws overlay boxes grouping capabilities by the teams responsible for them. Overlays usually indicate team ownership but can emphasize any interesting element of a map's topology.
Illustrates: the overlay notation for grouping map components by a common denominator.

## Startups and the death of the physical datacenter

In the early 2010s, startups like Facebook, Uber, and Digg all ran their own hardware in physical datacenters. Within five years, cloud infrastructure expanded so rapidly that a physical-datacenter presence went from the default startup approach to a relatively unconventional one. Any strategy written in 2010 that imagined hosting as static was destined to be invalidated.
Illustrates: even seemingly stable foundations evolve, so any strategy meant to last more than a year or two needs situational awareness of how its ground will shift.

## Dynamic eras that demanded situational awareness

The chapter cites several decades of highly dynamic contexts: the rise of web applications, the proliferation of mobile devices, and the expansion of machine learning techniques. In each, the industry around you, the competitors you're selling against, and/or the capabilities powering your product were shifting rapidly.
Illustrates: the environments where Wardley mapping is most essential, because broad change is the norm rather than the exception.

## Where maps don't help: the onboarding funnel

If you're iterating to optimize an onboarding funnel, something like systems modeling or strategy testing is likely to serve you better than Wardley mapping.
Illustrates: Wardley maps excel at broad ecosystem change but are less helpful in the details.

## Scenario forking: AI dominant vs. AI regulated away

When predicting evolution (step 6 of the process), the chapter suggests making multiple future maps for the knowledge-base product: one where AI-powered tools become the dominant authoring mechanism, and another where AI has been regulated out of most tools, then imagining how each future shapes the approach differently.
Illustrates: building multiple scenario maps instead of betting on a single predicted future.

## Wardley's doctrine and gameplay (mentioned, deliberately omitted)

Wardley's broader strategy system includes doctrine — universally applicable practices like knowing your users, biasing toward data, and designing for constant evolution — and gameplay, context-dependent moves like talent raiding (hiring from knowledgeable competitors), bundling products, and exploiting network effects. Larson leaves these out because he finds them lightly specialized for the needs of business strategy, and consequently a bit messy to apply to engineering strategy problems.
Illustrates: taking the mapping technique from Wardley's system while combining it with Rumelt's structure, rather than adopting either camp wholesale.
