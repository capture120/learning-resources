# Chapter 19: Customer Data Access Strategy — Examples

This chapter is itself a worked example: Document 19-1, "How Should We Control Access to User Data?" (2022), written by a CISO. It is an amalgamation of experiences at several companies rather than a single firm's document, because security topics are too sensitive to publish directly. The sections below capture the scenario, the actual policies and operational mechanisms chosen, the diagnosis specifics, and the exploration findings.

## The scenario: IPO readiness exposes a controls gap

A startup's leaders decide they must be ready to go public in 18 months, triggering IPO-readiness work. That work surfaces a gap in internal controls for managing access to user data. The company wants a meaningfully stronger security posture, but it carries baggage: years of failed security initiatives, most of which degraded workflows for teams like customer support and were reverted or subverted over time, leaving little long-term effect. The CISO writes this strategy to meet the IPO obligation while overcoming that history and, most importantly, doing right by users.

Illustrates: how an external forcing function (IPO) creates the mandate for a strategy, and why the strategy must confront prior failures rather than repeat them.

## The full policy set (Document 19-1)

The strategy commits to these policies and operating mechanisms:

1. **Controls must be significantly stronger before the IPO.** Senior leadership, legal, compliance, and security jointly reject the status quo for a public company. The target is resource-level access control: which rows, not which tables, a person may access. The Security team is accountable for the exact mechanisms.
2. **Continue the hybrid approach to resource-level access controls**, since it is the existing approach and the fastest available option.
3. **Expose the access log directly to users.** Build toward a user-accessible log of every company access of user data, with every rationale comprehensible and reasonable from the user's perspective, so users can judge the company's data use and vote with their feet.
4. **Never trade security against usability.** Pursue multi-dimensional tradeoffs that improve both. The security-versus-utility framing itself signals "the wrong discussion."
5. **Prioritize mechanisms that automatically authorize and document access.** Example: a support agent automatically gets access to a user's data while that user's open ticket is assigned to them, revoked on reassignment or resolution.
6. **Measure the percentage of accesses justified by a user-comprehensible, automated rationale.** Requirement expansions alone don't count (not automated, invites workarounds); usability improvements alone don't count (no increase in supported requests).
7. **Expire unused roles after 90 days** to enforce least privilege, with engineers on active on-call rotations exempted.
8. **Weekly strategy-testing syncs until progress is clear; monthly access reviews forever.** The CISO sponsors and the Principal Security Engineer guides the weekly meeting, which runs until the path to 100% automated-rationale coverage is clear. The monthly meeting samples real accesses for rationale quality and hunts for automated ways to flag high-risk accesses.
9. **Exceptions require written CISO approval** via the #ciso channel, overriding the company's usual advisory architecture process (from Andrew Harmel-Law's *Facilitating Software Architecture*).

Illustrates: a complete policy-and-operations section where every policy pairs a commitment with the mechanism that enforces it.

## The support-ticket access mechanism

The flagship example of automated authorization-plus-rationale: when a user opens a support ticket and it is assigned to an agent, that agent automatically gains access to the user's data. When the ticket is reassigned or resolved, access is automatically removed. The rationale ("agent assigned to your open ticket") is generated by the workflow itself and is immediately understandable to the user whose data was accessed.

Illustrates: tying access grants to legitimate work state so security and workflow efficiency improve together.

## The manual-rationale escalation channel

A private channel gives the security and compliance team visibility into every manual rationale for user data access. Anyone who repeatedly uses manual justifications has their manager notified. Manual access stays possible but becomes socially and managerially expensive, pushing teams toward automated paths.

Illustrates: an operational mechanism that discourages the old behavior without hard-blocking essential work.

## Diagnosis: strong baseline, hollow process

The company has a strong baseline of RBAC and durable, pervasive audit logging. But: roles accumulate unused as people change teams (some five-plus-year employees hold numerous unused roles); audit logs are typically used reactively after incidents, with limited proactive use; and the hybrid resource-access setup (a third-party platform for incoming user requests plus approval mechanisms inside the company's own product) requires busy people to manually copy rationale information across the two systems many times a day, with rationales later reviewed manually in batches. Two problems result: requesting teams see the process as a burdensome obligation with little benefit to them or the user, and manual batch review produces no verifiable evidence of review quality.

Illustrates: a diagnosis that locates the failure in process design (manual toil, misaligned incentives) rather than in people or tooling gaps.

## No misuse found, yet the controls still fail

Audits uniformly found a clear and reasonable basis for every access of user data, for example an open ticket the user themselves had raised. Yet rationale quality was consistently low because it depended on manual copying, making verification "somewhat arbitrary." The company satisfied literal compliance (rationales exist, audits happen) while it was unclear whether the majority of those audits improved user data security.

Illustrates: the gap between compliance theater and real security; a process can be both fully clean and nearly worthless as a control.

## The history of self-defeating security initiatives

Past investments produced temporary spikes in security posture. A year later, many showed increased scrutiny followed by gradual repeal or avoidance. The mechanism: most of the initiatives added friction to other teams' essential work (like handling customer requests), so those teams subtly subverted the controls while pursuing their immediate goals. The strategy concludes with calibrated confidence: high conviction the historical approach creates internal optical wins, limited conviction it creates lasting improvement unless something unlikely changes, such as colleagues being markedly less busy in a year.

Illustrates: the friction-then-subversion failure pattern, and why the strategy outlaws the Security team's prior approach.

## Exploration: how peer companies control resource-level access

Best practices were widely available through the company's networks but otherwise hard to find, possibly because the topic is uncomfortable to discuss publicly given potential liability and compliance exposure. RBAC and audit logs proved standardized everywhere; resource-level access control diverged into three patterns:

1. **Third-party enrichment.** Access is managed inside a vendor platform such as Zendesk, whose objects are enriched with product data and which implements actions (archiving, configuration) within its own permission structure. Costs: tight vendor coupling, the platform's limitations, and maintaining engineers fluent in both stacks. Companies choosing this typically run a sizable, long-standing platform team.
2. **First-party implementation.** Everything, including issue creation and management, lives in the core product. Most common at earlier-stage companies or where support leadership "grew up" internally without seeing peer approaches. Benefit: one tightly integrated, infinitely extensible platform. Cost: building and maintaining what a vendor could invest in more heavily.
3. **Hybrid.** A third-party platform handles most actions and also gates resource-level access in the first-party system, e.g., data access only while an open ticket from that user is assigned to you. Benefits: supports workflows beyond the platform's limits and avoids deep coupling; lets the work fold into existing teams instead of a dedicated one.

The RBAC example given: a customer support agent role may deactivate accounts, while a sales engineer role may configure new ones.

Illustrates: mapping the option space before choosing, and choosing the hybrid pattern as the pragmatic continuation of existing investment.

## Larson's commentary: two reasons this document matters

Larson highlights the document for two reasons. First, it shows executive-led strategy testing (the CISO-sponsored weekly sync) used to work through misalignment across teams, which he calls the most effective mechanism he has found for persistent organizational friction. Second, it shows policy explicitly constraining a team into alignment: the Security team's prior behaviors were "effectively outlawed," opening a path to a more effective way of working.

Illustrates: strategy as a tool aimed inward at the owning team, not only outward at the rest of the organization.
