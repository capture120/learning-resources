# Chapter 7: Diagnosis — Examples

## The bar-raiser program with a hidden diagnosis

A company Larson worked for rolled out a "bar-raiser" hiring program styled after Amazon's, requiring an interviewer from outside the team to approve every hire. Larson argued against it: he couldn't see what problem it solved, and management seemed uninterested in measuring whether it improved hiring outcomes. He learned much later that most of the senior leadership distrusted one specific peer and felt the CTO wasn't holding that leader accountable, so they created the program solely as a mechanism to control that manager's hiring decisions. Even senior leaders aren't generally allowed to write down "The Director of Product Engineering is a bad hiring manager," so the real diagnosis stayed hidden — and the strategy made little sense to anyone without it. (Leaders also didn't care much about enforcement, so hiring managers frequently ignored bar-raiser rejections.)
Illustrates: a strategy that makes sense with the full diagnosis makes little sense without it; omitting critical diagnosis elements makes strategies particularly hard to evaluate, copy, or recreate.

## Stripe's Sorbet diagnosis backed by data

The diagnosis behind Stripe's creation of Sorbet (covered in Document 22-3) included concrete data such as staffing numbers for the relevant teams and the extent of test coverage in the Ruby codebase, helping readers follow the author's reasoning.
Illustrates: incorporating data into a diagnosis so readers share the same facts and assumptions, making the strategy far simpler to evaluate.

## Private equity strategy: whispering the layoff risk

A strategy for navigating new private equity ownership (Chapter 18) included this diagnosis line: based on general practice, the new owners would likely expect R&D headcount cost reductions, but without concrete details no structured decisions could be made yet, and the strategy would depend significantly on the scale of any proposed reductions. The authors were probably upset that colleagues might be eliminated, and may also have been upset that they were stuck preparing for a wide range of outcomes, but the diagnosis stuck to precise, factual statements with no blame.
Illustrates: reframing an emotionally charged, controversial circumstance into palatable, factual diagnosis language.

## Uber service migration: flat headcount stated without commentary

Uber's service migration strategy (Document 16-1) diagnosed that a four-engineer team owned service provisioning, that infrastructure was growing at a similar rate as product engineering, yet none of the added headcount was being allocated directly to that team — and that this was not expected to change. The team disagreed with the staffing decision, but acknowledged the reality factually, without editorializing.
Illustrates: acknowledging an unwelcome organizational reality in nonjudgmental terms rather than litigating it inside the strategy document.

## "Executives change their minds too often"

Early-career leaders often tell Larson that a problem they've identified makes strategy work impossible — for example, that they can't do strategy work at their company because the executive team changes its mind too often. The insight is almost certainly true, but it becomes far more powerful reframed as diagnosis: "If we don't find a way to show concrete progress quickly and use that to excite the executive team, our strategy is likely to fail."
Illustrates: transforming a blocker into a condition the strategy must address.

## Calm: informal prioritization as a fixed constraint

In the strategy for resourcing Engineering-driven projects at Calm (Document 21-2), the authors knew the company's informal approach to prioritization wasn't going to change — even winning over product management peers wouldn't change the executive team's informal planning. Rather than preventing the strategy, this dynamic clarified what kind of approach could actually succeed.
Illustrates: an immovable organizational constraint, written into the diagnosis, sharpens rather than blocks the strategy.

## Larson's own silence on diagnosis

Larson notes that his earlier book, The Engineering Executive's Primer, has a strategy chapter that is notably silent on how to perform diagnosis — evidence that even practitioners treat diagnosis as a dark art: unspecified, undiscussed, and uncontrollable.
Illustrates: why this chapter offers an explicit structured process where most treatments leave diagnosis unexplained.
