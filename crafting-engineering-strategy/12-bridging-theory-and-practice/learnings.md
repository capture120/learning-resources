# Chapter 12: Bridging Theory and Practice

This chapter concedes that the book's clean, step-by-step theory of strategy gets messy in real organizations, then shows how to keep doing good strategy anyway: under unrealistic deadlines, without executive authority, in chaotic environments, with indecisive stakeholders, and underneath other people's bad strategy. The single most important takeaway: reality will always pull you away from perfect strategy, but none of those pulls is an excuse to stop — "effective diagnosis trumps authority," and the only guaranteed way to fail is to wait until you're certain no new information will appear.

## Core ideas

### Theory is "right" to look fake — twice over

Skeptics who call engineering strategy a theoretical or fake construct are right in two ways. First, the book teaches clean, refined, definitive strategy documents, but most real strategy artifacts initially look messy. Second, applying the techniques requires real creativity; it sounds easy and is hard in practice. The chapter exists to close that gap rather than deny it. Strategy documents come across as overly pristine not because development was tidy, but because they're trying to communicate clearly about a complex topic.

### Reader-oriented documents are not superficial

Thinking is a linear-ish journey from problem to solution, so documents that feel intuitive to write narrate that journey. But good strategies are read for direction (a team wants to know how to solve a specific issue) far more often than to build agreement (stakeholder alignment during development). Presenting decisions before explaining how they were reached looks superficial to people raised on writer-oriented documents — but it "is simply prioritizing what most readers care about: the decision itself."

Why both halves matter:
- Writer-oriented-only organizations bury the answer; readers must reconstruct the author's journey.
- Reader-oriented documents that **omit rationale** feel prescriptive and heavy-handed. The infrequent readers who do want the thinking can't find it, and anyone proposing an alternative must argue without knowing the rationale behind current policy. "The absence of that context often transforms a collaborative problem-solving opportunity into a political conflict."

The frequent absence of reader-oriented, fully justified documents is a major opportunity to drive strategy in your organization, not evidence that such documents don't work.

### Unrealistic timelines mark the start of refinement, not the end of thinking

Rushed strategy is the most frequent strategy failure mode the author sees. The failure isn't the deadline itself; it's authors accepting that they must stop thinking when the artificial deadline hits. Claire Hughes Johnson's argument (in *Scaling People*): planning expands to fit any timeline, so she deliberately sets a short annual planning window of several weeks. Some teams treated the window as fixed and stopped planning when it ended; effective teams "never stopped planning before or after the planning window."

Reframe: when given an unrealistic timeline, deliver the best draft you can, then treat the deadline as the start of the refinement process rather than the finish line. It's an open secret that many strategies never leave refinement and are tweaked throughout their lifespans — a strategy with an early deadline is no different.

The catch: executives often impose artificial deadlines precisely to force action and quick thinking, and your colleagues may believe all decisions are final at the deadline even if you don't. So the deadline's meaning must be clarified socially, not just privately reinterpreted.

### Strategy works at every altitude — effective diagnosis trumps authority

Some engineers argue that only executive-level strategy is "real," because any lower-altitude strategy can be invalidated by a new higher-altitude one. (Some executives make the mirror argument: they can't write engineering strategy because a missing product or business strategy might add constraints.) The author rejects this entirely. To do strategy at any altitude, you must accept the certainty that new information will arrive and force revision. Executives are not exempt: at least as many executives' strategies are "ravaged by reality's pervasive details" as are overridden by higher-altitude strategies. Waiting for certainty that nothing will change is the one sure path to failure.

### Strategies don't require stable environments — they require accurate diagnosis of the environment

Chaos comes from many sources: rapid technological evolution (e.g., the LLM ecosystem), rapid hiring or layoffs, load-bearing senior leaders departing, slowing core-business revenue triggering scrambles for a new business. The strategy response is not to wait for stability. In a stable period a strategy might run for years with little deviation; in a dynamic period the authors might know they can only protect capacity in two-week chunks before the next critical initiative lands. Good strategy is possible in both — what's impossible is good strategy without effectively diagnosing which context you're in.

### Missing decisions are the norm, not the exception

Often the path forward would be obvious if a few key decisions were made, you know who owns them, and you simply cannot get a decision. Rather than blocking entirely or pointing fingers, a strategy should explicitly acknowledge what's missing and move forward where it can: block only the parts that genuinely depend on the missing decision, and proceed on the rest. Sometimes moving forward means taking risks; sometimes it means delaying for clarity — "but it never means accepting that you're stuck without options other than pointing a finger."

### Surviving other people's bad strategy: the private shadow strategy

Sometimes you're told to follow a "strategy" that is really just a policy with no strategic thinking behind it — no diagnosis, no operational approach. Causes include leaders who see themselves as the one important decision-maker and don't believe explaining is valuable, and leaders forced to roll out a policy they themselves disagree with (top-down adoption of high-hype technologies like blockchain during the crypto boom). Such leaders find it hard to explain a strategy they don't understand.

The response the author has found most effective: write your own private strategy that treats the bad broader strategy as "a static, unavoidable truth" in its diagnosis. From that honest diagnosis you can make practical decisions that fit the real context, even though you'd never have chosen that context. Keep it private by default; share the thinking only when a colleague says they can't understand your decision-making.

## Actionable guidance

- Write reader-oriented strategy documents: lead with the decisions, then include the full rationale behind them. Never omit the rationale — its absence often turns a collaborative problem-solving opportunity into a political conflict.
- Treat the scarcity of reader-oriented, fully justified documents in your organization as your opening to drive strategy, not as proof the format fails.
- When handed an unrealistic strategy deadline: ship the best draft you can by the deadline, then immediately continue as refinement. Never stop thinking because a planning window closed.
- Clarify with the deadline-setting executive what the deadline actually means (forcing function vs. decision freeze). If you can't reach them directly, find someone with a communication path and articulate the issue clearly; until then, expect colleagues to treat the strategy as literal and unalterable, and work with that. The impasse ends either when you get the conversation or when something goes wrong badly enough that the executive starts paying attention again.
- Don't wait for executive sponsorship or higher-altitude strategy before doing strategy at your altitude. Accept up front that new information will force revisions; build that expectation into the work.
- When a team blames lack of executive engagement for lack of progress, re-diagnose: look for levers (like workflow ergonomics) that need no top-down support.
- Diagnose how stable your environment is and size your commitments to match: multi-year arcs in stable periods, capacity protected in two-week chunks in chaotic ones.
- When a needed decision won't come: name the missing decision explicitly in the strategy, block only the work that truly depends on it, and execute everything else that addresses the underlying problem.
- When forced to execute a thinking-free policy from above: write a private strategy whose diagnosis accepts that policy as immovable context, decide practically within it, and walk colleagues through it only when they ask why you're deciding the way you are.

## Pitfalls and misconceptions

- Dismissing decision-first documents as "superficial" — they prioritize what most readers need.
- Publishing prescriptive policies without rationale, which often turns would-be collaborators into political opponents.
- Stopping strategy thinking when an artificial deadline arrives — the most frequent strategy failure mode.
- Privately reinterpreting a deadline as "start of refinement" while colleagues treat every decision as frozen; the mismatch must be surfaced.
- Believing only executive-altitude strategy is valid, or that missing higher-altitude strategy blocks yours.
- Waiting for certainty that no new information will arrive — the only way to guarantee your strategy fails.
- Assuming strategy needs a stable environment rather than an accurate diagnosis of the current one.
- Treating a stakeholder's missing decision as total blockage, and responding by finger-pointing instead of moving where you can.
- Publicly fighting a bad top-down policy instead of absorbing it into your own diagnosis and deciding well within it.
