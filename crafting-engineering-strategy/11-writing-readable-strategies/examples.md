# Chapter 11: Writing Readable Engineering Strategies — Examples

## The database question, asked three ways

Larson illustrates the three goals of professional writing with one topic phrased three ways. "How do we select databases for our new products?" is thinking-refinement writing, where an academic argument-building structure can be useful because the reasoning is the subject. "What database have we selected for our new analytics product?" is approval-seeking writing, where the academic structure confuses stakeholders who want to review the specific proposal, not the reasoning. "Databases are allowed for new products" is policy communication, where engineers need to understand the permitted options, with rationale available for those who want to understand or challenge it.
Illustrates: the same subject demands different document structures depending on whether the goal is thinking, approval, or policy communication.

## McKinsey and the Pyramid Principle

Most engineers only ever learn academic essay structure as students. Some business consultancies are the exception: McKinsey teaches its new hires Barbara Minto's *The Pyramid Principle* as part of business-writing training. Larson cites this as the rare counterexample to engineers learning document structure only as students.
Illustrates: engineers default to academic structure because no one trained them otherwise.

## Don't Make Me Think applied to strategy readers

Larson borrows the core lesson of Steve Krug's web usability classic *Don't Make Me Think*: users (and readers) don't understand, they muddle through. Strategy readers behave the same way, so expecting them to invest deep effort in your document is "an act of hubris." If the answer isn't quickly findable, they conclude the document has no clear direction.
Illustrates: design strategy documents for skimming, impatient readers, not ideal ones.

## Three kinds of approval meetings

The first kind has no feedback; someone gripes "this meeting could've been an email," but the document is approved. The second has two stakeholder groups with incompatible goals who need a senior decision-maker to break the tie — genuinely valuable, since you generally can't progress without that mediation. The third derails early into questions about your research, whether you considered other options, and whether the work is even relevant. Larson's experience is that the third kind usually happens not because the strategy is wrong, but because the policy wasn't presented up front — stakeholders who would have accepted the policy got pulled into debating thinking they didn't need to agree with.
Illustrates: presenting policy first prevents approval meetings from drowning in rationale debates.

## "I guess the previous engineers here were just dumb"

A team publishes two documents: one with the full strategic thinking, one with only the policy and operations. This tends to work quite well initially, but over time team members depart and new people are hired. Eventually a newcomer challenges the strategy as obviously wrong, usually because their previous employer used different policies. Because the policy-only document omits the diagnosis and exploration, the newcomer can't trace the reasoning and often leaps instead to the simplistic conclusion that the previous engineers were simply incompetent.
Illustrates: keeping the full reasoning in the document (at the end) preserves alignment across staff turnover.

## Refactoring the LLM adoption strategy

The book's LLM adoption strategy (Document 17-1) applies two refactors beyond simple inversion. It merges the Refine section into Diagnose, keeping the map and models closer to the specific topics it explores. It discards the Operation section entirely and places each operational detail alongside the policy it applies to in the Policy section.
Illustrates: strategy refactoring in practice — dissolving and merging sections so readers engage with the full implications of the strategy at once.

## Reading a stranger's strategy document

To quickly build empathy for readers, Larson suggests reading someone else's strategy document that you aren't familiar with. If its authors followed the academic structure, you will quickly appreciate how challenging it can be to identify the actual proposal.
Illustrates: the fastest way to feel the cost of thinking-first structure is to be the unfamiliar reader yourself.
