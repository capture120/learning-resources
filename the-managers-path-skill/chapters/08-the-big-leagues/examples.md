# Chapter 8: The Big Leagues — Examples and Anecdotes

## "What Is My Job?" — guest essay by James Turnbull

**Situation:** James Turnbull came into engineering from a nontraditional background and carried a strong sense of impostor syndrome around colleagues with formal computer science training, especially when leading people he saw as more technical than himself.

**What happened / what was done:** That insecurity, combined with a desire to be seen as smart and "right," pulled him into unproductive technology debates. He would argue the merits of a language or technology choice purely on technical grounds — becoming just another engineer arguing in a group of engineers. Over time he realized his job was not to be the smartest person in the room or to be right; it was to help the team make the best possible decisions and implement them sustainably and efficiently. He still cares about technology, but a good leader shapes technology discussions to inject strategic objectives and account for the nontechnical implications of technical decisions.

**Lesson:** Senior leadership is not about being the lead engineer, chasing the newest framework, or having the shiniest technology. It is about building a team with the tools and attributes to build the best possible product for customers.

## Figurehead CTOs without management power

**Situation:** At a previous employer, the most senior members of technical staff in large business areas often held the title of CTO for their area. They were highly respected, technically capable, understood the business and its technical challenges, and were called on to inspire engineers and help with recruiting.

**What happened / what was done:** Despite the title and respect, these CTOs had trouble being successful. They lacked direct management oversight of any teams, and because technology in that company was viewed as an execution arm, they had little strategic influence.

**Lesson:** A leader with no power over business strategy and no ability to allocate people to important tasks is at best at the mercy of influence with other executives, and at worst a figurehead. You cannot give up the responsibility of management without giving up the power that comes with it; a CTO who relinquishes all management gives up the most important lever they ever had over business strategy.

## "Wanting to be a CTO is like wanting to be married"

**Situation:** Fournier, having served as both VP of Engineering and CTO (both via promotion), reflects on advice about chasing senior titles.

**What happened / what was done:** Her own answers to the "where do I fit" questions are a mix: she was always thinking about technical architecture, and when the job required it she was happy to focus on the organization — but an organizational focus alone doesn't keep her motivated. She likes thinking about organizational structure yet gets bored with the details of process and roadmap planning, and needs some technical and business strategy oversight to stay engaged. Her own VP of Engineering once told her: "Wanting to be a CTO (or VP of Engineering) is like wanting to be married. Remember that it's not just the title, it's also the company and the people that matter."

**Lesson:** Titles are not everything. The job is defined by the specific company, stage, and people, not by the words on the business card.

## The CEO's morning revelation

**Situation:** A CEO wakes up one morning and sees an opportunity for a new product line that could push the business into a new stage of growth. She sketches the vision and presents it to the senior leadership team, who get on board and begin moving.

**What happened / what was done:** Change doesn't happen quickly. There are in-flight projects to worry about; some work is almost complete and would be a shame to cancel. Teams are slow to converge on the new initiative — until the question comes down: "So, why aren't you working on the top priority?" Leaders removed from day-to-day schedules forget that teams have priority lists mapped out weeks or months ago and expect change to happen immediately.

**Lesson:** Expect this question from your boss, and when you're tempted to ask it of your own teams, first ask why they don't understand the priorities. Often nobody explicitly went through in-flight work to kill or postpone things; declaring a top priority is different from making the schedule tradeoffs that get people moving on it. Being taken to task over priorities is a sign that you and the CEO have a misaligned understanding of reality and need to get on the same page.

## Setting strategy for the Rent the Runway board

**Situation:** In the summer of 2014, as SVP of Engineering at Rent the Runway, Fournier learned her CEO wanted to put her up for promotion to CTO at the next board meeting — but as part of that promotion she would present the technology strategy to the board. The CEO returned every attempt until the work met her standards.

**What happened / what was done:** Fournier suspects the exercise wasn't required for the promotion (the board was happy with team growth and the platform's stability and feature throughput), but she's grateful for it. She got coaching from the CEO and an executive CTO coach, solicited input from executive peers, and posed theoretical questions to senior engineers. Her process: research the team, technology, and company (engineer pain points, where executives expected growth, scaling challenges, productivity bottlenecks, the technology landscape around personalization and mobile); then sit alone with a whiteboard slicing systems and teams across attributes (customer-facing vs. internal-operations tools like warehouse and customer service, backend vs. frontend), where she realized the technology modeled most of the business's data — a unique insight into data flow and possible evolution axes; then draft actionable ideas about operational efficiency, features, and growth (e.g., should personalization systems operate on the real-time state of the world, or act more like view adjustments on data subsets?). The CEO rejected two things along the way: an underdeveloped plan that was almost entirely system and architecture detail with few ideas beyond 6–12 months and no link to business drivers, and a sparse, speaker-style slide deck — boards read decks before meetings and need information-dense documents. The result transformed her from a reactive leader into a forward-thinking one: she could show engineers a vision of the technology platform, and the architecture led the technology organization's strategy, which in turn shaped some of the company's organizational strategy.

**Lesson:** Good technology strategy covers architecture, team structure, and the underpinnings of the business; it "enables the many potential futures of the business" rather than reacting to current problems. The hardest part is getting started; the second hardest is getting comfortable guessing about the future with highly imperfect information. And tailor the artifact to the audience — a board deck is read, not presented.

## How you announced your own departure

**Situation:** A thought experiment within the advice on delivering bad news: one piece of news you may someday deliver is that you are leaving.

**What happened / what was done:** When people resign or move teams, they don't announce it by memo (except maybe to HR). They pull people aside and tell them face-to-face if they'd want to know before it's public. There may be a going-away party, a farewell letter, or a final talk about what they learned.

**Lesson:** Think about how you would like to be told. It's OK in some circumstances to celebrate sad changes, as long as it's done with grace — and all of these lessons apply to delivering hard news to your team.

## Reporting to a nontechnical CEO at Rent the Runway

**Situation:** Fournier's first experience with a fully nontechnical manager came when she started reporting to the CEO at Rent the Runway.

**What happened / what was done:** She found reporting to a nontechnical manager can be a total culture clash and developed practices to manage it: avoid jargon and excessive technical detail, run your own 1-1s with a prepared topic list (sending agendas ahead and befriending the executive assistant), bring solutions rather than problems, ask for advice, repeat important issues (three times is often the magic number), be supportive, and seek coaching and skill development outside the company since a boss is not a manager.

**Lesson:** With a nontechnical boss, you own the relationship: you filter information, drive the agenda, and find your professional development elsewhere.

## A friend dating someone you don't love

**Situation:** An analogy for handling disagreement with executive peers' management styles or use of their expertise in areas that don't directly affect your team.

**What happened / what was done:** Treat such disagreement the way you'd treat a good friend who dates people you don't love: unless asked for your opinion, stay out of it as much as possible, and bring kindness to any disagreement you choose to raise.

**Lesson:** Cross-functional peers own their areas and you own yours. Respectful deference on a peer's turf is fundamental to first-team trust; the right venue for real disagreement is one-on-one or the leadership team meeting.

## The specter of Steve Jobs

**Situation:** An illustration of how a senior leader's modeled behavior outlives their presence in any given room.

**What happened / what was done:** People who overlapped with Steve Jobs at Apple talk about "Steve" and his impact on projects they worked on. Apple employees used the specter of Steve to argue for and against decisions — as a moral compass for what the organization should be doing.

**Lesson:** The culture you build and reinforce will have some of this effect at your company. People may not invoke your name, but they learn the behaviors you model and copy them: yelling teaches that yelling is OK; openly making mistakes and apologizing teaches that mistakes are OK; the questions you always ask become the questions people ask themselves; the roles you visibly value are the ones ambitious people pursue. Use this power for good.

## Playing favorites by staying "one of the team"

**Situation:** Fournier addresses why senior leaders must socially detach from organizations they may have personally built.

**What happened / what was done:** She advises going for one drink at happy hour and then leaving, because closing down the bar with your whole organization has bad consequences for everyone. From her own experience: maintaining strong social ties with people who report up to you leads to actually playing favorites, and having her team believe she played favorites made the overall team unhappy and made her job a lot harder.

**Lesson:** Detach socially with regularity. The accusation of favoritism is usually accurate when strong social ties exist, and a "buddy" image leaves reports unable to distinguish a friend thinking out loud from a boss telling them what to focus on.

## Camille and Michael: two good leaders

**Situation:** A parable opens the "Ruling with Fear, Guiding with Trust" section. Camille is a good leader: technical, charismatic, decisive, gets things done. But she is short-tempered; when people fall short or things go wrong, she is visibly annoyed and openly angry. Michael has the same strengths but keeps his cool: when things seem off, he gets curious and asks questions, which often lead the team to their own realizations about what's going wrong.

**What happened / what was done:** Camille's people don't want to risk being blamed for failure or openly criticized for mistakes, so they take fewer risks and hide their mistakes. She has accidentally created a culture of fear. Michael's curiosity, by contrast, draws information out.

**Lesson:** Identical strengths produce opposite cultures depending on how the leader reacts to failure. Anger and visible blame teach hiding; curiosity teaches openness.

## Fournier's own first review as a senior leader

**Situation:** The Camille of the parable above is real: it is Fournier's own experience accidentally creating a culture of fear when she became a senior leader, coming from the finance industry where harsh criticism was normal culture.

**What happened / what was done:** Her very first review as a senior leader included this feedback: even people who loved her admitted being fearful of her and her potential criticism; people were afraid to take risks or fail in front of her for fear of public reprimand in front of peers; her attacks had created a culture where team members were afraid to engage with her, ask questions, or ask for feedback — a vicious cycle of her not trusting them and them making mistakes. It was shocking and uncomfortable. She could make excuses (people take criticism more harshly from women; finance culture; "everyone needs to toughen up"), but it was clearly a problem: people afraid to take risks cannot become an independent team that sets its own direction. Her correction program: practice relatedness (she had focused so hard on efficiency — diving straight into the issue, the status update, the problem — that she had no personal relationships with her team, making them afraid of how she'd react to mistakes and questions), apologize briefly and honestly when she screwed up, get curious instead of attacking when she disagreed, and hold people accountable without making them "bad."

**Lesson:** Culture of fear often comes from valuing being correct, hierarchy-affinity, and backgrounds where open conflict was normal — engineering's debate culture included. When you become the leader, the dynamic changes: people who would have fought back when you were a peer now feel threatened. Fear-but-respect can survive while the company grows and the problems are interesting, but people with better options leave the moment other frustrations appear. She knows firsthand that being feared but respected isn't enough.

## Apologies that fit in one sentence

**Situation:** Part of correcting a culture of fear: leaders worry that apologizing makes them look weak.

**What happened / what was done:** Fournier offers model apologies: "I'm sorry, I should not have yelled at you and I have no excuse for my bad behavior." "I'm sorry, I did not listen to you and I know I contributed to your frustration at this situation." "I'm sorry, I made a mistake when I neglected to tell you about Bob." Apologies should be short and take responsibility; drawn-out apologies often become excuses or distractions.

**Lesson:** The goal is showing people that you know your behavior impacts others, and role-modeling that making mistakes is OK but hurting people warrants an apology. Apologizing doesn't make a leader weaker; it makes the whole team stronger.

## Setting True North as CTO of a commerce company

**Situation:** As CTO at a commerce company (Rent the Runway), Fournier had to define the baseline of technical excellence — "True North" — for the organization.

**What happened / what was done:** She set True North for the most fundamental technical decision making: production readiness, scaling, systems design, architecture, testing, and language choice. That didn't mean she made all of those decisions; she guided the standards by which such decisions would be evaluated. For mobile and UI-specific development, she delegated True North but pushed the senior technical staff in those areas to articulate what their standards looked like.

**Lesson:** A technical leader sets the standards rather than making every call, and delegates standard-setting deliberately rather than leaving gaps. True North leaders rely on instincts honed over years of staying technical, which is why depth earned early in a career matters: it enables fast, sound judgment calls later, when there's no time to dig into details.
