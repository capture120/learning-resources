# Management 101 — Examples and Anecdotes

## "Benign neglect" as a best-case manager
**Situation:** Fournier's friends, asked about the best managers of their careers, describe them as managing with "benign neglect": the engineer just kind of knows what to work on, and the manager leaves them alone entirely. In the most extreme case, one person reported meeting with his manager only twice in six months — and one of those meetings was to receive a promotion.
**What happened / what was done:** Fournier uses this to show how low the bar is. Compared with neglectful managers who only surface to announce you're failing, micromanagers who question every detail, or abusive managers who ignore you until they want to yell, being left alone doesn't seem so bad. But she insists other options exist: managers who care about you as a person, grow your career, teach skills, give feedback, and help you focus on what matters.
**Lesson:** If you think neglect is the best management can offer, you've simply never seen good management. Knowing what good looks like lets you ask for what you need.

## The introvert's excuse
**Situation:** Fournier anticipates readers assuming she is the sort who loves making lots of work friends and therefore can't understand their reluctance to connect; she preempts this by stating she is not a buddy-buddy person at work. Some people give themselves a pass on caring about colleagues because they're introverts or don't want to make friends at work.
**What happened / what was done:** She states plainly that she does understand not finding the human side of the workplace interesting — and that being an introvert is still no excuse for making no effort to treat people like real human beings. Strong teams are built on human connection, and connection requires the willingness to be vulnerable. Practically, letting your manager into your life a little means that during stressful events (a death in the family, a new child, a breakup, housing trouble) it's much easier to ask for time off or support, and a great manager will notice when your energy changes and care enough to ask.
**Lesson:** You don't need to be extroverted or chummy to build the minimum human connection that makes a manager relationship work; you do need to make the effort.

## Ask the CTO: Big Ambitions (Fournier's advice column)
**Situation:** A new graduate writes in: they've just started working but already know their career goal is to someday become a CTO. What should they do now?
**What happened / what was done:** Fournier's answer, from her own experience: when she was first out of college she didn't really know how to do the job at all, because day-to-day tech work is very different from school. Her advice: first learn how to work — seek a workplace with mentorship and training in the craft of the job (testing, project and product management, collaboration) as well as new technical skills. Find the best managers and mentors you can and watch them work; work for people who push you, reward your success, and inspire you to stretch. Recognize that stretching means more than new technologies — great CTOs have strong communication, project management, and product sense — while still spending years getting genuinely good at writing and understanding high-quality code. Build a strong peer network, because early-career peers (schoolmates, teammates, conference and meetup contacts) turn into future jobs, and CTOs must learn to socialize across companies even if they're shy. Finally, most CTOs run small companies and are often technical cofounders of startups, so work at a company with a track record of alumni leaving to found companies — that's where you meet future cofounders and get early entry into new ventures.
**Lesson:** The path to CTO is built early through work fundamentals, deliberate observation of great leaders, broad skills beyond technology, deep coding competence, and a peer network — not through a title chase.

## Fournier's recurring career uncertainty
**Situation:** Fournier illustrates the claim that everyone goes through periods of career uncertainty, often in the first two to five years out of school — and, in her own case, recurring roughly every 5 to 10 years after.
**What happened / what was done:** She felt so unsettled early in her career that she went to grad school for a couple of years — which turned out to be a quest for security in the familiar academic setting and an escape from a job she didn't know how to navigate well. She hit uncertainty again after climbing the technical ladder at a big company, only to feel somewhat powerless. She hit it a third time after climbing the management ladder, when she encountered the challenges of executive leadership. Given her track record, she expects it every 5 to 10 years until she retires.
**Lesson:** Career uncertainty recurs at every level; getting the job you thought you wanted eventually stops being enjoyable (the cool startup is a mess, management isn't rewarding the way you expected). The only person who can pull you through is you — use your manager to learn what's possible where you are, but look inward to decide where to go next.

## The strong engineer who is the wrong manager
**Situation:** Engineers often evaluate prospective managers by whether they like them personally or respect them as engineers.
**What happened / what was done:** Fournier draws the distinction: strong managers know how to play the game at their company — they can get you promoted, get you attention and feedback from important people, and use their networks to get you jobs even after you stop working for them. Plenty of great engineers make ineffective managers because they don't know or don't want to deal with the politics of leadership. A strong engineer may make a great mentor-manager for someone early in their career, yet be a terrible advocate-manager for someone more senior who needs organizational clout.
**Lesson:** When evaluating a job, weigh the manager — and weigh them for advocacy strength appropriate to your career stage, not for friendship or engineering prowess.

## Casey Stengel's epigraph
**Situation:** The chapter opens with a quotation from baseball manager Casey Stengel: the secret of managing is keeping the people who hate you away from the ones who haven't made up their minds.
**What happened / what was done:** The quip frames the chapter's starting question: you want to be a good manager, but do you even know what one looks like? Have you ever had one?
**Lesson:** Most people's mental model of management is cynical or absent; the chapter exists to replace it with concrete expectations.
