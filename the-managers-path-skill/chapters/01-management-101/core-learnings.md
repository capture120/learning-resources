# Management 101 — Core Learnings

## What this chapter is about

Everyone's first experience of management is being managed, and that experience is the foundation of your own management philosophy. This chapter defines what you should expect from a good manager — one-on-ones, feedback, and career support — and what you owe in return: knowing what you want, advocating for yourself, and cutting your manager reasonable slack.

## The landscape of managers

Many engineers have never had a good manager, so they don't know what to expect. The common patterns, from least to most damaging:

- **Benign neglect.** The manager leaves you alone entirely; you just figure out what to work on. Some engineers even call this their "best" management experience, which says more about the alternatives than about the approach.
- **Neglectful managers.** They ignore you when you need help, avoid meeting with you, never give feedback, then suddenly tell you that you're underperforming or unpromotable.
- **Micromanagers.** They question every detail and refuse to let you make any decisions.
- **Actively abusive managers.** They neglect you until they want to yell at you.

All of these characters walk around our companies, wreaking havoc on the mental health of their teams. Against that backdrop, being left alone seems fine. But there's a real alternative: managers who care about you as a person, actively work on your career growth, teach skills, give valuable feedback, help you navigate difficult situations, help you figure out what you need to learn, want you to take their job someday, and — most importantly — help you understand what's important to focus on and enable that focus. At minimum, expect the tasks below and learn to ask for what you need.

## One-on-one meetings

1-1s with your direct manager are an essential feature of a good working relationship, yet many managers neglect them or make them feel like wasted time. They serve two purposes:

1. **Human connection.** Not small talk for its own sake, but letting your manager into your life a little. When something stressful happens (a death in the family, a new child, a breakup, housing trouble), it's far easier to ask for time off or support from someone who has context on you as a person. Great managers notice when your normal energy level changes and care enough to ask about it. Trust — real trust — requires the ability and willingness to be vulnerable in front of each other. Being an introvert is not an excuse for refusing to treat colleagues as human beings; human connection is the bedrock of strong teams.
2. **A regular, private channel.** A predictable, scheduled opportunity to discuss whatever needs discussing. Predictability matters because you should plan for the meeting — the agenda is not solely your manager's job.

Guidance:

- **Expect a few minutes of personal talk — not a whole meeting of it.** Human connection doesn't mean spending the whole 1-1 talking about hobbies, families, or weekend small talk. It means your manager will hopefully treat you like a human who has a life outside of work, and spend a few minutes talking about that life when you meet.
- Expect 1-1s on a predictable schedule so you can prepare. Put thought into what you want to discuss beforehand.
- Frequency can flex. Doing them every few weeks is fine — just don't eliminate them. If you want more frequency, ask.
- **Good 1-1s are not status meetings.** Exception: if you're a manager reporting to senior management, the 1-1 may legitimately cover status of critical projects or nascent work that isn't written down anywhere yet. For an individual contributor, a status-report 1-1 is repetitive and boring — move status to email or chat and bring real topics instead.
- **Share responsibility for making 1-1s good.** Come with an agenda. Prepare. If your manager cancels or reschedules regularly, push for a more stable slot. If that fails, confirm the day before (or that morning for an afternoon meeting) and share what you want to discuss so the manager knows the meeting matters.

## Feedback and workplace guidance

The second thing to expect from a manager is feedback — not just at performance-review time.

- You will inevitably screw up, and a good manager tells you quickly. It's uncomfortable, especially for people new to the workforce who've never had behavioral feedback from anyone but their parents. But the only thing worse than getting behavioral feedback is not getting it at all, or only hearing it at your annual review. The sooner you know about bad habits, the easier they are to correct.
- The same applies to praise: a great manager notices the little things you do well day-to-day and recognizes them.
- Track the feedback you receive, good and bad, and use it in your yearly self-review.
- **Praise in public, criticize in private** is the ideal. Quick critical feedback right after a meeting is not necessarily a sign your behavior was terrible — good managers know fast delivery beats waiting for a convenient moment. Public praise lets the team see what laudable behavior looks like. If you hate public praise, tell your manager; don't suffer in silence.
- **Ask for feedback beyond behavior.** Presentations, design docs, written work — peers review your code, but your manager should be a resource for improving everything else. Asking for advice also signals respect; managers, like everyone, like to feel helpful.
- **Your manager should be your number-one ally for your role at the company.** If your company has a career ladder and you want a promotion, ask what areas to focus on. If you're struggling with a teammate or someone on another team, your manager should be there to help you navigate the situation — including working directly with the other person or team as necessary to get you to resolution. But this requires you to speak up: don't expect a promotion to appear magically or interpersonal problems to be addressed if you never raise them.
- Good managers assign stretch projects that grow you — and beyond that, they help you see the value of work that isn't fun or glamorous, showing how it fits the team's goals. Mundane work becomes a source of pride when you understand its contribution to the company's success.
- **Seniority changes the feedback.** As you become more senior, personal feedback (good and bad) decreases — you're operating at a higher level, and your manager is operating at a very high level. Expect the feedback to shift from personal toward team- and strategy-related input. It becomes even more important that you drive your own 1-1s and bring topics, because your manager is unlikely to spend much time on you outside performance reviews.

## Training and career growth

- Your manager is your main liaison to the company's bureaucracy and holds some responsibility for helping you find training and growth resources: a conference, a class, a book, or a pointer to an expert elsewhere in the company.
- This is not universal. Some companies run training through a dedicated arm you tap directly; small companies may have no budget or see training as an unnecessary perk.
- **Wherever you work, expect to be largely responsible for figuring out what training you want** — especially as an individual contributor seeking technical training. Your manager won't have a list of conferences at his fingertips.
- The other direct contribution is **promotion and, probably, compensation.** In committee-based promotion systems, your manager guides you through preparing your promotion packet — the set of materials the committee will review. Where the management hierarchy decides directly, your manager is essential in advocating for and securing your promotion.
- However promotions happen at your company, your manager should have an idea of whether you're qualified to be promoted. When you want a promotion, ask for specific areas to focus on. Managers usually can't guarantee promotions, but good ones know what the system rewards and help you build those achievements and skills.
- This only goes so far: at senior levels, promotion opportunities are rare, and you may need to find and propose the achievements that qualify you for the next level yourself.

## Ask the CTO: Big ambitions

Question: a new graduate already knows they want to become a CTO someday — what should they do now? Distilled advice:

- First, learn how to work. Day-to-day tech work differs greatly from school. Seek a workplace offering mentorship and training in the craft of the job — testing, project and product management, collaboration — alongside new technical skills.
- Find the best managers and mentors you can and watch them work. Work for people who push you to succeed, reward you for it, and inspire you to stretch yourself.
- Stretching is more than learning new technologies: great CTOs have strong communication skills, project management skills, and product sense, in addition to technical sense.
- Still spend a lot of time writing code and getting genuinely good at understanding how high-quality code is written. This takes years of focus and can't be rushed.
- Build a strong peer network. Early-career engineers underestimate how their current peers — schoolmates, teammates, conference and meetup contacts — become their future job opportunities. Shyness is OK, but CTOs must learn to socialize widely and build cross-company networks.
- Most CTOs lead small companies; they're often technical cofounders of startups. If that's the path, work somewhere with a track record of alumni founding companies — that's where you'll meet future cofounders and find early entry into new companies.

## How to be managed

Part of becoming a good manager is figuring out how to be managed — related to, but not the same as, managing up. Develop ownership and authority over your own work experience instead of relying on your manager to set the entire tone of the relationship.

### Spend time thinking about what you want

- Your manager can point out opportunities, show you projects, and give feedback — but she cannot read your mind or tell you what will make you happy. Whether you're brand new or 20 years in, that burden is yours.
- Expect recurring periods of career uncertainty. Many people feel very uncertain in their first two to five years out of school, as they settle into independent adulthood; Fournier, based on her own track record, expects it every 5 to 10 years until she retires. It's nearly universal that once you get the job you wanted, the enjoyment fades and you start looking for something else. The cool startup turns out to be a mess; management turns out to be hard and not rewarding in the ways you expected.
- The only person who can pull you through that uncertainty is you. Use your manager to discover what's possible where you are, but look inward to figure out where you want to go next.

### You are responsible for yourself

Knowing yourself is step one; going after what you want is step two.

- Bring agendas to your 1-1s when you have things to discuss.
- Ask for the projects you want to work on. Advocate for yourself. If your manager isn't helpful, find help elsewhere.
- Seek out feedback, including constructive feedback on areas to improve — and take it graciously even when you disagree.
- When you're persistently unhappy, say something. When you're stuck, ask for help. When you want a raise, ask for it. When you want a promotion, find out what you need to do to get it.
- **Your manager cannot force work-life balance on you.** If you want to go home, figure out how to get your work done and go home — even if that means going against the cultural grain and feeling uncomfortable setting boundaries. Conversely, if you want a bigger job, expect to work more hours to get it.
- You won't get everything you ask for, and asking isn't comfortable, but it's the fastest way forward. A conscientious manager will appreciate the candor; a manager who likes you less for asking has told you something valuable about your situation. There's no guarantee it goes well, but if you've set a goal, you owe it to yourself to pursue it.

### Give your manager a break

- Managing is a job. Your manager will be stressed, imperfect, will say dumb things, will do things that feel unfair or harmful, will hand you work you don't want, and will get annoyed when you complain. Her job is to do the best thing for the company and the team — not to make you happy all the time.
- The relationship is like any close interpersonal relationship: the only person you can change is yourself. Give your manager feedback, but accept she may not listen or change no matter how much you think she should.
- If you find yourself starting to actively resent your manager, you probably need to move to a different team or look for a new job. If you resent every manager you work for, consider whether the cause is them or you — maybe you'd be happier somewhere without a manager.
- Especially as you become senior, your manager expects **solutions, not problems.** Don't make every 1-1 about what you need, what's wrong, or what you want. When you have a problem, instead of demanding the manager solve it, ask how she might approach it. Asking for advice is always a good way to show respect and trust.

### Choose your managers wisely

- Your manager can make a huge difference in your career. When evaluating job opportunities, weigh the manager alongside the job, the company, and the pay.
- Strong managers know how to play the game at their company: they can get you promoted, get you attention and feedback from important people, and — through their networks — get you jobs even after you stop working for them.
- A strong manager is not the same as a manager you like as a friend, or even one you respect as an engineer. Plenty of great engineers make ineffective managers because they don't know or don't want to deal with the politics of leadership. A strong engineer may be a great mentor-manager for someone early in their career but a terrible advocate-manager for someone more senior.

## Pitfalls and anti-patterns

- Treating benign neglect as good management because you've only seen worse.
- Letting 1-1s degrade into status meetings, or letting them get repeatedly canceled without pushing back.
- Suffering in silence: expecting promotions, raises, or conflict resolution without ever raising the topic.
- Saving all feedback (giving or receiving) for the annual performance review.
- Using introversion as an excuse to avoid human connection with colleagues.
- Expecting your manager to manage your work-life balance, your career direction, or your happiness.
- Making every 1-1 a list of complaints and demands, especially as you grow senior.
- Picking jobs based only on company, role, and pay while ignoring who the manager is — or confusing "manager I'd like as a friend" with "manager who can advocate for me."

## Assessing your own experience

- Have you had a manager you considered good? What did that manager do that you found valuable?
- How often do you meet 1-1 with your manager? Do you bring topics to discuss? If your 1-1 is a status meeting, can you convey that status some other way?
- Do you feel you can tell your manager when you have a major life event? Does your manager know something about you personally?
- Has your manager delivered good feedback to you? Bad feedback? Any feedback at all?
- Has your manager helped you set any work-related goals for this year?
