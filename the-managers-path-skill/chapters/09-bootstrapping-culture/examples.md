# Bootstrapping Culture — Examples and Anecdotes

## Setting up culture from scratch at Rent the Runway
**Situation:** When Fournier joined Rent the Runway as a senior engineering leader, the team was still running on the classic, unstructured "scrappy startup" model with few cultural structures in place.
**What happened / what was done:** She had the opportunity to introduce many cultural structures and practices to the team and its members — career ladders, values work, team reorganization, and engineering processes — and treats the chapter as the lessons from that experience.
**Lesson:** Setting the culture of the engineering function is part of the senior leader's job, and first-time CTOs commonly underestimate how important it is to be clear and thoughtful about it.

## Startup surveys that equate structure with bureaucracy
**Situation:** Surveys of startup teams asked about introducing structure and process.
**What happened / what was done:** Respondents reacted with words like "slow" and "innovation-crushing," believing structure is why large companies move slowly, foster bureaucracy, and bore bright people.
**Lesson:** Skeptics hear "structure" and "process" as threats. Reframe the conversation: talk about learning instead of structure and transparency instead of process, because the real goal is learning from successes and failures and sharing those lessons.

## Companies that forgo titles
**Situation:** Some young companies decide to have no job titles at all.
**What happened / what was done:** By foregoing titles, they remove an entire category of future decisions — no deciding anyone's title, no promotion machinery, no apparatus for future title decisions.
**Lesson:** "Deciding not to decide" is a legitimate early-stage strategy; at the scale of a few people, the question genuinely does not matter yet.

## Jo Freeman's "The Tyranny of Structurelessness"
**Situation:** Freeman wrote about early feminist/anarchist collectives that claimed to operate without structure.
**What happened / what was done:** She showed that pretending to lack structure creates hidden power structures, and enumerated the only conditions under which unstructured groups actually work: the group is task-oriented, small and homogeneous, communicates intensely, and has low skill specialization (everyone is interchangeable). Communication overhead grows geometrically with participants, capping effective unstructured groups at about five people, or 10–15 if composed of overlapping subgroups.
**Lesson:** These conditions describe early startup engineering teams almost exactly — full-stack hires sourced from the current team's professional and social networks, collocated, acting as the execution arm of the product or founder. Such teams are either less self-directed than members believe or run by hidden hierarchies, often both.

## Spaghetti code as structurelessness in the codebase
**Situation:** Early startups routinely accumulate spaghetti code.
**What happened / what was done:** When work is done to satisfy immediate tasks, in one unified codebase, by a team of interchangeable generalists, the result is tweaks and hacks rather than thoughtful structure. Later, making it scalable requires refactoring — which is the act of identifying and explicitly drawing out structure.
**Lesson:** The structureless-team dynamic applies to technical artifacts as much as to organizations; structure is how software, teams, and processes scale.

## The five-person team with rigid hierarchy
**Situation:** A hypothetical team of five where the fifth reports to the fourth, who reports to the third, and so on down a chain — or a tiny struggling business spending meetings deciding which toilet paper to stock.
**What happened / what was done:** Nothing productive; the example exists to show misplaced priorities.
**Lesson:** Structure can come too early and harm a group that should be focused on other things. (Though in small companies, structure arriving too late is the more common failure.)

## The founder who keeps making every decision
**Situation:** In a small company, one person gets used to making all decisions and changing his mind frequently. This works fine when it is just him and a couple of others.
**What happened / what was done:** He keeps operating the same way with a team of 10, then 20, then 50.
**Lesson:** The result is high confusion and wasted effort; the cost of each change of mind grows more and more expensive as the team scales. This is the classic "structure came too late" pattern.

## On Freud's race car, commercial flight, and spaceship (named contribution)
**Situation:** On Freud, a friend of Fournier's who has been in engineering management at several startups, offered an analogy for startup leadership across growth stages.
**What happened / what was done:** The earliest startup is a race car: close to the ground, feeling every move, fast-turning, at constant risk of a crash that takes down only the driver. Growth brings a commercial flight: farther from the ground, more lives at stake, movements made more carefully, but the plane still turns relatively quickly. Eventually it is a spaceship: no quick moves, course set long in advance, but capable of going very far while carrying tons of people.
**Lesson:** Recognize the size of the vessel you are steering — determined by headcount, company age, existing infrastructure, and risk tolerance — and adjust how you make and change decisions accordingly.

## Gall's law from Systemantics
**Situation:** John Gall's book *Systemantics* describes how systems actually work and fail.
**What happened / what was done:** Gall's law: a complex system that works invariably evolved from a simple system that worked; a complex system designed from scratch never works and cannot be patched into working — you must start over with a working simple system.
**Lesson:** Companies start as simple systems and evolve complexity. Do not overdesign team structure or process while the team is small and functioning well; evolve structure in response to real failures.

## The career-path quit threshold
**Situation:** A leader is wondering whether the lack of a career ladder is a problem.
**What happened / what was done:** One person quitting over the lack of a career path may not be enough to justify building a ladder, but multiple people quitting or candidates refusing to join should trigger reconsideration.
**Lesson:** Use failure frequency and cost to decide when to add structure; weigh the value the absence of structure gives the team against the cost of losing people you want to employ.

## Frequent software releases becoming safe
**Situation:** For a long time, releasing software frequently was difficult and expensive because software was shipped to users, making any shipped bug costly.
**What happened / what was done:** In the modern SaaS world, bugs can be fixed easily, so the risk of shipping a bug is now much lower than the risk of failing to expand features fast enough to keep up with competition.
**Lesson:** Technology changes can flip the risk calculus that justified an old structure. Unconditional attachment to old structures is as dangerous as refusing to adopt structure when needed.

## Three failures caused by missing structure
**Situation:** A growing team experiences recurring problems.
**What happened / what was done:** Every new hire slows the team down for months (no onboarding process); people regularly leave because there is no path to advancement or growth (no career ladder); a third production outage occurs because someone logged directly into the database and accidentally dropped a critical table (no operational guardrails).
**Lesson:** Each of these is a failure due to lack of structure. Adding structure is fundamentally about identifying the causes of frequent failures and changing something to solve them — it is about learning.

## "Engineers who graduated from MIT" versus actual values
**Situation:** Companies fear that defining core values will create discrimination.
**What happened / what was done:** Fournier contrasts two framings: "engineers who graduated from MIT" — not a culture, and a filter only a narrow subset of humanity can pass — versus "people who value technology innovation, hard work, intellect, scientific process, and data," which admits a much broader set of people while ensuring shared values.
**Lesson:** A thoughtfully created set of genuine values reduces surface discrimination and broadens the community, rather than narrowing it.

## Rent the Runway tech team values: diversity and learning
**Situation:** Rent the Runway had company-level core values; Fournier led the tech team within it.
**What happened / what was done:** The tech team mapped company values onto itself and added its own: it explicitly valued diversity — caring about what candidates could do and their potential rather than whether they fit checkbox screening criteria — and layered a learning culture on top of company values because that mattered specifically to engineers.
**Lesson:** Define culture by mapping company values onto your team and adding or interpreting values for your context; expect every subteam to develop a slightly distinct culture.

## "Keeping it dope" shoutouts at all-hands
**Situation:** Rent the Runway's technology department held all-hands meetings.
**What happened / what was done:** People gave shoutouts to each other for "keeping it dope" and going above and beyond. Fournier found the exercise personally uncomfortable — she is shy about public praise and sharing feelings — but pushed through by connecting with the part of herself that cares about the people she works with.
**Lesson:** Reinforce culture by publicly rewarding people for exhibiting its values; the stories a community tells bond it together, and they can be shared without being forced or fake.

## Learning "happiness and positivity is a choice"
**Situation:** One of Rent the Runway's actual core values was "happiness and positivity is a choice." Fournier came from a fairly professional, critical work culture and did not arrive with a background of workplace happiness.
**What happened / what was done:** She learned to appreciate looking at things in a positive light. It was never the easiest value for her to adopt and she did not fully adopt it, but it was not a deal breaker, and she kept her critical eye.
**Lesson:** People can change to adopt values they do not start with. Imperfect alignment on one value is survivable; use core values to coach people in misaligned areas, turning ambiguous friction into something articulable.

## The airport test and hiring friends
**Situation:** Many interview processes assess "culture fit" through friendship markers such as "would you like being stuck in an airport with this person?"
**What happened / what was done:** Fournier notes she has had great working relationships with people she would not chat with outside work, and terrible working relationships with people she would happily be stuck in an airport with. Friendships form around shared background experiences, which correlate closely with schooling, race, class, and gender.
**Lesson:** Culture fit is not about hiring friends. Friendship-based fit tests are almost certain to be discriminatory, and the shortcuts gained by hiring friends are not usually the values a strong team needs. Be specific about values match instead.

## Compatible values, incompatible contexts
**Situation:** Evaluating whether particular people fit particular teams.
**What happened / what was done:** A very smart engineer who deeply values independence may not fit a team where everyone must collaborate extensively on all projects. Someone who believes the most analytical argument always wins may not work well in a company that values empathy and intuition over pure analytical skill.
**Lesson:** All of these values are good in some situations and incompatible in others — which is exactly why explicit, written values are a powerful measure for candidates, praise, and performance reviews.

## The salary review that exposed the missing ladder
**Situation:** Rent the Runway's HR team ran a salary review for the engineering team.
**What happened / what was done:** Fournier realized there was no salary structure at all: most people were paid based on a combination of their previous job's salary and their negotiating skills. The team also struggled to define who they should be hiring — what "senior engineer" even meant, and what about management roles.
**Lesson:** The time for adding structure is usually when things are failing; an unfair, incoherent pay situation was the failure that justified building a career ladder.

## The first career ladder flop
**Situation:** After a nudge from HR, Fournier set out to create her first engineering career ladder.
**What happened / what was done:** She asked friends who ran other startups for examples. One friend shared his ladder: eight levels from entry-level engineer to executive, across four categories (technical skills, getting stuff done, impact, communication and leadership), with only a sentence or two per skill per level. For each skill at each level there were only one or two sentences (perhaps four points per category even after her additions), and the worst parts were the earliest levels, which were the most basic and gave early-career engineers very little guidance. She tweaked it, added some details, renamed levels, and rolled it out the same way her friend had: telling the team it existed to ensure fairness in compensation and as a growth-discussion tool, that it "wasn't a big deal," that they should not obsess over their level — and pointing them at John Allspaw's blog post "On Being a Senior Engineer" for inspiration. The ladder was a flop. Her team, drawn from diverse backgrounds (mostly small companies and startups, some big finance, a couple of big-tech veterans), had no shared cultural habits to fill in the gaps. Her friend's team was largely composed of people from the same large tech company, so much understanding never needed to be explicit. Instead of preventing level-obsession, the vague ladder caused engineers to obsess more, arguing they deserved higher levels precisely because the details were vague — a constant series of headaches.
**Lesson:** What works for one company will not always translate to another, even when the companies look similar in size and stage. A team without shared background needs much more written detail; copying a template is a starting point, not a solution.

## Rebuilding the ladder with the team
**Situation:** After the first ladder flopped, Fournier needed a better one.
**What happened / what was done:** She changed her approach: enlisted senior managers and engineers to provide feedback and details, asked people to flag what they did not understand and propose rewrites, and had subgroups own the sections they cared about — the most senior individual contributors wrote the technical and skills expectations for IC levels. She also gathered more ladder examples from friends at other companies; the best details came from bigger employers with strong technical reputations, which helped articulate the scope of work expected at very senior technical levels.
**Lesson:** Solicit participation from the team — the best processes and documents reflect the whole team rather than one leader's current bias — and borrow detail from organizations that have already articulated senior expectations well.

## Overlapping salary bands for wide levels
**Situation:** Designing salary bands for a ladder with fewer, widely spaced levels.
**What happened / what was done:** The bands are made large and overlapping — for example, a software engineer band of $50–100K and a senior software engineer band of $80–150K — so a strong software engineer may earn more than a senior software engineer.
**Lesson:** The overlap provides wiggle room to retain people performing well at their level who are not ready for the next one's responsibility, and to hire borderline candidates into the lower level with the expectation of quick promotion.

## The breakpoint level around senior engineer
**Situation:** Companies often treat some early-career levels as "up or out," where failure to advance signals insufficient maturity or independence to stay.
**What happened / what was done:** Fournier defines the breakpoint level as the lowest level at which someone can sit forever — never promoted, but not underperforming. For many companies this is somewhere around senior engineer: a person who has made it that far is a solid team member who may stay at that level indefinitely by choice.
**Lesson:** Know where your breakpoint is, consider making the ladder noticeably harder to climb past it, and expect the team to cluster at that level with fewer people above or below.

## The staff engineer maturity bar
**Situation:** Deciding whether years of experience should gate ladder levels.
**What happened / what was done:** Fournier distinguishes keystone levels by expectations of increased maturity, which correlate with years in the industry. Her example is staff engineer: the distinguishing feature is the individual maturity to think through large projects. Brilliant programming alone is not enough; the title requires a demonstrated track record of completing and supporting long-running work.
**Lesson:** Do not make years of experience a strict requirement, but use rules of thumb about maturity, especially when writing and rolling out a ladder for the first time.

## The infrastructure-biased ladder and frontend engineers
**Situation:** Fournier's own focus was infrastructure development, and the ladder she wrote reflected that.
**What happened / what was done:** Frontend-focused developers found the ladder hard to interpret, so it had to be tweaked to better describe what senior performance meant in the frontend world.
**Lesson:** A career ladder is a living document; expect to miss details and evolve it as the company grows and as blind spots surface.

## The customer photo reviews pod at Rent the Runway
**Situation:** When Fournier joined Rent the Runway, engineering was split into roughly two groups: storefront (the customer-facing website) and warehouse (software running warehouse operations); storefront soon split into frontend and backend during a rewrite from a PHP monolith to Java- and Ruby-based microservices. Customers struggled to find dresses that would fit well.
**What happened / what was done:** Toward the end of her first year, the company ran an experiment: a new feature letting shoppers see photos other customers had uploaded of themselves in the dresses, with self-reported size, height, weight, and shape (athletic, pear, curvy, and so on). To build it, they formed a cross-functional team: frontend UX engineers, backend services engineers, a product manager, designers, a data analyst, and even a customer service representative. The project was a massive success — a good feature delivered fairly quickly — and contributors all felt they understood the goals and worked better because of the structure. Before this, the company was stuck in "us versus them" patterns where each function saw itself as "us" and the rest of the organization as "them"; the collaboration unit let people see the whole group as "us." The win was so clear that the entire product engineering organization was reorganized into cross-functional pods.
**Lesson:** Putting everyone needed for a project's success into one group focuses members on the project, makes group-wide communication far more effective, and dissolves inter-functional tribalism — a clear organizational health win that justified restructuring the whole org.

## Banks as cross-functional precedent
**Situation:** Cross-functional team structure can sound like a startup fad.
**What happened / what was done:** Banks often attach technology teams to specific business areas: management structure is formed by engineers, but the roadmap and day-to-day work are jointly determined by the business unit and its engineering team, with a centralized infrastructure team supporting fundamental systems and shared frameworks. Many technology companies are structured the same way, with "business units" headed by former engineers acting as product or business managers.
**Lesson:** Cross-functional alignment with the business is a long-standing pattern across company sizes, typically paired with a centralized infrastructure function.

## First encounter with unit tests, and first hated process
**Situation:** Two formative process experiences from Fournier's earlier career.
**What happened / what was done:** The first time she worked in a codebase with unit tests expected to run before check-in, she was diligent about it and got very upset whenever someone broke the build by skipping the tests. Later, after years at an organization with no required code reviews, no ticketing, and no tracking, a central bureaucracy mandated all of those measures at once in a push for standard software development lifecycle management. It felt unnecessary, slow, and burdensome — and no one bothered to explain why the changes were happening.
**Lesson:** Process earns commitment when its purpose is understood and it matches the team's needs; imposing sweeping process without explanation breeds resentment even when individual practices have merit.

## Ask the CTO: the Scala lone wolf and zero-to-process anxiety
**Situation:** A head of engineering at a small, quickly growing startup has very little process: no code reviews, Trello used loosely, and architecture decisions made by whoever is working on the project at the time, with the leader's sign-off. Engineers have begun complaining that new people are checking bad code into the systems and want required code reviews. The leader has also discovered that someone has been building a new system in Scala even though the rest of the codebase is Ruby; that person is the only one who knows Scala, the support burden is worrying, but the project is too far along to simply kill. The leader is nervous about jumping from zero process to lots of process at once.
**What happened / what was done:** Fournier's answer: think of process as risk management. As teams and systems grow, no individual can hold the systems in their head, so processes evolve around coordinating work in order to make risks obvious. A process's complexity should act as a proxy for how hard or rare the underlying action ought to be — complicated processes belong only on rare activities or those with non-obvious risks, and "complicated" can mean requiring sign-off from busy people or meeting a high bar, not just being long. Therefore: keep code review light if it applies to all changes, since slowing minor changes hurts the whole group's productivity; and actively hunt for hidden risks (like the lone-wolf Scala system) to draw them into the open. She closes with the political saying that "a good political idea is one that works well in half-baked form" — good engineering processes have value even when followed imperfectly, and that value lies largely in socializing change and risk to the team.
**Lesson:** Add process as targeted risk management, calibrated so complexity matches the rarity and riskiness of the action, rather than swinging from no process to bureaucracy.

## Postmortem laundry lists
**Situation:** Teams running outage postmortems (learning reviews) generate long lists of possible improvements — cleaning up alerts, adding role restrictions, following up with a third-party vendor to understand its API.
**What happened / what was done:** Teams that try to do everything on the list typically end up doing none of it.
**Lesson:** Pick the one or two takeaways that are truly high-risk and likely to cause future problems, and explicitly acknowledge the items being let go for now.

## The unrelated-area veto
**Situation:** Designing who sits on an architecture review board.
**What happened / what was done:** Fournier warns against static guru panels and company-wide review: the deciding group should be scoped to the people who will be closely impacted by the decision, partly to get the senior leader out of the hot seat for every technical decision and partly so those who live with the outcome evaluate it.
**Lesson:** Nothing is more demoralizing than having someone from a completely unrelated area veto a project; keep review boards composed of the genuinely affected.
