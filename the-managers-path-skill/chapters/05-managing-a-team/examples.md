# Chapter 5: Managing a Team — Examples and Anecdotes

## Becoming a people manager (guest essay, bethanye Blount)
**Situation:** Blount was an informal team lead at a company that had resisted having traditional managers. When the company finally introduced the manager role, the whole organization met the change with trepidation, and engineering was divided among new managers with care taken over who would chafe at reporting to a former peer.
**What happened / what was done:** Blount was lucky: most of her new reports had worked with her long enough to accept her as manager, so pushback was minor. The harder part was that she now managed a few engineers far more technically senior than herself. This was not impostor syndrome — she genuinely was out of her league, and they knew it. She talked openly with the two most senior engineers about the awkwardness: everyone had a job to do, and hers was to help them succeed however she could. One senior engineer helped her continuously with feedback, and she worked hard to learn what mattered to him and what he needed. The other engineer couldn't adjust to her being his manager and transferred to another team — but months later, somewhat contrite, he returned and agreed to work with her.
**Lesson:** Being a good manager isn't about having the most technical knowledge. Supporting people matters far more to management success, and acknowledging the awkwardness of managing more-senior engineers openly can turn them into allies.

## Making releases daily
**Situation:** At Fournier's previous job, a critical part of the system was released only once a week. Releases took hours, were painful, and were regularly derailed by people cramming in last-minute changes that broke tests and slowed everyone down.
**What happened / what was done:** The team agreed this was a problem and worked together to improve the codebase and automation so releases could happen faster. Toward the end of the effort, Fournier pushed the team to go all the way to daily releases.
**Lesson:** The impact on the team was immediate. Releases are a point of resource contention; when people compete for a scarce resource, conflict and unhappiness are nearly inevitable. Making the ability to ship far less scarce immediately improved morale — and infrequent releases had been hiding pain points like poor tooling, manual testing, oversized features, and developers who didn't know how to break down work.

## Teams that ran smoothly while the world burned
**Situation:** Fournier managed teams during periods when, from her vantage point, the company around them seemed to be in chaos.
**What happened / what was done:** She watched with mixed pride as her teams continued functioning smoothly, insulated from drama that didn't concern them.
**Lesson:** Shielding works — people can and should focus on what they can impact and ignore what they can't, since workplace drama is mostly an ego-entertaining drain. But the chapter pairs this with the warning that shielding has limits: teams still need context about why goals exist, and pretending no drama exists (for example around layoffs elsewhere) backfires.

## A reluctant manager warms up to team bonding
**Situation:** As a new manager, Fournier was reluctant to organize social bonding — team lunches, Friday outings, casual chat.
**What happened / what was done:** She came to see that even most introverts want a feeling of relatedness with their team, and that small efforts (lunches, leaving early for a fun event, PG-rated humor in chat rooms, asking about people's lives) warm a group considerably, assuming there's no underlying people drama.
**Lesson:** Don't dismiss light social rituals as fluff; they cultivate the relatedness that underpins team cohesion.

## Jason, the conflict avoider
**Situation:** Jason's team is overworked. Everyone knows Charles should be helping with the big system rewrite, but he has spent months on his own pet project — which Jason has never raised with him, so Charles assumes he's doing the right thing.
**What happened / what was done:** After hearing complaints, Jason gathers the team and asks them to vote on which projects to drop. Predictably, they vote to kill Charles's pet project, blindsiding him. The team's crunch also persists because Jason won't stand up to other teams: he hates saying no to new projects but also won't ask for more people. Everyone agrees Jason is "cool," but he won't act on conflicts or make hard decisions.
**Lesson:** A seemingly democratic style does not produce an empowered team. Jason's refusal to say no or own decisions leaves no one feeling secure, the team overworked and unable to prioritize, and grudges festering — and using a group vote to deliver bad news he should have delivered himself was cruel to Charles.

## Lydia, the conflict tamer
**Situation:** Lydia's team is also crunched, and she has her own Charles: she had promised him time for a side project, but priorities have changed and his work must change with them.
**What happened / what was done:** In their 1-1, Lydia explains the current workload and tells Charles directly that the team needs him on the system rewrite. Charles is unhappy and Lydia doesn't enjoy the conversation, but she knows she's responsible for keeping the team on the most important work. She pushes for more headcount while making sure the team understands why she took on the big project, works with them to prioritize, and guides technology disagreements through a structure for presenting options and soliciting feedback.
**Lesson:** Her team describes her as tough but fair; disagreements happen but the team works through challenges and collaborates well. Owning hard decisions, delivering bad news directly, and giving structure for disagreement beats favoring artificial harmony.

## The manager who bullied other teams
**Situation:** A friend of Fournier's was withholding critical feedback from his own team while behaving aggressively toward other teams after cross-team incidents.
**What happened / what was done:** In his own words: he wasn't telling his people the 10% of things they needed to improve because he feared they'd miss the message about the 90% that was good — so he took that pent-up desire for accountability out on other teams. He realized he wanted everyone to be fully accountable and needed to express that in a healthy way both internally and externally.
**Lesson:** Conflict-avoidant managers often displace suppressed frustration with their own team onto outsiders. Bullying other teams can be a symptom of feedback you're afraid to give at home.

## Nice versus kind: the promotion conversation
**Situation:** A worked contrast the chapter uses to define managerial kindness. An employee believes she is ready for promotion, but she isn't.
**What happened / what was done:** The book grounds "nice" in concrete behaviors first: nice is saying "please" and "thank you," holding doors for people struggling with bags or strollers, and answering "I'm fine" when asked how you are instead of "I'm in a really crappy mood and I wish you would leave me alone." Nice is a good thing in casual conversation — but managerial relationships go deeper than polite society, so the manager's goal should be to be kind, not nice. The kind manager tells her plainly that she isn't ready, and backs it up with the specific work she needs to do to get there. The "nice" manager strings her along with "maybe you could get promoted" and then watches her fail. Similarly, it is kind — awkward and uncomfortable, but kind — to tell someone that his behavior in meetings is disrupting the group.
**Lesson:** Nice is the language of polite society; kind is the deeper obligation of a manager. Difficult conversations delivered honestly are kindness; comfortable evasions are not.

## The pizza test for happy teams
**Situation:** Fournier was once given a test for a happy engineering team: buy them pizza in the evening — will they stick around and socialize, or race out the door?
**What happened / what was done:** She quibbles with the test: employees with hard obligations that pull them out of the office at a set time are no less engaged than those free to linger.
**Lesson:** The literal test is flawed, but the underlying point stands: gelled teams share camaraderie — joking, coffee, lunches — and don't treat the team as something to escape. The real target is psychological safety, the willingness to take risks and make mistakes in front of one another.

## The brilliant jerk's trajectory
**Situation:** A composite scenario the chapter walks through: a team member produces individually outsized results but bullies with her intellect, cuts down dissent harshly, and radiates contempt for anything she deems stupid — creating fear and dislike around her.
**What happened / what was done:** Companies claim not to tolerate such people, but managers run rings around the idea to justify keeping them ("how much jerk is too much?"). Feedback produces brief improvement, then worse behavior. The chapter's prescription: don't hire one; if you have one, expect a tooth-and-nail fight over all feedback, and recognize that no evidence changes a person who doesn't want to change. The concrete in-the-moment tactic: publicly and neutrally refuse to tolerate visible bad behavior — "Please do not speak to people that way; it is disrespectful" — keeping emotion tightly controlled so the feedback can't be dismissed as emotional or as picking on her. Reserve public correction for behavior harming the whole group; if she's only undermining you personally, handle it privately.
**Lesson:** The brilliant jerk has probably been rewarded for her brilliance for a very long time and clings to it like a life raft. Protect the team first, individuals second, yourself last — and take comfort that while you may not have the guts to fire her, you won't be foolish enough to promote her, so brilliant jerks often remove themselves.

## The noncommunicator's secret project
**Situation:** A team member hides information from his manager, teammates, and product manager — preferring to work in secret and unveil a perfect finished project. He reverts others' commits or takes their tickets rather than talking, and dodges code review and design review on big projects.
**What happened / what was done:** The prescribed response: nip the hiding habit immediately, making clear if necessary that he isn't meeting expectations, then dig for the root cause — fear of being found lacking, fear of being assigned boring work, or a belief that he deserves more responsibility and disrespects his manager. Also examine the team: is the culture harsh, lacking psychological safety? Is the team excluding him over a different background or skill set?
**Lesson:** If the team is rejecting the individual, choose deliberately between correcting the team and moving the person. Sometimes moving him is the kindest option; sometimes the right fix is changing the team culture that excludes new people. Left alone, his fear sets an example that spreads.

## The employee who doesn't respect you
**Situation:** A team member simply doesn't respect her manager or her teammates.
**What happened / what was done:** The chapter's script: ask her if she wants to be on your team. If she says yes, lay out your expectations clearly and calmly. If she says no, start the process of moving her to another team or out of the company.
**Lesson:** That's the entire playbook. Keeping someone who disrespects you erodes team cohesion as everyone wonders whether she's right. Pull the Band-Aid off quickly; handling it yourself rather than escalating shows great character.

## Ask the CTO: managing a former peer
**Situation:** A reader is promoted to run a team over a senior-engineer peer who also wanted the manager job.
**What happened / what was done:** The advice: acknowledge the awkwardness directly and be a little vulnerable — admit you won't be perfect and that you need his honest help. Avoid using managerial power to override technical decisions; resist micromanaging former peers, who are sensitive to feeling you were "rewarded." Hand over some of your old technical work openly to former peers and give new challenges to juniors, shrinking your own coding to small features and bug fixes.
**Lesson:** Show the team your role takes nothing from them — it adds neglected responsibilities and shifts your old work to them. If your former peers all quit because they can't stand working for you, you've failed; pick your battles and handle the transition with maturity.

## Ask the CTO: joining a small team as an outside manager
**Situation:** A newly hired manager of five engineers — experienced at managing, but brand new to the company, the technology, and the team — asks how to spend the first weeks.
**What happened / what was done:** The advice: learn the software without annoying the team. Get a walkthrough of systems, architecture, and the test/release process; complete the normal developer onboarding; get comfortable in the codebases and watch code reviews. Work on at least a couple of features in the first 60 days: build a specced-out feature, pair both ways with an engineer, get code reviewed by a team member, perform a release, and do a couple of days of support rotation if the team owns support.
**Lesson:** Management onboarding goes slower this way, and that slowdown is worth it. Knowing the code, processes, and tools yields both the understanding needed to manage the team and the technical credibility needed for the team to see you as a capable leader.

## The doubling rule of estimation
**Situation:** A widely repeated rule of software estimation: whenever asked for an estimate, take your guess and double it.
**What happened / what was done:** Fournier endorses the rule for off-the-cuff guesses, but for projects expected to take more than a couple of weeks, she doubles the estimate and explicitly reserves planning time before committing.
**Lesson:** Long tasks can take far more than double the initial guess; careful planning before committing the team to a big unknown project is worth the time.

## Example future-looking technical bets
**Situation:** Illustrations of thinking two steps ahead from a product and technology perspective.
**What happened / what was done:** Examples given: rewriting a checkout system so it can plug in new payment types like Apple Pay, or moving to a JavaScript framework supporting streaming data over WebSockets to enable a more interactive experience.
**Lesson:** Many technical projects are justified by their power to enable future features more easily; knowing where the product roadmap is going is what lets you pick these bets.
