# Chapter 2: Mentoring — Core Learnings

## What this chapter is about

Mentoring is usually an engineer's first, unofficial act of people management: through luck of the draw, you find yourself responsible for an intern, a new hire, or a junior teammate. The chapter treats mentoring as a low-risk training ground for the basic skills of management — listening, communicating, and adjusting to another person — and also covers the mentee's side, the "alpha geek" anti-pattern, and how managers should set up mentoring relationships on their teams.

## Core principles

**Mentoring is a safe trial run at management.** You get to feel responsible for another person without the full stakes of being their manager. You're unlikely to be fired for being a bad mentor (unless you behave inappropriately — don't hit on your mentee). In a healthy organization, onboarding mentorship benefits both parties: the mentor experiences responsibility for another person, and the mentee gets an overseer focused on her alone, with no other reports clamoring for the mentor's attention. The skills you practice — listening, communicating what needs to happen, calibrating to responses — are exactly the skills managing requires, whether or not you ever take the title.

**Weak mentoring squanders great talent.** The worst realistic outcomes of mentoring are (a) the mentee drains your time, or (b) you do such a poor job that someone the company wanted to hire or keep walks away. The second is far more likely than the first. Weak mentors ignore their charges, waste their time on trivial projects, or intimidate and belittle them out of ever wanting to join.

**Listening is the first and most basic skill of managing people.** It is a precursor to empathy, a core skill of any quality manager — needed even by principal engineers with no reports. People are bad at saying precisely what they mean, so listening goes beyond hearing words: read body language, eye contact, sighs and frowns; these signal whether the person feels understood. Fournier's framing for why this is hard: we have yet to achieve Borg hive mind or Vulcan mind meld, so we're constantly pushing complex ideas through the eye of the needle of language — and language is not something that most engineers have mastered in nuance and interpretation. That compression loss is why listening has to go beyond the literal words.

**Assigned mentoring needs explicit purpose and structure (the best mentoring evolves naturally out of shared work).** Formal cross-team matching programs are often an ambiguous obligation for both mentor and mentee and yield little without guidance and structure; intern and new-hire mentoring have a built-in purpose — get the person up to speed and productive — so keep that purpose explicit. In any created relationship, define what it is for, what each party expects, and the time commitment.

**Your network is your career.** Mentoring builds your network of trusted people, and your career ultimately succeeds or fails on the strength of that network. Even with zero interest in management, you can't build a career at a multi-team company without one. Treat network building as a worthwhile, conscious investment — including for introverts.

**A mentee's fresh eyes are a gift.** Newcomers expose unspoken rules, jargon, and assumptions you no longer notice. Use their questions to find things you thought you understood but cannot explain clearly.

## Mentoring an intern

The intern is a temporary employee, usually a student. If they're more than a year from graduating, expect them to know very little and to intern somewhere else next year unless the experience is exceptional. Even if you don't end up wanting to hire them, you want them to leave loving the company — they'll tell their classmates, which affects your ability to hire from that school.

**Have a project ready.** Ideally the mentor isn't the one stuck inventing the project idea — coming up with one is a daunting task in itself. But have one ready regardless: without a project, the intern will be lost and bored all summer; figuring out what to do in a workplace is hard even for experienced hires. You don't need the whole summer planned — at minimum, have something to get them started for the first couple of weeks. If you're drawing a blank, carve off small features of your own current project that would take you a few days.

**Onboard like a new hire, but closer.** The first few days: sit with them as much as possible, get the IDE installed and code checked out, and touch base several times a day so they aren't drowning in new information.

**Practice project management first.** Once you have a project, apply your budding project-management knowledge: if it isn't broken into milestones, spend a little time in the intern's first few days breaking it down. Walk the breakdown through with the intern, check it makes sense to them, and listen to and answer their questions.

**Then practice three management skills:**

- **Listen carefully.** Watch your own behavior while the mentee talks — if you're composing your reply or thinking about your own work, you're not listening. Say complex things several times in different ways. If a question confuses you, repeat it back differently and let them correct you. Use whiteboards and diagrams. Remember the power imbalance: the intern is nervous, eager to please, and afraid to look stupid, so they may not ask questions even when lost. Actively pull questions out of them — the cost of answering questions is far smaller than the cost of an intern heading in the wrong direction.
- **Clearly communicate.** If the intern asks for help constantly without trying first, state your expectations: do research before asking. Have them explain a piece of code or a process back to you, pointing to the docs that should explain it. If they can't manage even with pointers, you're learning something about their potential. As a fallback, hand them the first milestone and tell them to work alone on it for a day or two — this is the payoff of breaking the project down up front: you've already done some of the harder thinking. They may surprise you by finishing much faster than anticipated; generally, though, expect to provide nudging and clarity along the way.
- **Calibrate your response.** Break the project into milestones and walk the breakdown through with them. Many things can happen: the intern may far outstrip your expectations, struggle with simple tasks, produce work quickly but at poor quality, or work very slowly toward something overly perfect. In the first few weeks, learn the right check-in frequency — it may be daily, it may be weekly. Check in at least weekly regardless, and treat any spare time as an extra interview-and-sell cycle for the company.

**Use the summer as a self-diagnostic.** Hopefully it ends on good terms: the intern completed a project that has some value and leaves thinking happy thoughts about the company, and you got practice listening, communicating, and adjusting — and you have gained some insight as to whether you want to do this management thing now, soon, or ever.

### Ask the CTO: Mentoring a summer intern (distilled)

Preparation shouldn't take long but is crucial:

1. **Prepare for arrival.** Know the start date; make sure desk, computer, and system/software access exist on day one. Even big companies overlook this for interns.
2. **Have a project.** Specific but not urgent, relevant to the team, and completable by an entry-level engineer in about half the internship (a 10-week intern gets roughly 5 weeks of new-hire-sized work). That leaves room for program activities, and an early finish means they know the codebase well enough for more work. They're still in school — expect slow progress and be pleasantly surprised by overshoot.
3. **Plan an end-of-program presentation.** It gives exposure beyond the mentors, sets the expectation that the project gets finished, and feeds the decision to make a full-time offer — or to bring the intern back for another summer if she isn't graduating yet. Coach them on it; match the team's demo-day format. Interns who feel the company appreciated their work are the ones most likely to come back after graduating.

### Ask the CTO: Hiring interns (distilled)

An internship program is a hiring pipeline, not a way to get cheap summer labor.

- **Don't hire interns who won't graduate the year after the internship.** Students two or more years out will likely explore other companies before committing; with only a handful of slots, every intern should be a plausible future full-timer.
- **Hiring interns is relatively easy compared to hiring full-time graduates** because demand is lower. Use that slack deliberately — push for candidates from underrepresented groups. Diversity in the intern class becomes diversity in new-grad hiring, which becomes diversity in the organization.

## Mentoring a new hire

A new-hire mentor's job: onboarding, helping the person adjust to life in the company, and building both your networks of contacts. It's often easier than mentoring an intern, but the relationship lasts much longer. The mentor doesn't have to be senior: sometimes the mentor is another junior person, perhaps only a year or two into the organization, who still clearly remembers her own onboarding and relates closely to the newcomer; other times it's a senior engineer who can offer technical mentoring on top of process help.

- **Surface the unspoken rules.** Every company has spoken rules (the HR vacation policy) and unspoken ones (in ecommerce, you don't take vacation the week after Thanksgiving; how long you're expected to struggle alone before asking for help). Process, culture, and jargon that feel second-nature to you are foreign to a newcomer. Clarifying them helps the new hire and often improves your own understanding.
- **Use onboarding documents actively.** Effective teams maintain step-by-step guides for dev environment setup, tracking systems, and tools. Have the new hire work through them and update them with every surprise she hits. This signals commitment and gives her the power and obligation to learn and share.
- **Introduce them around.** Companies run on informal human networks that move knowledge fast; people who stay at one company a long time, particularly at large companies, often find their opportunities through these informal networks. Bringing the new hire into your networks speeds her up and gives you an entree into whatever networks she builds later — she may someday be on a team you want to join, or someone you want to hire onto your own team.

## Technical or career mentoring (mentor and mentee hygiene)

Fournier spends only a few words here because this type of mentoring is usually not directly related to the path of management — but most careers include some technical or career mentoring, on one side or the other. The best mentoring relationships evolve naturally in the context of real shared work — a senior engineer making a junior teammate more productive benefits both: the senior gets better code with fewer revisions; the junior gets hands-on instruction from someone who deeply knows the context. This is often an informal, expected part of a senior engineer's job. Formal cross-team matching programs can sometimes enhance networks, but they are often an ambiguous obligation for both sides; if you're in one, be specific about expectations and goals.

**When you are a mentor:**

- Tell the mentee what you expect (for example, questions sent in advance of meetings).
- Be explicit about your time commitment.
- Be honest when they ask questions — professional distance lets you give candid advice they can't get from their manager or coworkers.
- It's OK to say no. Don't mentor unless you think it will be rewarding for both of you, and you don't owe a reason for declining. Saying no to your own manager is trickier — give concrete reasons (current workload, planned vacation, other commitments).
- Whatever you do, don't say yes and then fail to actually do the work.

**When you are a mentee:**

- Decide what you want from the relationship and come to sessions prepared — especially if the mentor is outside your company and volunteering their time.
- If you can't find time to prepare, ask whether you need a mentor at all. Sometimes we take mentors because someone thinks we should have one.
- You may actually need a friend, a therapist, or a coach instead. Unpaid mentor time is easy to undervalue; consider paying a professional for what you actually need.

## Good manager, bad manager: the alpha geek

The alpha geek is driven to be the best engineer, to always have the right answer, and to solve every hard problem personally. She values intelligence and technical skill above all else and believes those traits should decide who makes decisions. She can't handle dissent, is threatened by anyone who might upstage her, believes herself to be the best and responds only to messages that support that view, and — while trying to create a culture of excellence — creates a culture of fear. Usually an excellent, effective engineer, she goes into management either because she was pushed into it or because she believes the smartest person on the team should be the manager.

- **At best:** inspirational to younger developers, with deep system knowledge and a lot to teach, and capable of designing great systems. Many engineers respect the intelligence enough to tolerate the downsides.
- **At worst:** claims credit for every good idea (but none of the bad ones), gleefully exposes others' ignorance, is rigid and closed to ideas she didn't originate, gets defensive when her past technical decisions are criticized, hates taking direction from anyone she doesn't respect intellectually, and demeans nontechnical colleagues. She belittles mistakes, redoes teammates' work without warning, and may take credit for the team's output.

**The habit often first shows up in mentoring.** If people don't come to you for help despite your obviously strong technical skills, ask whether you're showing alpha-geek signs: Do you pride yourself on "not pulling punches"? Do you hunt for gotchas and mistakes? Are you reluctant to admit someone else had a good idea or wrote good code? Do you believe correctness justifies fighting hard for whatever you believe is correct?

**Mentoring can break the habit** — if you treat the mentee as someone to teach and guide in the way that works best for her, you start to see where your aggressive style blocks learning, and you practice nurturing, coaching, and phrasing things so that others will listen, instead of just shouting them down. If you're unwilling to change your style to help a mentee succeed, don't volunteer to mentor.

**Alpha geeks and management:** they make terrible managers unless they let go of the identity of smartest, most technical person in the room. Highly technical hands-on managers can work for small teams of senior engineers, but alpha geeks are usually better kept out of management and pointed at technical strategy and system design — for example, the CTO of a technology-focused startup paired with an execution-focused VP of Engineering. If you can promote people, be very careful about giving alpha geeks management roles, and watch their impact closely: the culture undermines anyone who can't fight back, and an alpha geek who believes her value comes from knowing more than others may hoard information, making the whole team less effective.

## Tips for the manager of a mentor

What you measure, you improve — apply goal-setting to mentor assignment like everything else. Figure out what the relationship is supposed to achieve, then pick the person who can achieve it.

- **Know why the relationship exists.** New-hire and intern mentoring has a clear purpose: get the person up to speed and productive. Cross-team career programs with no guidance beyond "you've been matched" usually yield little; a disengaged or overcommitted mentor disappoints the mentee, and a mentee who doesn't know what to ask for makes it forced socializing. If your company runs such programs, push for structure before pushing people in.
- **Treat mentoring as a real additional responsibility.** A good mentor's own productivity may slow down some during the mentoring period — so you may not want to assign mentoring to an engineer on a time-sensitive project. Hand it out like any other meaningful responsibility, to someone you believe can succeed and who wants to distinguish herself beyond coding ability.

Three common pitfalls:

- **Treating mentoring as low-status "emotional labor."** Emotional labor is a way to think about traditionally feminine "soft skills" — skills that address the emotional needs of people and teams. Because the outcome is hard to measure quantitatively, it's often dismissed as less important than writing software and expected to be provided free. You don't need to pay extra for mentoring, but recognize the work, treat it as a first-class responsibility relative to the mentor's other duties, and budget time for it. The company already invested heavily (hiring dollars, program overhead); following through yields better employee networks, faster onboarding, and higher intern conversion.
- **Assuming like must mentor like.** Don't default to women mentoring women, people of color mentoring people of color, and so on. Such pairings have their place, but Fournier — as a woman in tech — is personally tired of the only mentoring on offer being organized around lines of diversity. Unless the program's explicit purpose is diversity-driven, give each person the best mentor for their situation. The one place like-matching makes sense is similar job roles: when the mentoring includes job-skills training, the best mentor is someone further along in mastering those exact skills.
- **Failing to use mentoring to observe and grow future leaders.** Leadership requires human interaction. Encouraging a brilliant introverted developer to mentor one-on-one builds her external perspective and network even if she never wants to manage; an impatient young engineer may learn humility from helping an intern succeed (under your supervision).

## Key takeaways for the mentor

- **Be curious and open-minded.** Years of "best practices" and scars from past mistakes build up unconsciously, cloud thinking, and reduce creativity; a closed mind loses the most valuable skill for a long technical career, since technology never stops changing. A mentee's questions reveal what's not obvious about your organization, surface things you can't actually explain, and expose hidden patterns — creativity is as much about seeing patterns others miss as seeing new things, and you can't see patterns when your only data points are your own experience.
- **Listen and speak their language.** Mentoring forces you to hone communication, and especially listening: if you can't hear the question, you can't give a good answer. Senior engineers develop the bad habit of lecturing and debating anyone who disagrees or doesn't understand. Working with a newcomer requires communicating so that *this person* understands, even if it takes several attempts. Software is a team sport; teams that can't communicate get nothing done.
- **Make connections.** The person you mentor could introduce you to your next job or come work for you someday. Don't abuse the relationship from either seat — your career is long and the tech world is small, so treat the other person well.

## Pitfalls and anti-patterns (summary)

- Leaving an intern or new hire alone to "figure it out" — it discourages them and drives talent away.
- Assuming the mentee will ask when confused; the power imbalance keeps questions bottled up.
- Answering every question without ever teaching self-sufficiency.
- Saying yes to mentoring out of obligation and then not doing the work.
- Mentees taking up a volunteer mentor's time without preparing, or using a mentor when they really need a friend, therapist, or paid coach.
- Alpha-geek mentoring: punishing questions, hunting for gotchas, shouting people down instead of teaching.
- Managers treating mentoring as free, low-status emotional labor; assigning it to people with no slack; matching mentors by demographics instead of fit; running structureless matching programs.
- Treating an internship program as a source of cheap labor rather than a talent pipeline.

## Assessing your own experience

- Does your company have an internship program? If so, can you volunteer to mentor an intern?
- How does your company handle onboarding? Are mentors assigned to new hires? If not, can you propose trying it and volunteer to mentor someone?
- Have you ever had a great mentor? What made that person great — how did they help you learn, and what did they teach you?
- Have you ever had a mentoring relationship that didn't work out? Why did it fail, and what lessons can you apply to avoid similar failures?
