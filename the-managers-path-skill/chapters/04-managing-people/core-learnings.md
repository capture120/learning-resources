# Chapter 4: Managing People — Core Learnings

## What this chapter is about

This chapter covers the basic mechanics of managing individual humans for the first time: starting a new reporting relationship, running one-on-ones, delegating without micromanaging, building a culture of continuous feedback, writing performance reviews, cultivating careers and promotions, and handling underperformers up to and including firing. The framing mindset comes from Marc Hedlund's epigraph: "new manager" is an entry-level job with no seniority on any front, and treating it that way is the best way to start leading. Part of your focus throughout this adjustment period is figuring out your own management style. Your team is only as healthy as its individuals, and as the individual manager you have a huge impact on each person.

## The main tasks of managing people

- Taking on a new report.
- Holding regular 1-1s.
- Giving feedback on career growth, progression toward goals, areas for improvement, and praise as warranted.
- Working with reports to identify areas for learning, and helping them grow through project work, external learning, or additional mentoring.

## Starting a new reporting relationship off right

### Build trust and rapport with direct questions

Ask new reports questions that reveal how to manage them well:

- How do you like to be praised, in public or in private? (Some people genuinely hate public praise.)
- What's your preferred method for serious feedback — in writing so you can digest it, or informal and verbal?
- Why did you decide to work here? What are you excited about?
- How will I know when you're in a bad mood or annoyed? Are there things that always put you in a bad mood I should know about? (Religious fasting, on-call stress, review season.)
- Are there any manager behaviors you know you hate? (Fournier's own answers: skipping or rescheduling 1-1s, neglecting to give feedback, avoiding difficult conversations.)
- Do you have clear career goals I should know about so I can help?
- Any surprises since joining, good or bad? (Missing stock options, an unpaid relocation bonus, "why are we using SVN and not Git?" — or, on the good side, "I didn't expect to be so productive already!")

For more question ideas, Fournier points to Lara Hogan's blog post on the topic.

### Create a 30/60/90-day plan

Help new reports build a plan with basic goals: getting up to speed on the code, committing a bug fix, performing a release. The plan is especially valuable for new hires and for people transferring in from other areas of the company. The more senior the hire, the more they should participate in creating the plan. Clear goals show whether the person is learning the right things, and they require work from you and the team, because almost nothing is self-evident or well documented to a newcomer.

The plan is also your mishire detector. A realistic set of expected goals for the first 90 days lets you catch a mishire quickly and makes the needed correction clear to both of you. Base the milestones on prior hires, the current state of your technology and project, and the incoming person's level.

### Have new hires update the onboarding documentation

For early- to mid-career hires, make contributing to the team's onboarding docs part of onboarding. Every new hire edits the docs to reflect changed processes or tools and points they found confusing. You don't have to walk the new hire through this personally — a peer, mentor, or tech lead can — but you may need to put the process in place and enforce it for everyone who joins.

### Communicate your style and expectations

The new hire needs to understand your expectations and style as much as you need to understand theirs. Be specific: how often you'll meet, how you'll share information, when and how often you'll review their work. If you expect a weekly progress email, say so. Tell them how long to struggle alone before asking for help — an hour on some teams, a week on others.

### Get feedback from your new hire

The first 90 days are a rare window of fresh eyes; new people see things the established team can't. But they also lack context, so take observations with a grain of salt, and don't encourage criticism of established processes in a way that makes the existing team feel attacked.

## Communicating with your team: 1-1s

Marc Hedlund: "Regular 1-1s are like oil changes; if you skip them, plan to get stranded on the side of the highway at the worst possible time."

### Have regular 1-1s

Every person and team is different, and a few very experienced leaders skip 1-1s successfully. Unless you're a CTO with years and years of management experience, start by assuming you need regularly scheduled 1-1s.

### Scheduling

- Default to weekly; adjust frequency only if both of you agree it's more than you need. Weekly keeps meetings short and focused and tolerates the occasional missed week. At lower frequency, every missed 1-1 must be rescheduled, which drags on both of you.
- Pick times when both of you are likely to be in the office. Avoid Mondays and Fridays (long weekends). Mornings before the day gets busy work well, but only for reports who also come in early and don't have morning standups.
- Respect the "maker schedule": don't put 1-1s in the middle of a report's productive workflow hours.

### Adjusting 1-1s — factors to weigh

- **How often you interact offhand.** Frequent informal contact may reduce the need for a weekly set-aside.
- **Coaching need.** A junior person new to the team may want more time; a senior person in a groove may want less — unless they're pushing through a difficult new project and want dedicated help with details.
- **How well they push information up.** People who don't volunteer information need more face time.
- **Relationship quality — careful here.** Many people need regular 1-1 time even in good relationships. Things seeming smooth to you doesn't mean your report agrees. Don't make the fatal error of spending all your time on problem employees and ignoring your stars.
- **Stability of the team or company.** In times of rapid change or uncertainty, keep 1-1s regular, answer questions, stabilize the team, and slow the rumor mill.

## Different 1-1 styles

The most effective style depends as much on the report as on the manager.

### The to-do list meeting

One or both parties bring a list of objectives, covered in order of importance. Professional and efficient, if sometimes cold; nothing gets forgotten. The structure also forces reports to think beforehand about the meeting and what they might want to discuss. Downside: the list is often artificial, made of things that could have been handled by chat or email. If you adopt this style, make sure the lists carry nuance that deserves synchronous verbal discussion. A shared running document of topics that both parties can add to works well — each side sees what's on the other's mind before the meeting.

### The catch-up

Fournier's own preferred style (though she'll adopt to-do-list requirements if her reports want them). Fluid and report-driven: listen first, give the report space to raise whatever matters, treat the 1-1 as part creative discussion, part planning meeting. The downfall is when the rambling 1-1 becomes a complaining session or therapy. Empathetic leaders can slide into unhealthy closeness; if you spend the energy hearing complaints and commiserating, you may be making the problem worse. Workplace problems need to be either dealt with or set aside by mutual agreement. There is very little value in repeatedly dwelling on drama.

### The feedback meeting

Devote some 1-1s to informal feedback and coaching at a regular interval — especially for early-career employees. Quarterly is frequent enough to give the topic attention without making every conversation about career development. Use the time to review progress against goals, formal or personal.

- For an employee with performance issues, hold feedback meetings more frequently.
- If you're thinking of firing someone, document the feedback meetings: the issues discussed and the expectations set, in writing, sent to the person (usually email).
- Don't wait for the 1-1 when something needs immediate corrective feedback (insulting a colleague, missing a critical meeting, inappropriate language). Address it as soon as possible after; the longer you wait, the harder it gets and the less effective the feedback. The same goes for praise — give it freely in the moment, don't save it up.

### The progress report

When you manage managers, much of your 1-1 time goes to project details you can't dig into yourself. When you manage only a handful of individuals, use a 1-1 for progress reporting only for a side project you aren't personally overseeing. Status updates from people you already work with closely are a waste — you just hear the delta since the last standup. If your 1-1s become status meetings, ask reports to prepare answers to non-status questions, or to bring questions about the team and company. If someone genuinely has nothing but progress to discuss, take it as a sign to meet less frequently.

### Getting to know you

Whatever the style, leave room for the human being. Don't pry, but show you care: let them talk about family, friends, hobbies, pets. Learn their career so far and long-term goals — not just the next skill or promotion. Show you're invested in them now and in the future.

### Mix it up

Walking meetings, coffee, or lunch add variety, but you won't be taking notes, so don't use them for critical conversations. Do 1-1s in private when possible so sensitive topics are safe. Keep notes in a shared document per report, with you as note taker: context, takeaways, to-dos, and a record of what feedback was given and when. It becomes an essential historical record for writing reviews. If a laptop distracts you mid-meeting, add notes at the end.

## Good manager, bad manager: micromanager vs. delegator

Micromanagement creeps up on you. A high-stakes project seems at risk, so you step in. You delegate, dislike the team's technical choices, and order a rewrite. You make everyone come to you before deciding anything because "they can't be trusted" or you always end up paying for mistakes.

Key distinctions:

- **Trust and control are the core issues.** Micromanagement usually means you don't trust the task will be done right, or you want the outcome to meet your exact standards. This hits talented engineers turned managers hard: your value shifted from what you're great at (writing code) to what you don't yet do well (managing people), so you treat reports as mini-mes. When a deadline slips — and it will — you read it as a failure to control precisely enough and ratchet up the attention, which seems to confirm that micromanaging was warranted. It's a self-reinforcing loop.
- **Autonomy is an essential element of motivation.** Autonomy means having control over some part of your work. Strip creative, talented people of it and they lose motivation fast — which is why micromanagers find it so difficult to retain great teams. Nothing is worse than being unable to make a single decision alone, or having everything double- and triple-checked.
- **Sometimes micromanagement is right.** Junior engineers often thrive under detailed oversight; some projects go off the rails; occasionally you must override a decision with big negative repercussions. The problem is micromanagement as your habit and default, which undermines exactly the people you need to grow and reward.
- **Delegation is not abdication.** When you delegate responsibility, you're still expected to stay involved as much as necessary to help the project succeed — clarify goals and responsibilities, then provide support and guidance.

## Practical advice for delegating effectively

Being a good leader means being good at delegating.

### Use the team's goals to decide which details to dig into

When you feel the urge to micromanage, ask the team how they're measuring their success and ask them to make it visible on an ongoing basis. Then sit on your hands for a week or two. If they have nothing to share, that's a sign you may need a course correction, which probably means digging into more details. Fournier's rule: if the team is making progress on its goals, the systems are stable, and the product manager is happy, she rarely digs in beyond a cursory overview. That rule requires goals with a plan, and a product manager for a second perspective. If the team has no clear plan, use the details you'd want to monitor to help them create one: what are you holding them accountable for this month, this quarter, this year? If you can't answer, the first step is helping the team create those goals.

### Gather information from systems before going to people

Version control, the ticketing system, alerts, metrics, and on-call history will tell you the status of work and the stability of systems without the team doing anything. The worst micromanagers constantly demand information they could easily get themselves. It's fine to ask for status summaries that surface the most important signals from these sources, but use a light touch — a team that spends half its time gathering information for you will be neither productive nor happy. And remember: systems data is one piece of context, meaningless without the goals.

### Adjust your focus to the project stage

If you directly manage one or two teams, you should know project status through regular team processes like standups. Beyond that: in early and design stages, be more involved to help shape good project goals and system design. Near a delivery date, progress details matter more because there are more decisions and the specifics are actionable. During normal workflow, it's usually enough to know what's moving and what's taking longer than expected — useful for reshuffling work or helping a struggling team member.

### Establish standards for code and systems

If you're a deeply technical manager who finds letting go hard, build structure instead of intervening. Develop basic standards as a team: how much unit testing each change needs (generally, some tests always required), and when a technical decision needs review by a larger group (such as adding a new language or framework to the stack). Standards help people communicate in code and design reviews, depersonalize technical feedback, and tell everyone which details matter when building.

### Treat open sharing of information — good or bad — neutrally to positively

When someone struggles silently, the right response is to require more proactive sharing, not punishment. You might ask for daily updates briefly, but only briefly: heavy structure punishes you with overhead and undermines their accountability for their own work. The goal is to teach what to communicate, when, and how. Caution: if you treat a struggling engineer or project as a massive failure of the person or their manager, people will feel blamed and criticized, and next time they'll hide information until it's too late. Intentionally hiding important information is a failure; getting stuck or making a mistake is usually just an opportunity to learn.

### The long-run cost of not letting go

If you never learn to let go of details, delegate, and trust your team, you suffer personally: your hours grow as your responsibilities do, even if the team doesn't quit first. Try a forcing exercise: cap yourself at 45 hours this week. Would you really spend five of them nitpicking a junior developer's code, or combing a healthy project for minor errors? Or would you direct attention to bigger problems and the future? Your time is too valuable to waste, and your team deserves a manager willing to trust them.

## Creating a culture of continuous feedback

A good review experience starts long before reviews are written — with continuous feedback: a commitment to regularly sharing both positive and corrective feedback as things happen, instead of saving it for the review cycle. Tools can help, but culture matters most. For a new manager, the habit trains you to pay attention to individuals, which makes it easier to recognize and foster talent, and gives you practice with small, occasionally tricky performance conversations before the stakes get high. Few people are comfortable giving one-on-one praise or correction; practicing continuous feedback is how you get over that feeling of awkwardness.

Steps to be great at it:

1. **Know your people.** Understand each person's goals, strengths, weaknesses, current level, and what they'd need for the next level. Read previous reviews if available, but also sit down with everyone and ask for their own perspective. This baseline frames your feedback.
2. **Observe your people.** You can't give feedback without paying attention — and the biggest benefit of attempting continuous feedback is that it forces you to pay attention. Start the habit early in your management career, while you still manage only a few people, to build up the observational muscles. Practice spotting talents and achievements first; good managers identify strengths and help people draw more from them. If you spend most of your time correcting weaknesses, your style becomes continuous criticism. Set a concrete goal: find at least one thing per week to recognize about someone on your team — better, something weekly for everyone who reports to you. It doesn't have to be public.
3. **Provide lightweight, regular feedback.** Start with positive feedback; it's easier and more fun to give, many people respond better to praise than correction, and kudos guide behavior by emphasizing what was done well. Positive feedback also earns you a hearing: reports who believe you see their good work are more open to hearing where they can improve. Give critical feedback quickly after an obvious misstep, and raise things that don't seem to be going well as you start noticing them, rather than waiting for the review cycle.
4. **Bonus: provide coaching.** Pair feedback with coaching: ask what they might have done differently; when things go well, praise and suggest how it could be even better. This builds a partnership for growth that goes beyond "good job." It's a bonus because it isn't always a core need and you won't always have the qualifications or capacity. Coaching matters most for early-career team members and those with the potential or desire to advance. Many people are content doing a job they know well; as long as they're doing it well enough, coaching them is not a good use of your time. Save coaching for the receptive.

## Performance reviews

Continuous feedback is not a replacement for a formal, 360-based review process. The 360 model collects feedback from a person's manager, teammates, anyone who reports to them, and coworkers they regularly interact with, plus a self-review. (An engineer with no reports might solicit reviews from two teammates, the new hire she mentored, and the product manager she works with.)

Why it's worth the time: you synthesize a large body of information about one person; you get a high-level view of what others think of your reports; self-reviews reveal how people see their own strengths, weaknesses, and accomplishments; and writing the summary forces you to look at the big picture over a long period — surfacing patterns and trends that day-to-day continuous feedback misses.

Why reviews go wrong: people aren't given time to prioritize them and find them hard to write; recency bias overweights the last couple of months; and unconscious biases make us criticize some people for behaviors we don't even notice in others. All true — and the process is still incredibly valuable. You, the manager, make it more or less valuable by how you approach it.

### Writing and delivering a review

- **Give yourself enough time, and start early.** You can't do this well in an hour. Block solid, uninterrupted time; work from home if needed. Read the collected feedback, take notes, and process before writing the summary; revisit the draft at least once before submitting. Even at companies with open (non-anonymized) peer feedback, read it and use it — the manager's review is often considered the most important summary of all the feedback.
- **Account for the whole year, not just the last couple of months.** Easiest if you've kept a running summary of your 1-1s, including feedback delivered. If you haven't, walk through your email month by month to reconstruct what launched and put yourself back in each period. The goal is recognizing not just early accomplishments but the growth and change since.
- **Use concrete examples and excerpts from peer reviews.** Anonymize if needed. If you can't support a point with a concrete example, ask whether the point belongs in the review at all. Forcing specificity steers you away from reviews based on underlying bias.
- **Spend plenty of time on accomplishments and strengths.** Celebrate achievements and praise good work — in the writing and especially in the delivery. Don't let people skip the good stuff to obsess over their weaknesses. Strengths are what you'll use to decide promotions; write them down and reflect on them.
- **Keep areas for improvement focused.** In the best case, a couple of clear themes run through the peer feedback and your own observation. Common themes: people who can't say no to distractions and help other projects instead of finishing their own; people who do good work but are overly critical or rude in meetings and code reviews; people who can't break work into intermediate deliverables or balance planning with shipping; people who work well with engineers but poorly with other departments; people who cut corners and ignore the team's accepted practices.
- **Filter scattershot feedback.** Much peer feedback is scattershot and only moderately helpful — some reviewers reach for something to say, others carry uniquely harsh impressions. Sanity-check before delivering: if only one reviewer calls the work sloppy, is it sloppy, or does that reviewer have higher standards than the team? Share what's valuable; don't blindly relay every grudge.
- **Little meaningful improvement feedback means it's time to stretch.** It indicates the person is ready for promotion or more challenging work. If they're solid at their level but not promotion-ready, name the one or two skills to expand. Some people never need to be promoted out of their level — but tech skills need refreshing, so focus on new technical learning opportunities.
- **Avoid big surprises.** Set expectations before delivery. A broad underperformer should not be hearing it for the first time in a review. A recently promoted person should be warned she'll now be judged against higher standards.
- **Schedule enough time to discuss.** Fournier hands people a printed copy the evening before so they can read at home and arrive ready to talk. Even if they've read it, go over every section, starting with strengths and accomplishments — don't let them jump to the improvement areas; skipping praise undermines its value in reinforcing talent. If your company uses scaled ratings (1-5, or "fails to meet / meets / exceeds"), expect the score to be the hardest discussion for anyone below the top. People are uncomfortable being told they merely meet expectations, especially early in their careers; come prepared with the reasons for the score and examples of how to achieve a higher one.

## Ask the CTO: identifying potential

The critical mistake is treating potential as inborn traits or credentials: "He went to a great school," "She's very articulate," or, bluntest, "He's handsome, tall, and male." Biases lead us to assume potential and extend the benefit of the doubt long past the evidence.

The rule: a person who has never shown reasonable performance, and who has been with the company long enough for you to observe performance, probably doesn't have potential — at least at that company. The imagined potential is a figment of your imagination or your biases.

Real potential shows itself quickly: working hard, going the extra mile, offering insightful suggestions, helping the team in neglected areas. A high-potential person not yet performing still shows up for the team in ways others don't, even if their assigned work is slow-going. True potential paired with genuinely poor performance is rare; when performance is slightly below average, the fix is often moving the person where the potential can be realized — a strong visual designer struggling with coding tickets may thrive in UI/UX; a great firefighter who hates planning may suit an operations-focused team. Don't confuse potential as a grade-school teacher might describe it with the potential you care about: you aren't molding young minds, you're asking employees to do work and help you grow a company. Potential must be tied to actions and value produced, even if it's not the value you expected. The sooner you get over the disappointment that a "high-potential" person didn't work out, the sooner you can find and develop the true high-potential stars.

## Cultivating careers

As a manager you play a key role in getting people promoted. Sometimes you decide; more commonly promotions are reviewed by your management or a committee, so you must both know who deserves promotion and make the case for it.

- **Review your team a couple of times a year** and ask who is close to the next level. For early-career staff the answer is usually "yes, soon": people fresh out of college tend to get promoted at least once in their first couple of years because they're hired at an "up or out" level. (Example: a company hires college grads at level E2 with a policy that an engineer who shows no sign of advancing past E2 after two years at that level has no future there; the policy holds for E2-E4, but at E5 you can stay forever.) For this group, keep them promotable every couple of years — mostly by not getting in the way of the process: make sure they're learning to estimate their own work, deliver roughly within estimates, and learn from mistakes. Promotion evidence looks like independently completed projects or features, on-call and support participation, and engagement in team meetings and planning.
- **Learn how the promotion game is played at your company.** Every company differs, and you probably survived the process yourself without seeing its machinery. Ask your manager: how are decisions made, how early do packets need preparing, are there limits on promotions per year? Be fairly transparent with your team — when someone wants a promotion without a strong case, explaining what goes into the process shows them what to change.
- **Identify promotion-worthy projects and route them to people close to promotion.** Depending on how work is assigned, either give stretch projects directly or encourage volunteering. Keep an eye out for growth opportunities.
- **It gets harder as the team gets senior.** Many people won't advance past a certain level within the same company or team; there are fewer chances to show the leadership or breadth of impact senior promotions require. Sometimes all you can do is refer them to other leaders in the company for mentoring — and accept that they may be better off on another team or at another company, however much it hurts to lose them.
- **"Act at the next level before promotion."** Many companies require this to prevent the Peter Principle (people promoted to their level of incompetence); it also signals there's room on the team for someone working at that level. If there's no growth potential on your team because there's no room to work more senior, that may be a sign you need to rethink how work is divided so individuals can take on bigger responsibilities.

## Challenging situations: firing underperformers

One of the hardest things a manager does. HR shapes most of the process even at small companies, and the upside is that you have a procedure to follow — typically a **performance improvement plan (PIP)**: clearly defined objectives the person must achieve in a fixed period; achieve them and come off the plan, otherwise be fired. Depending on the company, a plan may be a genuine turnaround effort, but it's often written so the goals can't possibly be achieved in the time allotted — effectively a generous runway to find another job before being fired.

Principles:

- **Coaching out starts long before HR paperwork.** A basic rule of management is **no surprises, especially negative ones**. Understand what the person is supposed to deliver, and if it isn't happening, tell them early and often that they're not meeting expectations.
- **The ideal is expectations so clear you can say "You aren't doing X, Y, and Z. Do more of those things."** Know exactly what job the person is supposed to be doing, so corrective feedback names the specific missing behaviors. Of course, like all perfect circumstances, reality is rarely so simple — which is why late, vague feedback runs into believable excuses.
- **Excuses become unanswerable when feedback comes late.** An underperformer confronted only after six months will have believable excuses — bad onboarding, interruptions, your vacation, no one to ask — and some of them will be partly true. This is exactly why you give feedback early and often, treat feedback as a conversation, and keep records of what was delivered. Two failure modes to avoid: managers who ignore all excuses and lose employee after employee to a team that genuinely fails to onboard, coach, and set clear goals; and managers who accept every excuse until the problem can't be hidden and the team is furious at the inaction.
- **Always keep a written record.** Anywhere HR is active you'll need documented negative feedback to fire someone. With no HR, still deliver improvement feedback in writing, with a timeline, and have the person acknowledge it in writing (email is fine). It protects you legally and helps you treat people fairly.
- **Never put someone on a plan you wouldn't be happy to lose.** Smart employees read a formal plan as a sign the organization isn't a fit and leave as quickly as possible — even when they easily hit the plan's goals.

## Ask the CTO: coaching someone out of the company

The question: an employee has been around a couple of years, does OK work, asks what's needed for the next level, gets told, then retreats to his comfort zone. The answer: this is common; he's topped out in the organization and losing energy, and it may be time to coach him out.

Many organizations run "up or out" for early-career employees — entry levels of most engineering ladders expect progression within a set period, and someone who doesn't progress isn't meeting expectations and will be fired. Past those points, long-term employees should at least handle day-to-day work independently. When someone past up-or-out gets stuck: some people happily cruise at a level for their whole career, and if you're both satisfied, that's fine. But if the person wants to progress and can't do it on your team, you owe them clarity. Coaching out means saying plainly: you've been told what the next level looks like, you haven't been able to show it, and this team isn't the right place for you to grow — you need to move on if you want to progress. You aren't firing them. Give them the chance to find a role elsewhere in the organization or at another company, and when they go, let them go happily and preserve goodwill — like a couple that breaks up over not seeing a shared future and stays friends.

## Assessing your own experience

- Have you set up regular 1-1s with your direct reports?
- When did you last talk with your reports about career development? If it was more than three months ago, can you put it in your next 1-1?
- Have you given feedback to your reports in the last week? When did you last hand out kudos in front of the team?
- When did someone last behave in a way that needed correction? How long did it take you to give the corrective feedback? Did you give it in private or in public?
- Have you ever received a performance review that felt like a waste of time? What was missing that would have made it valuable?
- What is the most useful piece of performance feedback you ever received, and how was it delivered?
- Do you know how promotions work at your company? If not, can you ask someone to walk you through the process?
