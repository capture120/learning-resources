# Chapter 7: Improve Your Project Estimation Skills

Project estimation is one of the hardest skills an effective engineer must learn, and businesses depend on it for long-term planning. Because we always operate with imperfect information, successful project planning requires two things: increasing the accuracy of estimates and increasing our ability to adapt to changing requirements. This matters most on large projects — short projects slip by days, but multi-month projects slip by months or years, and a 2009 Standish Group study of 50,000+ software projects found 44% delivered late, over budget, or missing requirements, 24% failing entirely, and the average slipped project overrunning its time budget by 79%.

## Core Principles

- **Estimates should drive the plan, not the other way around.** Steve McConnell defines a good estimate as one clear enough about project reality that leadership can make good decisions to control the project. Estimates (an engineer's best guess of the work required) are distinct from targets (a desired business goal set by managers). Schedules slip when targets are allowed to alter estimates — engineers massage numbers to fit an immovable deadline, and reality catches up later. When estimate and target conflict, the productive conversation is whether to hold the date and cut features, or hold features and move the date.

- **Estimates are probability distributions, not best-case scenarios.** Tom DeMarco observed that we tend to treat an estimate as the most optimistic prediction with non-zero probability of coming true. Instead, express estimates as a range of outcomes — e.g., "50% chance we deliver in 4 weeks, 90% chance within 8 weeks." Estimates carry high variance early in a project; variance narrows as details get fleshed out, so revise estimates continuously with incoming data.

- **Budget for the unknown.** Slipped projects rarely fail from one calamity — as Frederick Brooks put it, the disaster comes from termites, not tornadoes. Many small unestimated tasks and interruptions compound. Estimated work time is not calendar time: an 8-hour workday does not yield 8 hours of project work once meetings, bugs, interviews, on-call, and one-off interruptions are factored in. The longer the project, the more likely some subset of interruptions hits the team, and slippage compounds further after a missed deadline (deferred work, vacations, pent-up demand for engineering time).

- **Specific goals and measurable milestones reduce risk.** Fuzzy definitions of success make tradeoffs impossible and let scope creep in. A well-defined goal filters must-haves from nice-to-haves and builds stakeholder alignment: state the goal, the constraints, and the assumptions you're making, and align every stakeholder on them — skip this and it's easy to over-optimize for what you believe are the main concerns, only to have a manager ask right as you're about to ship, "What about these key X, Y, and Z features that you didn't address?" Alignment also prevents a tragedy of the commons where each engineer's individually rational detour (a codebase cleanup, a rabbit-hole refactor, a partially-related feature) adds up to unacceptable aggregate delay. The mechanism is an asymmetry: the benefits of local engineering tradeoffs mostly don't materialize until after the project finishes, while the extra work lands inside the project's time window and introduces delays now. Building alignment makes team members internalize those delay costs and make consistent tradeoffs, and a well-defined scope lets teammates check on each other with "Does what you're doing contribute to the main goal?" Measurable milestones — a specified feature set, ideally with a target date, that either works or doesn't — keep the team honest about progress (versus the usual "almost done" or "90% code complete"), act as channels for communicating progress to the rest of the organization, and provide checkpoints to revise the plan — extend the deadline or cut tasks.

- **Reduce risk early.** Engineers gravitate toward visible progress on easy, well-understood parts, creating a false sense of being on track while risky areas stay unpriced. Tackle the riskiest areas first to surface estimation errors when adjustment is cheapest. System integration is a universal risk: it almost always takes longer than planned because complexity grows with interactions between code, not lines of code, and it's extremely hard to decompose integration into granular tasks early on, when the end state is still unclear.

- **Rewrites are the riskiest projects — approach with extreme caution.** Sam Schillace (who ran Gmail and Google Apps for four years) called rewriting from scratch "the cardinal sin," the costliest mistake engineers make. Familiarity with the original makes us underestimate rewrites more drastically than new work, and bundling extra improvements is tempting (Brooks's "second-system effect"). During a rewrite, every new feature must either wait for the rewrite or be built twice, and both costs grow with the timeline. Successful rewrites convert one large project into a series of smaller, incremental, behavior-preserving phases. Incremental rewriting is high-leverage because each step preserves the flexibility to shift to other, higher-leverage work — whether the rewrite is taking longer than expected or something unexpected comes up; it may increase the overall amount of work, but the dramatically reduced risk is often worth it. Expect resistance to a phased rewrite: it's discouraging to write intermediate code you know you'll soon throw away. The counter is that it would be even more demoralizing to miss the target date by a wide margin, delay new feature launches, or be forced to build urgent functionality twice.

- **Don't sprint in the middle of a marathon.** Overtime is not a cure for poor planning. Working 25% more hours does not produce 25% more output: hourly productivity falls with fatigue; the schedule slip usually means the whole project (including remaining work) was underestimated — and we estimate project beginnings far better than endings, where integration surprises can each cost a week or more; burnout and "undertime" follow; team dynamics suffer when not everyone can put in extra hours; coordination overhead spikes near deadlines; and sprinting incentivizes technical debt. Unless there is a realistic plan showing extra hours actually hit the date, redefine the launch scope or move the deadline.

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

- **Estimate vs. target (McConnell):** An estimate is the best guess of how long the work takes; a target is the desired business goal. Engineers create estimates; managers set targets. A good estimate gives leadership a clear enough view of reality to make good control decisions.
- **Estimates as probability distributions (DeMarco):** Report a range with confidence levels (e.g., 50% by week 4, 90% by week 8) rather than a single optimistic date.
- **Parkinson's law:** "Work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion" — the reasoning managers use to push estimates down. Granular estimates are the defense.
- **Anchoring bias (Dan Ariely):** In Ariely's experiment, students who wrote down higher last-two social security digits then estimated significantly higher wine prices, sometimes more than twice as high — arbitrary numbers anchor estimates. A manager's casual ballpark can anchor a project low; never commit to a number before outlining tasks.
- **The mythical man-month (Brooks):** People and time are not interchangeable. Communication overhead grows quadratically with team size, and new members need ramp-up time, so adding people rarely shortens a timeline. "Nine women can't give birth to a baby in one month."
- **Termites, not tornadoes (Brooks):** Disastrous slippage usually comes from many small unknowns and decisions, each losing a day at a time, not one major calamity.
- **Timeboxing:** Allocate a fixed time budget to open-ended tasks (research, technology choice) and commit to the best decision available when it expires, instead of estimating how long the exploration "will take."
- **Second-system effect (Brooks):** The second system is the most dangerous one a person designs — overconfidence leads to overdesign with all the ideas sidetracked from the first, making rewrites especially delay-prone.
- **Incremental, behavior-preserving refactoring (Fowler):** Small steps reduce the risk of introducing errors and keep the system working throughout, allowing gradual restructuring over time.
- **Undertime (DeMarco & Lister, Peopleware):** Overtime is almost always followed by an equal period of compensatory undertime while workers catch up on their lives; overtime's upside is exaggerated and its downsides (errors, burnout, turnover) are substantial.
- **Work time vs. calendar time:** One month of estimated engineering work takes longer than one calendar month to deliver; conflating the two is a primary source of surprise deadlines.

## Actionable Practices

- Decompose large projects into granular tasks; if a task estimate exceeds two days, break it down further. A long estimate is a hiding place for nasty surprises.
- Estimate how long tasks will actually take, not how long someone wants them to take. One workable compromise: estimates set the public goal, the manager's demands set an internal stretch goal.
- Give stakeholders confidence intervals instead of single dates (e.g., "50% likely in 4 weeks, 90% within 8").
- Let the person who will do the task make the estimate — skill and codebase familiarity vary, and divvying up estimation builds team-wide skill and visibility (most people underestimate). Schedule dedicated team sessions for estimating work when setting project goals.
- Never give a ballpark number before outlining the tasks; a low initial number anchors everything after it.
- Estimate the same task multiple ways to check soundness: (1) bottom-up from granular tasks, (2) historical data from similar work, (3) count subsystems times average time per subsystem.
- Don't assume adding people shortens the timeline; communication overhead grows quadratically and new members need ramp-up.
- Validate estimates against historical data (Joel Spolsky's approach): if you historically underestimate by 20%, scale estimates up by 25%. Some teams adopt a rule of thumb of multiplying engineering estimates by 2 to cover unestimated tasks.
- Timebox open-ended activities: commit to the best possible decision after, say, three days of research rather than estimating the research itself.
- Review estimates at a team meeting so others can challenge poor or incomplete ones; the accuracy and buy-in are worth the overhead.
- Track estimated vs. actual time per task (even a simple spreadsheet works — on one Python-to-Scala port, most members initially underestimated by 2x and calibrated within a week or two).
- When small tasks slip, pause and ask whether the same causes will affect future tasks; revise the plan with incoming data rather than letting it go stale.
- Build buffer into schedules for interruptions. Be explicit about hours per day each person will realistically spend on the project — Asana's Jack Heart maps each ideal engineering day to 2 workdays.
- Lay out a week-by-week schedule annotating who works on what, blocking off holidays and vacations (Alex Allain's lightweight sanity check at Dropbox).
- Explicitly track time spent on tasks that weren't in the original plan to build awareness of where the schedule leaks.
- Define a specific project goal tied to the problem being solved (e.g., "reduce 95th-percentile home-page latency below 500ms"), and use it to rule tasks in or out of scope.
- Outline measurable milestones — each delivering value that either works or doesn't — and scrutinize every task with "Is this a prerequisite for this milestone?"
- Do the riskiest tasks first; prototype end-to-end, benchmark core code, and characterize new infrastructure early to maximize learning and minimize risk.
- Reduce integration risk by building end-to-end scaffolding early: stub incomplete modules and stand up a partly functional system as soon as possible. Front-loading integration forces you to think through the glue between pieces (refining estimates), lets you fix breakages along the way while code complexity is still low, and amortizes integration cost through development so you know how much integration work actually remains.
- For rewrites, work incrementally: keep old and new versions deployable side by side (proxy routing, hybrid embedding), or, if that's impossible, break the rewrite into separate targeted phases (port first, refactor second).
- If you decide a small dose of overtime is truly necessary: secure buy-in from everyone; make sure everyone understands the real causes of the slip and that they won't persist; produce a revised, realistic plan with measurable milestones explaining why extra hours will hit the date; and be ready to abandon the sprint and cut losses if you slip from the revised timeline.
- Don't use the possibility of overtime as a crutch for skipping a contingency plan.
- Practice estimation on smaller projects where the cost of being wrong is low; the leverage of good estimation grows with project size.

## Key Takeaways

- Incorporate estimates into the project plan: estimates are an input for deciding whether a feature set can ship by a date; if not, negotiate features or the date. Never let the desired target dictate the estimates.
- Allow buffer room in the schedule for the unknown — competing obligations, holidays, illness — because the longer the project, the more likely some of these occur.
- Define measurable milestones that alert you whether you're on track or behind, and use them as opportunities to revise estimates.
- Do the riskiest tasks first to reduce estimate variance and project risk early; don't manufacture an illusion of progress by starting with what's easy.
- Know the limits of overtime: teams burn out by sprinting far from the finish line. Only work overtime when you're confident it will actually get you to the deadline.
