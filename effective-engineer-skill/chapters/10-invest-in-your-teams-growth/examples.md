# Chapter 10 Examples: Invest in Your Team's Growth

### Edmond Lau's "sink-or-swim" onboarding at Ooyala
On Lau's first day at the startup Ooyala, CTO Sean Knapp told him "sink or swim." His first assignment was to build and launch an already-promised video-scheduling feature in two weeks, inside a codebase full of technical debt, no documentation or unit tests, variables named things like `qqq`, and functions named `load2` and `load3` — mostly in ActionScript, a Java-like language he didn't know. Before he could even start, he had to learn ActionScript, get comfortable with Ruby on Rails, and familiarize himself with Flash video and graphics libraries. He shipped on schedule only by pulling two 80-hour weeks, one of the most tense experiences of his career, and subsequent new hires had to suffer similarly. The stress was an ineffective use of time and energy, and it convinced him that a smooth onboarding experience is extremely valuable.
**Illustrates:** Poor onboarding wastes time and energy and its costs repeat with every hire; investing in onboarding is high-leverage.

### Marc Hedlund's ladder definitions
Hedlund, former Senior VP of Product Development and Engineering at Etsy and later VP of Engineering at Stripe, distilled senior engineering levels: a staff engineer makes a whole team better than it would otherwise be, a principal engineer makes the whole company better, and a distinguished engineer improves the industry.
**Illustrates:** Seniority is measured by impact on the people around you, not individual output.

### Yishan Wong's magic wand thought experiment
Wong, drawing on a decade leading Silicon Valley teams, asks: if you could wave a magic wand and make every person in your company succeed at their job by 120%, what would happen? The company would almost certainly be a huge success, and even if you did nothing else you'd be swept along in everyone's success. His conclusion: the secret to career success is to focus primarily on making everyone around you succeed.
**Illustrates:** Helping the people around you be successful drives your own success.

### Andy Rachleff on credit and company success
Rachleff, co-founder of Benchmark Capital — a venture firm that has invested in over 250 companies and manages nearly $3 billion — tells his Stanford students that you get more credit than you deserve for being part of a successful company and less credit than you deserve for being part of an unsuccessful one.
**Illustrates:** Your career success depends largely on your company and team's success.

### Lau's 20-day interview stretch at Quora
When Quora had about 30 people, Lau interviewed one engineer per day for 20 days, spending roughly two hours daily on the interview, feedback write-up, and debrief. The 40 exhausting hours would have been justified by even one hire producing 2,000+ hours of yearly output; in fact that batch yielded five full-time engineers and one intern.
**Illustrates:** Hiring is extremely high-leverage when viewed in aggregate, especially at small companies.

### Albert Ni moves from code to recruiting at Dropbox
Ni, one of Dropbox's first ten engineers, built the company's original analytics and payments code. In October 2011, when Dropbox had 30 engineers and was struggling to hire, he switched his focus to recruiting even though he loved his engineering work — interviews weren't standardized, candidate sourcing wasn't organized, and campus recruiting wasn't formalized. He reviewed all inbound resumes, screened all interview feedback, attended every debrief, did the scheduling, and talked to candidates about the process. By early 2014 the engineering team had grown past 150, more than five times its size when he started.
**Illustrates:** Making hiring everyone's responsibility and investing in the recruiting process can outleverage traditional engineering work.

### Quora's practical coding exercise
Quora augmented whiteboard interviews with a laptop exercise where candidates navigated, debugged, and extended a large open-source codebase in their own editors, using Google and Stack Overflow freely. It revealed whether candidates could use a terminal, invoke basic UNIX commands, dive into unfamiliar libraries, set up a tight development loop, and write clean code — signals whiteboards miss.
**Illustrates:** Design interviews for high signal-to-noise on the qualities that predict success on your team.

### Hands-on interviews at Stripe, Ooyala, and others
Stripe designed on-site interviews to simulate daily engineering work: designing and implementing a small end-to-end system, squashing bugs in a popular open-source codebase, refactoring a poorly organized application, and pair programming on a self-contained project. Ooyala had candidates implement and demo a working Tetris game to test project management and technical tradeoffs under time constraints. Dropbox, Airbnb, Uber, Square, and others adopted hands-on or take-home exercises; the questions cost more to design and calibrate, but growing adoption suggests the payoff is worth it.
**Illustrates:** Hands-on interview problems yield better signal about whether engineers get things done.

### Airbnb's culture-fit interviews
Airbnb devotes at least two of its interviews to evaluating culture fit, because the company attributes much of its success to everyone's alignment on core values.
**Illustrates:** Use unconventional interview approaches when they surface the signals your team values most.

### Building Quora's onboarding program
After ad hoc onboarding at both Ooyala and Quora, Lau volunteered to build Quora's onboarding program. He researched Google's EngEDU training program and Facebook's 6-week Bootcamp, and interviewed engineers at other companies about what worked. The resulting program had four goals (fast ramp-up, culture transmission, breadth of fundamentals, social integration) and four pillars: codelabs for core abstractions (WebNode, LiveNode, DataBox, debugging tools), ten onboarding talks in the first three weeks, mentorship with daily check-ins then weekly 1:1s, and starter tasks — a commit on day one and a shipped bug fix, feature, or experiment by end of week one. He built the first codelab as a model, then recruited teammates to write the rest, and over time took on coordinating training materials, holding mentor-training workshops, and mentoring many new hires himself.
**Illustrates:** Design onboarding by defining goals first, then building mechanisms; upfront investment in reusable resources pays dividends with each hire.

### Mentors assigning starter projects at Quora
Quora mentors assigned small features or bugs from their own task lists as starter projects. New hires got guided learning on tasks the mentor had context on, while mentors freed themselves to move to higher-leverage projects. Lau advised mentors to pick projects they themselves could finish in a day, so even if ramp-up slipped, the hire could still likely ship in the first week. Lau told mentees on day one that ramping them up outranked his other work, and mentees sat physically close to their mentors.
**Illustrates:** Onboarding is a win-win — new hires get training while mentors get more done.

### The logs processor page on Mauna Loa
After months of 70-hour weeks sprinting to launch a rewrite of Ooyala's video player, Lau took a much-needed Hawaii vacation. While hiking the Crater Rim Trail on Mauna Loa, he got a text from Ooyala's CTO: "Logs processor down." He was the only person who knew how to run the system that turned raw viewer data from millions of online video viewers into customer analytics reports. Without laptop or Wi-Fi he could only reply that he'd look at it that night; he fixed it from his hotel, but the outage cost customers nearly a full day of new reports. His vacation was disrupted, and his team was stuck depending on an unavailable person.
**Illustrates:** Sole ownership makes you a bottleneck and a single point of failure; share ownership to raise the bus factor above one.

### Nimrod Hoofien on fungible engineers
Hoofien, Director of Engineering at Facebook, explains that when engineers are fungible, nobody is uniquely positioned to do any one thing — any task can be done by multiple people — which allows more degrees of freedom, more flexibility in development, and fewer constraints for on-call and support.
**Illustrates:** Shared ownership eliminates information silos and increases team flexibility.

### Ooyala's shift to shared on-call
Ooyala's engineering organization moved toward stronger shared code ownership: anyone on a team can be on-call and responsible for issues. This gives senior engineers more free time for other projects and gives junior engineers a chance to ramp up on the infrastructure and codebase.
**Illustrates:** Shared ownership frees senior engineers and develops junior ones.

### Champagne launches and the 5% infrastructure rewrite
While effective teams routinely run post-mortems after outages and high-priority bugs, they rarely give projects and launches the same healthy retrospection. Lau's scenario: your team launches a feature to a favorable press write-up, clinks champagne glasses to celebrate a job well done, and moves on to the next project — without ever asking how effectively the feature accomplished the team's goals. Or your team spends a few months rewriting infrastructure code to make it 5% faster; without pausing to debrief and review the data, it's hard to know whether that was the best use of the team's time. Even when project post-mortems do happen, the results often aren't widely distributed, so every team re-learns the same lessons on its own.
**Illustrates:** Healthy retrospection must extend beyond incidents to launches and projects, or opportunities for collective wisdom get lost.

### NASA debriefs and Flight Rules
NASA astronauts debrief with their support teams after every simulation and mission; a 4-hour simulation may be followed by a 1-hour debrief, and a space flight by a month or more of all-day debriefs, with experts firing barrages of questions at every decision. The cumulative lessons from 200+ space flights are captured in Flight Rules, a compendium begun by Mercury-era ground teams in the early 1960s, described by astronaut Chris Hadfield in An Astronaut's Guide to Life on Earth. It lists thousands of problematic situations — engine failures, busted hatch handles, computer glitches — with step-by-step solutions and the rationale behind each step; mission control consults it for every unexpected issue and adds to it after every new problem. With each shuttle launch costing $450 million, the time spent is easy to justify.
**Illustrates:** Blameless, rigorous debriefs that feed a documented playbook build durable collective wisdom.

### Five Whys at Amazon and Asana
To build their own version of Flight Rules, companies like Amazon and Asana use Toyota's Five Whys to find root causes of operational issues. Example chain: why did the site crash? Servers were overloaded. Why? A disproportionate share of traffic hit a few servers. Why wasn't traffic distributed? All requests came from one customer whose data lived only on those machines. By the fifth why, the team has moved from symptom to root cause.
**Illustrates:** Structured root-cause analysis turns incidents into transferable lessons.

### Lau's culture question across 500+ interviews
Having reviewed thousands of resumes and interviewed over 500 candidates — many from Facebook, Google, Amazon, Dropbox, Palantir, and Apple — Lau always asked each candidate to name one thing they liked and one they disliked about their previous company's engineering culture. He tallied the responses over time; the tallies painted pictures of toxic cultures that drove great engineers away and great cultures that attracted them, and the resulting list of ten traits informed what his own team's culture should look like.
**Illustrates:** What the best engineers seek in a culture — iteration speed, automation, code quality, shared ownership, learning — mirrors the high-leverage habits of effective engineers.
