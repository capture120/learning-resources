# Chapter 3 Examples: Prioritize Regularly

### User growth teams at fast-growing companies

Growth teams at companies like Facebook, Twitter, LinkedIn, Dropbox, Airbnb, Square, Uber, Lyft, and Path blend engineering, data, and product marketing to acquire and retain users. Their challenge is that the idea space is nearly unbounded: optimize the signup form, buy ads, speed up the app, create viral content, improve search rankings, iterate on engagement emails, drive referrals, improve mobile distribution, internationalize, and more. The right focus can compound small 0.5% wins into a million users, while the wrong focus can set growth back months or years.
**Illustrates:** Prioritization determines the leverage of everything else you do.

### Edmond Lau's Quora user growth team

As the first engineer and later engineering lead of Quora's user growth team, the author's team ran a repeating cadence: prioritize ideas by estimated return on investment, run a batch of experiments, learn from the data, and repeat. In one year the team grew Quora's monthly and daily active users by more than 3x. As the team ran more experiments, it got significantly better at identifying which changes could be done quickly and which activities had higher payoffs. His conclusion: successful growth teams prioritize rigorously and regularly.
**Illustrates:** Regular, rigorous prioritization drives outsized results.

### Atul Gawande's *The Checklist Manifesto*

Gawande documents how checklists drastically cut errors across fields, even for seasoned experts on routine work. Pilots with pre-flight checklists, surgeons with operation checklists, and construction managers with safety checklists all eliminate whole classes of avoidable mistakes simply by writing steps down and tracking them.
**Illustrates:** Track to-dos in a single, easily accessible list.

### Working memory research and memory champions

David Allen's *Getting Things Done* argues the brain is optimized for processing, not storage. The average brain actively holds only 7 +/- 2 items, and in experiments people fail to repeat longer sequences correctly over half the time. Memory champions can memorize 67,890 digits of pi only by spending enormous mental resources, and research shows that effort spent remembering reduces attention, impairs decision-making, and hurts physical performance.
**Illustrates:** Treat your brain as a processor, not a memory bank — offload tasks to a list.

### The author's early rejection of to-do lists

Early in his career, the author smugly dismissed to-do lists as overhead, trusting his head to track tasks. Within two years he noticed tasks falling through the cracks, adopted to-do lists, and they became integral to his workflow.
**Illustrates:** Even skilled engineers need an external canonical task list.

### Quora's wasted ranking exercise

The Quora growth team brainstormed hundreds of growth ideas and systematically estimated each one's impact (0.1%, 1%, 10%, or 100%) and implementation time (hours, days, weeks, or months). Many estimates were far off due to limited data, and every completed task spawned new tasks that jumped the queue, so most backlogged items were never reached. Much of the estimation work behind the fully ranked list was wasted.
**Illustrates:** Skip total orderings; use pairwise comparison against your current task instead.

### Yishan Wong on activity vs. production

Drawing on four years leading engineering teams at Facebook, Yishan Wong observed that activity is not necessarily production: status reports, organizing, building organizational systems, recording things multiple times, attending meetings, and replying to low-priority communications all have only weak, indirect connections to creating value.
**Illustrates:** Focus on what directly produces value.

### The latte vs. the investment portfolio

If you're saving money, skipping a $3 Starbucks latte barely moves the budget compared to spending an hour or two finding plane tickets that save a few hundred dollars. That in turn matters less than a few extra hours of job negotiation adding a few thousand dollars of salary, which long-term may matter less than a healthy investment portfolio compounding a few extra percentage points per year.
**Illustrates:** Make effort proportional to expected impact.

### Stephen Covey's four quadrants

In *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People*, Covey warns against confusing urgency with importance and advocates "putting first things first." He splits activities into four quadrants by urgent/non-urgent and important/unimportant. Letting Quadrant 1 (deadlines, high-priority support) and Quadrant 3 (most email, calls, meetings) run your schedule crowds out Quadrant 2 — career planning, relationship building, professional reading, better tools and abstractions, scalable infrastructure, learning, mentoring — which delivers the most long-term value.
**Illustrates:** Focus on the important and non-urgent.

### Nimrod Hoofien's quadrant labeling exercise

Hoofien, an engineering director at Facebook who also ran teams at Amazon and Ooyala, used to label every item on his to-do list 1 through 4 by Covey quadrant. He found it worked really well for whittling his work down toward the important and non-urgent, and called it a good tool for getting started.
**Illustrates:** A concrete tactic for prioritizing Quadrant 2 work.

### Csíkszentmihályi's flow research

Psychologist Mihály Csíkszentmihályi studied painters, violinists, chess masters, authors, and motorcycle racers, and described flow as effortless concentration so deep that people lose their sense of time, self, and problems. He called it the "optimal experience." Flow requires focused attention, and interruptions break it.
**Illustrates:** Protect your maker's schedule — engineers need uninterrupted time to be productive.

### Paul Graham's maker's schedule essay

In "Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule," Graham contrasts managers, who slice time into one-hour blocks, with makers like programmers and writers, who need units of at least half a day — an hour is barely enough to get started. Company meeting schedules built around manager time fragment maker productivity.
**Illustrates:** Consolidate meetings and preserve large contiguous blocks.

### Interruption-cost studies

A Microsoft Research study found employees take 10 to 15 minutes on average to return to focused work after handling email or instant-messaging interruptions; a UC Irvine study put the figure at 23 minutes.
**Illustrates:** The measurable cost of breaking the maker's schedule.

### David Rock's stage-and-actors metaphor

In *Your Brain at Work*, Rock likens working memory to a stage and thoughts to actors. The prefrontal cortex, which handles planning, decisions, and goals, must bring all relevant actors on the limited stage (room for 7 +/- 2) at once to decide anything. Working on too many things means spending mental energy shuffling actors on and off stage rather than watching their performance.
**Illustrates:** Limit the amount of work in progress.

### The author's overcommitment at Quora

Early at Quora, the author eagerly volunteered for two or three major projects at once and alternated between them. Progress came in fits and starts, momentum never built, no project got the attention it deserved, none was done excellently, and the dragged-out timelines made him feel less productive. He now deliberately serializes projects to keep momentum.
**Illustrates:** Fragmented attention across projects reduces quality and perceived productivity.

### The juggler in *Personal Kanban*

Barry and Benson compare work in progress to juggling: three balls take little effort, but six or seven demand far more concentration. They write that approaching capacity taxes the brain's resources and hurts performance, and that increasing work linearly increases the likelihood of failure exponentially.
**Illustrates:** Find and respect your personal work-in-progress limit.

### Gollwitzer's Christmas essay study

In the 1990s, psychology professor Peter Gollwitzer asked students heading to final exams to write an essay about their Christmas holidays and mail it within two days of Christmas. Half were also asked to specify when, where, and how they would write it. Of those who set these "implementation intentions," 71% mailed the essay; only 32% of the others did — a minor tweak that more than doubled completion.
**Illustrates:** If-then plans dramatically increase follow-through.

### Halvorson's if-then plans in *Succeed*

Social psychologist Heidi Halvorson formalizes the if-then plan: decide ahead of time that in a given situation you'll do a given task, such as "if it's after my 3pm meeting, then I'll investigate this long-standing bug." The plan links cue to behavior so the action follows nearly automatically, sidestepping the activation-energy barrier behind procrastination. Studies show if-then planning boosts goal completion for PSAT studiers, dieters cutting fat, smokers quitting, and people trying to use public transit more.
**Illustrates:** Fight procrastination with pre-decided cues and behaviors.

### The 20-minute-gap if-then plan

The author keeps a plan for short calendar gaps: "if I only have 20 minutes before my next activity, then I will ____," filled from a saved list of small tasks — finishing a code review, writing interview feedback, responding to email, investigating a small bug, or writing an isolated unit test. This avoids wasting half the gap deciding what fits.
**Illustrates:** If-then planning fills the small gaps in a maker's schedule.

### Survey of productivity systems

The chapter surveys workflow mechanics: David Allen's Getting Things Done groups to-dos by location context; Barry and Benson's Personal Kanban moves tasks across board columns ("backlog," "ready," "in progress," "done") with trial-and-error WIP limits; Francesco Cirillo's Pomodoro Technique times 25-minute single-task sessions; Nick Cern's *Todoodlist* champions pencil and paper. The author's conclusion after trying many: there is no best workflow — adapt general principles into a system that works for you, because the habit matters more than the mechanics.
**Illustrates:** Make prioritization a routine; mechanics are interchangeable.

### The author's Asana-based workflow

The author keeps his full personal and work backlog in Asana — chosen because it's fast, supports keyboard shortcuts, tags and filters tasks by project, and has mobile apps for updates on the go — and a weekly "Current Priorities" project with sections "This Week," "Today," and "Doing," borrowing from Personal Kanban. At the start of each week he fills it from the backlog and unfinished work, prioritizing tasks that directly produce value plus longer-term investments; because optimizing for learning is important and non-urgent, he deliberately includes learning tasks — at the time of writing, one thousand words per day on the book, learning about self-publishing, and daily progress on mobile-development tutorials. Each morning, when he has the most energy, he moves tasks into "Today," annotating each with its estimated number of 25-minute chunks (e.g., "[4]"); a typical day yields 10-15 chunks, with the rest lost to meetings. He uses if-then plans to pick tasks needing more mental effort and creativity in the mornings, blocks distracting sites via /etc/hosts, times sessions with a timer called Focus Booster, takes 5-minute breaks between them, and counts completed chunks to judge the day and check his estimates. While at Quora's Palo Alto office he tied the daily review to his walk to work, spending 5-10 minutes at a coffee shop reviewing to-dos and asking, when something slipped, whether he had worked on something more important, misprioritized, or procrastinated.
**Illustrates:** A concrete end-to-end implementation of a prioritization routine.

### Weekly and monthly planning sessions

The author commits to a 30-minute session each week (Sunday afternoon or Monday morning) to review what he accomplished, understand why planned items slipped, and scope the coming week — the place for larger course corrections, like resolving to build better team tools or finally brush up on mobile development. Roughly monthly, he holds a bigger session on the month's progress and future changes.
**Illustrates:** Daily reviews make small tweaks; weekly and monthly reviews make course corrections.
