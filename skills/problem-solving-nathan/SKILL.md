From Terrence Tao

* **Brain Dump** - “Mostly pen and paper actually… I have four giant blackboards and sometimes I just have to write everything I know about the problem… and then sit my couch and just see the whole thing.”
* **Visualize** - “I need lots of visual aids myself”
* **Speak Out Loud & Draw** - “It's always a combination of thinking inside your head and speaking out loud and working on the board.”
* **Explain the problem** - “A good rule of thumb is that if you cannot adequately explain the solution of a problem to a classmate, then you haven’t really understood the solution yourself, and you may need to think about the problem more.”
* **Understand the data.**
* **Understand the objective.**
* **Ask Dumb Questions** - “the answers to these questions will occasionally lead to a surprising conclusion, but more often will simply tell you why the conventional wisdom is there in the first place, which is well worth knowing.”
* **“if there are 10 things that are making your life difficult, find a version of the problem that turns off nine of the difficulties,** but only keeps one of them and solve that… after you know how to solve the 10 problems, 10 difficulties separately, then you have to start merging them a few at a time.” “We’re really stripping the problem down to its bare essentials.”
* **Partial Progress** - “one should value partial progress on a problem as being a stepping stone to a complete solution (and also as an important way to deepen one’s understanding of the subject).”
* “**Trial and error is one of the most important aspects of problem solving** … the way in which they fail is often very instructive.” “You eliminate certain strategies as being impractical. You isolate the key difficulties … every time you fail, you … see what the solution has to look like.”
* **“Write down what you know in the notation selected; draw a diagram.”**
* **“test examples should be as basic as possible.”**
* **“Abstract away any information that you believe or suspect to be irrelevant.”**
* **“Decompose or approximate data into a simpler one.”**
* **“Before I work out any details, I work on the strategy … a very complicated problem can split up into a lot of mini-problems.”**
* **“Reduce a problem into successively simpler sub-problems.”**
* **“If you have a complicated problem, you isolate a simpler version of the problem. Solve that first.”** - “when trying to solve a challenging problem using a given set of techniques, I recommend first replacing the problem with a simpler problem (such as a special case, or a toy model of the problem, or an informal version of the problem in which various non-rigorous ‘cheats’ are enabled”.
* **“Try simpler cases first** (while still capturing at least one of the difficulties of the problem that you cannot currently resolve)… if the simplified problem is unexpectedly easy, but one cannot extend the methods to the general case (or somehow leverage the simplified case to the general case), this is an indication that the true difficulty lies elsewhere.”
* **“If it is difficult to work globally, work locally instead.** Work locally on smaller sub-domains ‘then figure out how to patch things together later’”.
* **Use Analogies** - “You do try to isolate sort of the simplest metaphor for for your problem.”
* **“Draw pictures and try to build counterexamples”**… One should… draw a picture in which the hypotheses hold but for which the conclusion does not... the way in which your picture fails to accomplish this task is often very instructive.
* **“it is good to pause every now and then and recall *why* one is pursuing a particular goal”**
* **“Often the reason why something looks very deep or difficult at the beginning is you don’t have the right notation.”**
* **“Somehow, anthropomorphising the ‘enemy’—as well as one’s ‘allies’—can focus one’s thoughts quite well.”**
* **Try solving a problem that is just out of reach with your solution, but is solvable with a different method** - “A particularly good model problem to apply this method to is a problem which seems just out of reach of your intended technique, but can still be solved by a different method; in such cases the proof by the other method can provide valuable clues about how to proceed with your intended method, and can also save time by ruling out proof strategies that cannot possibly work because they contradict the conclusions coming from that other method.”
* **“If the conclusion and hypotheses look particularly close to each other, just expand out all the definitions and follow your nose**… sometimes, all one has to do is go back to first principles, write out all the definitions… and start plugging away at the problem.”
* **“The point of rigour is not to destroy all intuition; instead, it should be used to destroy bad intuition while clarifying and elevating good intuition.”**
* **“If you are trying to prove some ambitious claim, you might try to first look for a counterexample.”**
* **“When I get stuck … I write up how far I managed to get, and turn attention to something else … with … a fresh perspective.”** “Setting it aside … building up some skill on other related problems, and returning back to the original problem … is often the optimal strategy.”
* **“One has to know when one should be persistent … stubbornly working away at a dead end is not the most efficient use of your time.”**
* **“Modify the problem… By changing the language of your problem, you are activating different areas of your brain.”**
* **Play with problems** - “‘Playing’ with a problem, even after you have solved it, is very helpful … The long-term goal is to increase your understanding of a subject.” “You have to play with it, you have to tinker with it in order to really make it your own.”
