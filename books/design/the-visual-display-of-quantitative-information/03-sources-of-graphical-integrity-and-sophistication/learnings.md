# Sources of Graphical Integrity and Sophistication

Bad graphics are not mainly the product of bias; they are the product of who makes them and how. Tufte's central argument: the primary causes of lying and inept statistical graphics are the skills, attitudes, and organizational structure prevailing among those who design and edit them — artists with no quantitative training, governed by two false doctrines (statistics are boring; graphics are for the unsophisticated), and organizations that put art departments rather than people who know the substance in charge of data (p. 79). The cure is to reject both doctrines and to require three skills in graphical work: substantive, statistical, and artistic (p. 87).

## Diagnosis: why graphics lie

- "Why do artists draw graphics that lie? Why do the world's major newspapers and magazines publish them?" Bias and stereotyping cause some distortions, but the primary causes of inept graphical work are in "the skills, attitudes, and organizational structure prevailing among those who design and edit statistical graphics" (p. 79).
- Tufte (citing Howard Wainer) notes these errors were not made in Playfair's original work and were less widespread in the 1930s; the perceived need for graphs grew without a matching growth in training, and formal training in graphic presentation has declined at all levels of education (p. 79, n. 1).

## Cause 1: Lack of quantitative skills of professional artists (p. 79)

- Behind the inept graphic lies a lack of judgment about quantitative evidence. Nearly everyone producing graphics for mass publication is trained exclusively in the fine arts and has little experience analyzing data — yet that experience "is essential for achieving precision and grace in the presence of statistics."
- Even textbooks of graphical design are silent on how to think about numbers.
- Illustrators too often see their work as an exclusively artistic enterprise — "creative," "concept," and "style" combine as Big Think jargon for the small task of plotting a few data points. "Those who get ahead are those who beautify data, never mind statistical integrity" (p. 79).
- Actionable: anyone designing data graphics must develop judgment about quantitative evidence, not just visual style. Treat statistical skill as a prerequisite, not an optional extra.

## Cause 2: The doctrine that statistical data are boring (pp. 79-80)

- If artists believe statistics are tedious, it follows (falsely) that decorated graphics must pep up, animate, and exaggerate whatever evidence is in the data. Tufte documents this attitude in *Time*, Jan White's *Graphic Idea Notebook*, and the publisher's blurb for Herdeg's *Graphis/Diagrams* (pp. 79-80).
- The doctrine serves political ends inside organizations: if numbers are "dull dull dull," then an artist, an Art Department, and an Art Director are required to animate them. The doctrine thus encourages placing data graphics under control of artists rather than those who write the words and know the substance. "As the art bureaucracy grows, style replaces content" (p. 80).
- Key principle, quoted verbatim: **"If the statistics are boring, then you've got the wrong numbers."** Finding the right numbers requires as much specialized skill — statistical skill — and hard work as creating a beautiful design or covering a complex news story (p. 80).
- Actionable: when a dataset seems dull, fix the data selection (find the real news in the data), not the decoration.

## Cause 3: The doctrine that graphics are only for the unsophisticated reader (pp. 80-81)

- Many believe graphical displays should divert and entertain readers who find the words too difficult. Tufte's evidence: *Consumer Reports* fearing children would be "overwhelmed by too many facts"; an art director saying graphics exist to lure attention away from advertising rather than explain the news; a TV news director's rule "If you have to explain it, don't use it" (pp. 80-81).
- This thinking produces "unnecessary charts" — graphics that depict a single number that a sentence states better (p. 81).
- The audience is in fact smarter than illustrators believe: about 80 percent of the 1.5 million Sunday *New York Times* readers attended college, per a 1980 *Times* market survey. "The audience for statistical graphics is smarter than many illustrators believe" (p. 84, n. 12).
- Actionable: never patronize the audience. Do not design down. A graphic that needs no explanation because it contains nothing is worse than no graphic.

## The consequences (p. 81)

- Tufte applies E. B. White's rule for writing to graphics: "No one can write decently who is distrustful of the reader's intelligence, or whose attitude is patronizing."
- Contempt for graphics and their audience, combined with lack of quantitative skills, has "deadly consequences for graphical work: over-decorated and simplistic designs, tiny data sets, and big lies" (p. 81).
- Like censorship, these constraints lead to elliptical and eccentric communication: artists avoid the subtleties of the scatterplot and instead force bivariate data into convoluted univariate designs (p. 81).
- Actionable: when two variables relate, plot them against each other. Do not contort a relationship into side-by-side univariate bars.

## A measure of graphical sophistication: the relational graphic (pp. 82-84)

- **Definition.** Tufte's rough index of a publication's graphical sophistication is **the share of its statistical graphics that are relational** — designs that "link two or more variables but are not a time-series or a map" (p. 82).
- Why this measure: "Relational graphics are essential to competent statistical analysis since they confront statements about cause and effect with evidence, showing how one variable affects another" (p. 82).
- The design is not difficult: relational graphics have been used since 1765, are printed billions of times a year, and there is evidence twelve-year-old children understand them (p. 82).
- Method: randomly selected issues, 1974-1980, of 15 news publications; about 4,000 graphics examined; scaled by frequency and circulation, the sample represents 250-300 billion printed graphical images (p. 82).
- The relational graphic, unlike simpler designs, is an *explanatory* graphic — "surely a natural for news reporting and analysis" (p. 84).
- The relational share is only one of "a variety of measures of graphical intelligence and complexity"; another such measure, data density, is taken up in Chapter 8 (p. 82, n. 10).

Japan's lead is consistent with its quantitative culture — statistics as a national holiday, graph-drawing contests with about 30,000 entries, prize-winning relational graphics by seven-year-olds (p. 84).

### Tables 2 and 3 — Textbooks and tests outrank the press (pp. 84-86)

- Statistical graphics in college and even high school textbooks are more sophisticated than those in news publications. Top of Table 2 (p. 85): medicine and public health articles in Tanur's *Statistics: A Guide to the Unknown* (82% relational), Hilgard's *Introduction to Psychology* (68%), Pauling's *General Chemistry* (66%). Bottom: several social studies and government texts at 0%. Science texts consistently beat social science texts.
- Table 3 (p. 86): Japanese national university entrance examinations, 1979-1980, were 100% relational (n = 16); U.S. LSAT review materials 48%; U.S. standardized tests in science 67%, mathematics 41%, social studies 24%, general ability 21%.
- "Grade school children may experience a greater density of relational graphics than someone who reads only Business Week, the New York Times, Time, the Wall Street Journal, and the Washington Post" (p. 84).
- The verdict: "most news publications outside of Japan operate at a pre-adult level of intelligence in graphical design" (p. 84).

## The double standard (p. 86)

- Many news publications hold a double standard of integrity — one for words, another for graphics — and likewise a double standard of sophistication: "The statistical graphics are stupid; the prose is often serious and sometimes even demanding of expertise." Tufte demonstrates this with four genuinely demanding sentences from a single issue of the *New York Times* (on petrodollars, music criticism, sovereign immunity, and neuroleptics) printed alongside its childish graphics (p. 86).
- Actionable: hold graphics to the same editorial standard of accuracy and intelligence as the prose they accompany.

## Conclusion: the conditions of mediocrity and the remedy (p. 87)

The conditions under which many data graphics are produced — the lack of substantive and quantitative skills of the illustrators, dislike of quantitative evidence, and contempt for the intelligence of the audience — guarantee graphic mediocrity. These conditions engender graphics that:

1. lie;
2. employ only the simplest designs, often unstandardized time-series based on a small handful of data points; and
3. miss the real news actually in the data. (p. 87)

Further conclusions:

- "It wastes the tremendous communicative power of graphics to use them merely to decorate a few numbers" (p. 87). Much of the world is observed and assessed quantitatively, and well-designed graphics are far more effective than words in showing such observations.
- Remedy 1: reject once and for all the doctrines that data graphics are for the unintelligent and that statistics are boring. "These doctrines blame the victims (the audience and the data) rather than the perpetrators" (p. 87).
- Remedy 2, the chapter's organizing prescription: **"Graphical competence demands three quite different skills: the substantive, statistical, and artistic."** Most graphical work, particularly at news publications, is directed by a single expertise — the artistic. "Allowing artist-illustrators to control the design and content of statistical graphics is almost like allowing typographers to control the content, style, and editing of prose." Substantive and quantitative expertise must also participate in the design of data graphics if statistical integrity and graphical sophistication are to be achieved (p. 87).

## Designer's checklist from this chapter

- Bring statistical and substantive judgment into the design process; do not leave data graphics to visual specialists alone.
- If a graphic seems boring, find better numbers; never substitute decoration for content.
- Respect the reader's intelligence; avoid "unnecessary charts" that restate one number.
- Prefer relational designs (variable vs. variable) when the story is about cause and effect or how one thing affects another; do not flatten bivariate data into univariate bars.
- Apply the same integrity standard to graphics that the publication applies to its prose.
