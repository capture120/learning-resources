# Chapter 3: Sources of Graphical Integrity and Sophistication — Detailed Account

This chapter (pp. 79–87) closes Part I of the book, "Graphical Practice." Chapter 2 catalogued *how* graphics lie (the Lie Factor, design variation, missing context) and ended with six principles of graphical integrity (p. 77). Chapter 3 asks *why* the lying happens — and answers with a sociology of graphics production. The argument runs in two movements: first a diagnosis of the people and doctrines that produce inept graphics (pp. 79–81), then an original empirical study quantifying just how unsophisticated published graphics actually are, using Tufte's own cross-national content analysis of the world press, textbooks, and educational tests (pp. 82–86). The chapter ends with the book's organizational prescription: graphical competence demands substantive, statistical, *and* artistic skill, not artistic skill alone (p. 87).

The chapter carries no epigraph. (The epigraph that follows it — Richard Saul Wurman's "Everyone spoke of an information overload, but what there was in fact was a non-information overload," from *What-If, Could-Be*, Philadelphia, 1976 — belongs to Part II, "Theory of Data Graphics," which begins immediately after this chapter.)

## The opening question: why do artists draw graphics that lie? (p. 79)

Tufte opens with two blunt questions: "Why do artists draw graphics that lie? Why do the world's major newspapers and magazines publish them?" (p. 79).

His answer immediately sets aside the most common explanation. Bias and stereotyping are "the origin of more than a few graphical distortions," he concedes, but they are not the main story. Instead: "the primary causes of inept graphical work are to be found in the skills, attitudes, and organizational structure prevailing among those who design and edit statistical graphics" (p. 79). This is the chapter's thesis. Lying graphics are not chiefly products of propaganda or malice; they are products of *who* makes graphics, *what those people believe* about data and audiences, and *who controls* graphics inside publishing organizations.

A sidenote (n. 1, p. 79) supplies historical support, quoting Howard Wainer at length: "It is difficult to know why these same errors are being repeated. In Playfair's original work these kinds of mistakes were not made; moreover, these errors were not as widespread in the 1930s as they are now. Perhaps the reason is an increase in the perceived need for graphs . . . without a concomitant increase in training in their construction. Evidence gathered by the committee on graphics of the American Statistical Association indicates that formal training in graphic presentation has had a marked decline at all levels of education over the last few decades." (Howard Wainer, "Making Newspaper Graphs Fit to Print," in Paul A. Kolers, et al., eds., *Processing of Visible Language 2*, New York, 1980, p. 139.) The footnote does real argumentative work: it establishes that graphical lying is historically contingent — Playfair did not make these mistakes in the 1780s, and they were rarer in the 1930s — so the cause must lie in changed conditions of training and production, exactly where Tufte will look.

Tufte then identifies three sources of mediocrity, each given its own section: a skills deficit and two false doctrines.

## Cause 1: Lack of quantitative skills of professional artists (p. 79)

"Lurking behind the inept graphic is a lack of judgment about quantitative evidence" (p. 79). The structural fact: "Nearly all those who produce graphics for mass publication are trained exclusively in the fine arts and have little experience with the analysis of data" (p. 79). Yet such experience "is essential for achieving precision and grace in the presence of statistics" (p. 79) — note that Tufte makes data-analytic experience a precondition not only of accuracy ("precision") but of aesthetic quality ("grace").

The training gap is reinforced by the professional literature: "even textbooks of graphical design are silent on how to think about numbers" (p. 79). And by professional culture: "Illustrators too often see their work as an exclusively artistic enterprise — the words 'creative,' 'concept,' and 'style' combine regularly in all possible permutations, a Big Think jargon for the small task of constructing a time-series a few data points long" (p. 79). The sarcasm is pointed: grandiose vocabulary ("Big Think jargon") attached to a trivially simple statistical object (a short time-series). The section closes with the career incentive that locks the problem in place: "Those who get ahead are those who beautify data, never mind statistical integrity" (p. 79).

## Cause 2: The doctrine that statistical data are boring (pp. 79–80)

"Inept graphics also flourish because many graphic artists believe that statistics are boring and tedious. It then follows that decorated graphics must pep up, animate, and all too often exaggerate what evidence is in the data" (p. 79). The logical structure matters: Tufte presents exaggeration as the *deduction* artists draw from the boredom premise — if the data are dull, decoration and animation become the designer's duty.

To prove the doctrine is widespread rather than a straw man, Tufte cites three documents (pp. 79–80):

1. "*Time*'s first full-time chart specialist, an art-school graduate, says that in his work, 'The challenge is to present statistics as a visual idea rather than a tedious parade of numbers.'" (n. 2: *Time*, February 11, 1980, p. 3.)
2. "The opening sentence of the chapter on statistical charts in Jan White's *Graphic Idea Notebook*: 'Why are statistics so boring?' Sample illustrations supposedly reveal 'Dry statistics turned into symbolic graphics' and 'Plain statistics embellished or humanized with pictures.'" (n. 3: Jan V. White, *Graphic Idea Notebook*, New York, 1980, pp. 148, 165.)
3. "A fine book on graphics, Herdeg's *Graphis/Diagrams*, is described by its publisher: 'An international review demonstrating convincingly that statistical and diagrammatic graphics do not necessarily have to be dull.'" (n. 4: Walter Herdeg, ed., *Graphis/Diagrams*, Zurich, 1976.) Note the concession — even a book Tufte regards as *fine* is marketed through the boredom doctrine, showing how deep the assumption runs.

### The political function of the boredom doctrine (p. 80)

Tufte then advances the chapter's most distinctive sociological claim: the doctrine is not innocent error but serves organizational interests. "The doctrine of boring data serves political ends, helping to advance certain interests over others in bureaucratic struggles for control of a publication's resources. For if the numbers are dull dull dull, then an artist, indeed many artists, indeed an Art Department and an Art Director are required to animate the data, lest the eyes of the audience glaze over. Thus the doctrine encourages placing data graphics under control of artists rather than in the hands of those who write the words and know the substance. As the art bureaucracy grows, style replaces content. And the word people, having lost space in the publication to data decorators, console themselves with thoughts that statistics are really rather tedious anyway" (p. 80). The mechanism is self-reinforcing: the doctrine justifies an art bureaucracy; the bureaucracy's growth displaces substance; and the displaced writers adopt the doctrine as consolation, completing the loop.

The section ends with the chapter's most quoted maxim and its corollary: "If the statistics are boring, then you've got the wrong numbers. Finding the right numbers requires as much specialized skill — statistical skill — and hard work as creating a beautiful design or covering a complex news story" (p. 80). Boredom, in other words, is a symptom of bad data selection, and curing it is skilled statistical labor, not decoration.

## Cause 3: The doctrine that graphics are only for the unsophisticated reader (pp. 80–81)

"Many believe that graphical displays should divert and entertain those in the audience who find the words in the text too difficult" (p. 80). Again Tufte documents the doctrine with three sources:

1. "*Consumer Reports* describes the design of their new consumer magazine for children: 'For the first test issue, CU's professional staff produced an article about sugar that was longer on graphics than on information. We had feared children might be overwhelmed by too many facts.'" (n. 5: *Consumer Reports*, 45, July 1980, 408.)
2. "An art director with overall responsibility for the design of some 3,000 data graphics each year (yielding 2.5 billion printed images) said that graphics are intended more to lure the reader's attention away from the advertising than to explain the news in any detail. 'Unlike the advertisements,' he said, 'at least we don't put naked women in our graphics.'" (n. 6 identifies him: Louis Silverstein, "Graphics at the *New York Times*," presentation at the First General Conference on Social Graphics, Leesburg, Virginia, October 23, 1978.) The numbers here matter for the later argument: a single art director's output reaches 2.5 billion printed images a year, so these attitudes propagate at enormous scale.
3. "A news director at a national television network said that graphics must be instantly understandable: 'If you have to explain it, don't use it.'" (n. 7: Interview with author, July 1980.)

"This kind of graphical thinking leads to" (p. 81) — and the sentence resolves directly into a reproduced figure, the company cafeteria chart.

**Figure: the company cafeteria chart (p. 81).** A boxed chart titled "The Company Cafeteria was used by 9 Out of 10 Employees during the Fiscal Year 1949." Below the title sits a pictorial illustration of employees queuing with trays along a cafeteria serving counter, drawn over a horizontal scale running from 0 at the left to 100% at the right; the source line reads "Source: COMPANY REPORTS." The entire graphic exists to convey one number (90%) — a number its own title already states in words. The sidenote credits Mary Eleanor Spear, *Charting Statistics* (New York, 1952), p. 5, "who appropriately describes this as an 'unnecessary chart'" (p. 81). The chart is the reductio of designing for supposedly unsophisticated readers: a graphic so instantly understandable that it contains nothing.

## The Consequences (p. 81)

Tufte transposes a rule of prose style to graphics: "What E. B. White said of writing is also true of statistical graphics: 'No one can write decently who is distrustful of the reader's intelligence, or whose attitude is patronizing.'" (n. 8: in William Strunk, Jr., and E. B. White, *The Elements of Style*, New York, 1959, p. 70.)

Then the summary indictment, combining all three causes: "Contempt for graphics and their audience, along with the lack of quantitative skills among illustrators, has deadly consequences for graphical work: over-decorated and simplistic designs, tiny data sets, and big lies" (p. 81).

The next move is an analogy to censorship: "Like censorship, these constraints on graphical design lead to elliptical and eccentric communication. In seeking to avoid the subtleties of the scatterplot, artists drew up these convoluted specimens, forcing bivariate data into a univariate design" (p. 81). The point of the analogy: just as writers under censorship contort their prose to smuggle meaning past a constraint, artists who cannot or will not use the right design (the scatterplot) contort the data into the wrong one. Two figures illustrate:

**Figure: OECD tax loads and growth rates (p. 81).** A *New York Times* chart headed "Comparative Tax Loads and Economic Growth Rates in the Fifteen Largest OECD Countries." Fifteen countries (Japan at top, then Greece, Austria, France, Germany, Belgium, Canada, United States, Netherlands, Spain, Italy, Australia, United Kingdom, Sweden, and Portugal toward the bottom) are stacked in a single column. For each country a horizontal bar shows taxes as a percent of gross domestic product, read against a scale across the top running from 0 to 60 ("Taxes as Percent of Gross Domestic Product"); a separate stepped outline marks each country's economic growth rate ("Economic Growth Rate, 1973–1978 (Average annual increase in real per capita GNP)"), read against a different scale along the bottom (0.0 to 3.0). In-chart labels with pointers identify the two encodings — "Taxes/GDP (top scale)" for the bars and "Economic Growth Rate (bottom scale)" for the stepped line. Portugal's entry is starred ("PORTUGAL*"), with a small asterisked note inside the chart recording that its "growth" rate was in fact *minus* a fraction of a percent — negative growth, which cannot sit on the 0-to-3 bottom scale. Two variables that beg to be plotted against each other are instead threaded through one univariate bar arrangement with two competing scales, so the tax–growth relationship — the entire point of the data — must be reconstructed mentally. Sidenotes: *New York Times*, June 16, 1980, p. A-18; data from Allen D. Manvel, "Taxation and Economic Growth," *Taxation with Representation Newsletter*, 9 (June 1980), p. 3.

**Figure: "Views on the Economy Influence Carter Support" (p. 81).** A *New York Times*/CBS News Poll graphic rendered as pseudo-three-dimensional paired bars in heavy perspective on a dark ground. For each of ten states (Fla., Ill., Wis., N.H., Calif., Pa., Ohio, N.Y., Mass., N.J.), one hatched bar shows the percentage of Democratic voters saying their financial situation is worse than a year ago, and one white bar shows the percentage of the Democratic primary vote won by Carter; the two percentages are printed beneath each pair. A fine-print methodology note runs along the bottom of the chart: each electorate's feelings about their financial situation were determined by an identical question on separate *New York Times*/CBS News Polls in each of the 10 states, with the Democratic voters polled as they left voting places on their state's primary election day (exit polls). This is bivariate data (economic pessimism vs. Carter support, by state) that a scatterplot would display directly; instead the relationship is buried in twenty receding perspective bars.

## A systematic measure: the relational graphic (p. 82)

Having shown specimens, Tufte shifts mode: "But beyond reviewing a few examples, let us look more systematically at the level of graphical sophistication prevailing at different publications" (p. 82). To compare "a variety of newspapers, magazines, scientific journals, and books," he constructs "a rough measure of graphical sophistication — the share of a publication's graphics that are *relational*. Such a design links two or more variables but is not a time-series or a map" (p. 82).

The justification for the measure: "Relational graphics are essential to competent statistical analysis since they confront statements about cause and effect with evidence, showing how one variable affects another" (p. 82). The measure is therefore not arbitrary — it tracks exactly the analytic function (testing causal claims against evidence) that news reporting most needs.

Tufte pre-empts the objection that the standard is too demanding: "The design idea is a simple one, although not quite as simple as the bar chart, time-series plot, or data map. Relational graphics have been used since 1765 and are printed billions of times and ways every year; and there is evidence that twelve-year-old children understand the design" (p. 82). The twelve-year-olds claim carries a citation (n. 9): Clara Francis Bamberger, "Interpretation of Graphs at the Elementary School Level," *Catholic University of America Educational Research Monographs*, 13 (May 1942), with the note adding, "Additional data from textbooks and standardized tests are presented shortly." (The 1765 date refers back to Chapter 1's history: Lambert and Playfair's era and the first graphs of empirical variable-vs-variable data.)

"All these graphics count as sophisticated by our hardly demanding measure" (p. 82), Tufte writes, introducing the exemplar figures below. A footnote (n. 10) qualifies the measure's scope: a variety of measures of graphical intelligence and complexity are possible, and another such measure, data density, is discussed in Chapter 8.

### Exemplar relational graphics (p. 82)

**Figures: two Japanese high-school scatterplots (p. 82).** From Isao Sato and Miyohei Shinohara, *New Politics and Economics* (Tokyo, 1974), p. 113 — "a Japanese high school textbook" (sidenote). The first scatterplot puts an index of prices by industry (1965 relative to 1955, scale roughly 80–180) on the vertical axis against real net production per employed worker (1965/1955, scale roughly 140–280) on the horizontal, with labeled points for Japanese sectors — services, construction, agriculture and forestry, wholesale/retail, public utilities, mining, manufacturing — and a downward-curving fitted line: sectors with greater productivity growth had smaller price increases (services at the top left, manufacturing at the bottom right). The second plots economic growth rate (%, 0–12) against capital investment ratio (16–34%), with labeled country points — Britain at the bottom left, then South Africa, Denmark, Belgium, France, Italy, Sweden, Canada, Holland, West Germany, and Japan alone at the top right near 12% growth — and an upward fitted line: countries investing a larger share grew faster. Both are textbook relational designs, shown to high-schoolers.

**Figure: "Pace of City Life Found 2.8 Feet per Second Faster" (p. 82).** A clipped *New York Times* science story (February 29, 1976, p. 46, by Boyce Rensberger), reproduced with its headline, byline, text columns, and an accompanying street photograph of a pedestrian, topped by a scatterplot: walking velocity in feet per second on the vertical axis against city population on a logarithmic horizontal axis running from 100 to 1,000,000, with point symbols keyed by country (Czechoslovakia, France, Germany, Greece, Israel, United States) and labeled cities from Psychro at the lower left up through Itea, Corte, Bastia, Safed, Dimona, Netanya, Iraklion, Athens, and New Haven to Brooklyn, Jerusalem, Prague, and Munich at the upper right, with an upward-sloping fitted line — velocity rises linearly with the logarithm of population. The article reports a study by a Princeton University psychologist and his wife, an anthropologist, who measured how fast people walk along the main streets of municipalities of varying sizes and confirmed what most people have sensed informally: the bigger the city, the faster the inhabitants walk — about 2.8 feet per second faster in big cities than in small towns. Specific data from the story: on Flatbush Avenue in Brooklyn people walk at a brisk 5 feet per second, only a little slower than their counterparts in Wenceslas Square in Prague, who bustle along at 5.8 feet per second; both cities have populations over a million. By contrast the 365 citizens of Psychro, Greece, amble along at 2.7 feet per second, and the people of Corte, France (population 5,500), move at 3.3 feet per second. The example proves a relational graphic can carry a charming, genuinely explanatory news story — exactly what the press claims its readers cannot handle.

## The world press study: method (p. 82)

The study design, in Tufte's words: "The frequency of use of relational designs was counted for randomly selected issues from 1974 to 1980 of each of 15 news publications. A total of about 4,000 graphics were examined in sampled issues. Scaling up the observed data by the frequency and circulation of the publication indicates that the sample represents a population of 250 to 300 billion printed graphical images" (p. 82). Three method facts to retain: random issue sampling over seven years; roughly 4,000 graphics coded; and the population represented — a quarter-trillion printed images — which is what makes press graphical practice consequential at all.

So the best newspaper (9.3%) sits more than fourfold below the scientific journal standard (42%) — and the typical major paper sits at or near zero.

## Japan's statistical culture (p. 84)

Why does Japan lead? "Japanese graphical distinction is consistent with that country's heavy use of statistical techniques in the workplace and extensive quantitative training, even in the early years of school" (p. 84). The evidence is a long block quotation from Andrew H. Malcolm, "Data-Loving Japanese Rejoice on Statistics Day," *New York Times*, October 28, 1977, p. A-1 (n. 11):

> ". . . no nation ranks higher in its collective passion for statistics. In Japan, statistics are the subject of a holiday, local and national conventions, awards ceremonies and nationwide statistical collection and graph-drawing contests. 'This year,' said Yoshiharu Takahashi, a Government statistician, 'we had almost 30,000 entries. Actually, we had 29,836.'
> Entries in the [children's] statistical graph contest were screened three times by judges, who gave first prize this year to the work of five 7-year-olds. Their graph creation, titled 'Mom, play with us more often,' was the result of a survey of 32 classmates on the frequency that mothers play with their offspring and the reasons given for not doing so. . . . Other children's work examined the frequency of family phone usage and correlated the day's temperature with cicada singing."

(The quotation contains its own joke about statistical culture: the official correcting "almost 30,000" to the exact count, 29,836.) Tufte adds one dry sentence: "Note the relational design of the last children's graphic mentioned" (p. 84) — Japanese children correlating temperature with cicada singing produce, unprompted, the very design that zero percent of the *Washington Post*'s graphics achieve.

## The U.S. press at the bottom; the explanatory graphic (p. 84)

"The five U.S. publications examined rank toward the bottom of the world list, along with *Pravda* and a few European papers. Note, in Table 1, the complete dominance of non-relational designs at the lower-ranked newspapers and magazines" (p. 84). Why it matters: "This is unfortunate because the relational graphic, unlike the simpler designs, is an *explanatory* graphic — surely a natural for news reporting and analysis" (p. 84). The italicized term "explanatory" names the function: relational designs do not merely record, they explain, which is precisely the journalistic task.

Then the comparison that frames Tables 2 and 3: "The statistical graphics found in college and even high school textbooks are more sophisticated than those in news publications. Indeed, grade school children may experience a greater density of relational graphics than someone who reads only *Business Week*, the *New York Times*, *Time*, the *Wall Street Journal*, and the *Washington Post*. Tables 2 and 3 record the graphical sophistication of textbooks and of a variety of standardized educational tests" (p. 84). And the verdict: "A comparison between these data and Table 1 suggests that most news publications outside of Japan operate at a pre-adult level of intelligence in graphical design" (p. 84).

Sidenote 12 (p. 84) rebuts the implicit defense that newspaper audiences couldn't handle better: "Readers of news publications, particularly the elite press, have considerable educational and professional attainments, with the resulting graphical skills. About 80 percent of the 1.5 million readers of the Sunday *New York Times* attended college, according to a 1980 *Times* market survey. The audience for statistical graphics is smarter than many illustrators believe."

## The double standard of sophistication (p. 86)

Tufte now ties the empirical study back to the integrity theme of Chapter 2: "And so, just as there is a double standard of integrity at a good many news publications — one for words, another for graphics — so there is a double standard of sophistication. The statistical graphics are stupid; the prose is often serious and sometimes even demanding of expertise, as can be seen in these sentences from a single issue of the *New York Times*" (p. 86). Four passages follow, all from one day's paper, chosen to span economics, music criticism, law, and pharmacology:

1. "Recycling petrodollars may postpone the day of reckoning, but its effects would soon become intolerable without a steady depreciation in their purchasing power. Floating rates of exchange cannot restore even a semblance of equilibrium."
2. "Numerous facets of the performance seem decidedly unfashionable if not downright eccentric: the square-toed instrumental phrasing and the frequent plodding tempos in the arias, the Romanticized treatment of the chorales, the generous retards at every cadence, the often intrusively elaborate continuo improvisations and an inconsistent attitude toward expression which ranges from heaving Mahlerian emphases to mechanical literalism."
3. "The Court shows no sign of retreating from its view that a state government is protected by sovereign immunity against court orders to pay retroactive damages for past violations."
4. "And Dr. Garth Graham, a medical director with Smithkline Corp., makers of Thorazine, noted that neuroleptics produce no euphoria, and are therefore unlikely to be abused by patients with a history of drug or alcohol dependence. 'They are, if anything, dysphorogenic,' Dr. Graham said."

The argumentative force: a paper that trusts its readers with "continuo improvisations," "sovereign immunity," and "dysphorogenic" cannot honestly claim those same readers would be defeated by a scatterplot. The doctrine of the unsophisticated reader is applied to graphics alone — a double standard, not a considered editorial judgment.

## Conclusion (p. 87)

The conclusion gathers the three causes into one sentence of diagnosis: "The conditions under which many data graphics are produced — the lack of substantive and quantitative skills of the illustrators, dislike of quantitative evidence, and contempt for the intelligence of the audience — guarantee graphic mediocrity. These conditions engender graphics that (1) lie; (2) employ only the simplest designs, often unstandardized time-series based on a small handful of data points; and (3) miss the real news actually in the data" (p. 87). Note how the three failures map onto the book so far: (1) is Chapter 2's lie problem; (2) is this chapter's sophistication problem (and "unstandardized" recalls Chapter 2's principle about deflated and standardized units); (3) is the deepest failure — content, the wrong numbers altogether.

The stakes: "It wastes the tremendous communicative power of graphics to use them merely to decorate a few numbers. Moreover, much of the world these days is observed and assessed quantitatively — and well-designed graphics are far more effective than words in showing such observations" (p. 87).

"How can graphic mediocrity be remedied?" (p. 87). Two answers:

First, repudiate the doctrines: "Surely there is something to be said for rejecting once and for all the doctrines that data graphics are for the unintelligent and that statistics are boring. These doctrines blame the victims (the audience and the data) rather than the perpetrators" (p. 87).

Second, restructure who controls graphics — the chapter's closing prescription, quoted in full: "Graphical competence demands three quite different skills: the substantive, statistical, and artistic. Yet now most graphical work, particularly at news publications, is under the direction of but a single expertise — the artistic. Allowing artist-illustrators to control the design and content of statistical graphics is almost like allowing typographers to control the content, style, and editing of prose. Substantive and quantitative expertise must also participate in the design of data graphics, at least if statistical integrity and graphical sophistication are to be achieved" (p. 87). The typographer analogy is the capstone: typography is a genuine craft, as is illustration, but neither qualifies its practitioner to decide *what is said*. Content authority over data graphics must include people who know the subject and the statistics.

With this, Part I ("Graphical Practice") closes. Part II, "Theory of Data Graphics," opens next with the Wurman epigraph on "non-information overload" and Chapter 4's principle — "Above all else show the data" — turning from the sociology of bad graphics to a constructive theory of good ones.

## Reference index of terms, claims, and citations in this chapter

- **Relational graphic** (term of art, p. 82): a design that "links two or more variables but is not a time-series or a map"; essential to statistical analysis because it confronts cause-and-effect statements with evidence; in use since 1765; understood by twelve-year-olds (Bamberger 1942); the *explanatory* graphic (p. 84).
- **Graphical sophistication measure** (p. 82): share of a publication's statistical graphics that are relational; "hardly demanding"; one of several possible measures — data density is another (n. 10, Chapter 8).
- **Three causes of inept graphics** (pp. 79–81): lack of quantitative skills among fine-arts-trained illustrators; the doctrine that statistics are boring; the doctrine that graphics are for the unsophisticated reader.
- **Three consequences** (p. 81): over-decorated and simplistic designs, tiny data sets, big lies.
- **Three engendered failures** (p. 87): graphics that lie, that use only the simplest designs (unstandardized, few-point time-series), and that miss the real news in the data.
- **Three required skills** (p. 87): substantive, statistical, artistic.
- **Key maxims**: "If the statistics are boring, then you've got the wrong numbers" (p. 80); "As the art bureaucracy grows, style replaces content" (p. 80); "Those who get ahead are those who beautify data, never mind statistical integrity" (p. 79); "These doctrines blame the victims (the audience and the data) rather than the perpetrators" (p. 87); "The audience for statistical graphics is smarter than many illustrators believe" (n. 12, p. 84).
