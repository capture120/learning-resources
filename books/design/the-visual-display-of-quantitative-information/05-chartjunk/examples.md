# Chapter 5 Examples: Chartjunk: Vibrations, Grids, and Ducks

### Op art stripe band (p. 108)

A band of dense diagonal black-and-white stripes opens the moiré discussion. Tufte shows it to demonstrate how a pattern interacts with the physiological tremor of the eye to produce apparent vibration and movement that extends beyond the ink to the whole page. When exploited deliberately by op artists such as Bridget Riley and Victor Vasarely, the effect is eye-catching art; when it appears accidentally in statistical graphics, it is noise that clouds the flow of information.

### Brazilian cotton textiles statistical poster (p. 108)

"Tecidos de Algodão" (Cotton Textiles), from the Instituto de Expansão Commercial, *Brasil: Graphicos Economicos-Estatisticas* (Rio de Janeiro, 1929). A single page combines a large ring chart for 1927 with surrounding bar charts on cotton factories, capital, production, taxes, and imports, every element filled with heavy stripes, checkerboards, and cross-hatch patterns. Tufte uses it as a vivid early example of moiré vibration in a technical/statistical publication: the riot of competing hatchings makes the whole display shimmer, drowning the data.

### Aortic regurgitation 3D pyramid chart, New England Journal of Medicine (p. 109)

From Nicholas T. Kouchoukos et al., "Replacement of the Aortic Root with a Pulmonary Autograft in Children and Young Adults with Aortic-Valve Disease," *NEJM*, 330 (January 6, 1994). Serial echocardiographic severity grades for 31 patients are drawn as rows of hatched 3D pyramids receding into fake depth. Tufte's diagnosis: what should have been a simple table became "a duck moiré with an unintentional Necker Illusion" — the two back planes optically flip to the front, some pyramids conceal others, and one variable (the stacked depth of the pyramids, which he calls "stupid") has no label or scale. We learn very little about the data.

### Equally spaced hatched bar charts from Science and a psychology journal (p. 109)

Two more published figures: James T. Kuznicki and N. Bruce McCutcheon, "Cross-Enhancement of the Sour Taste on Single Human Taste Papillae," *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*, 108 (1979); and Eain M. Cornford and Marie E. Huot, "Glucose Transfer from Male to Female Schistosomes," *Science*, 213 (September 11, 1981). Both use hatched, evenly spaced bars. Lesson: these displays teach little about the data but do reveal that moiré vibration may well be at a maximum for equally spaced bars.

### JASA style-sheet histogram, "a figure prepared in the proper form" (p. 110)

The style sheet once provided by the *Journal of the American Statistical Association* ("JASA Style Sheet," *JASA*, 71, March 1976) offered a histogram of average probabilities as an exemplary figure. Tufte counts the cost: the display required 131 line-strokes and 15 digits to communicate its simple information, and the vibrating vertical lines are poorly drawn, unevenly spaced, and misaligned with the vertical axis. Lesson: even an official statistical journal's model of "proper form" was vibrating chartjunk.

### Survey: moiré vibration in the ten most-cited scientific journals (p. 110)

Tufte tabulates a random sample of issues published 1980-1982 from the ten most frequently cited scientific journals, recording the percentage of graphics with moiré vibration. Results range from 2% (Biochemistry, 568 graphics sampled; Journal of Biological Chemistry, 565) through 11% (Nature), 12% (PNAS), 15% (Lancet), 17% (Science), to 21% (New England Journal of Medicine, 338 graphics). Lesson: vibrating chartjunk frequents even the most prestigious scientific journals, and the more clinical/medical journals are the worst offenders.

### Catalog of vibrating fill patterns (p. 111)

A full-page specimen sheet of dozens of hatching, dot, wave, and cross-hatch fill patterns — once produced with thin plastic transfer sheets, now generated instantly by computer programs such as Excel. Tufte displays them as "a few of the many vibrating possibilities" to make two prescriptions concrete: replace cross-hatching with tint screens of shades of gray, and label areas with words rather than encoding them with hatching.

### Survey: moiré vibration in graphics textbooks and software manuals (p. 112)

A second table measures the percentage of graphics with moiré vibration in handbooks, textbooks, and computer-graphics manuals, ordered by publication date: Brinton, *Graphic Methods for Presenting Facts* (1914) 12%; Satet, *Les Graphiques* (1932) 29%; Arkin and Colton (1936) 17%; Spear, *Charting Statistics* (1952) 46%; Rogers (1961) 32%; Monkhouse and Wilkinson (1971) 14%; Schmid and Schmid (1979) 22%; MacGregor (1979) 34%; the *SAS/GRAPH User's Guide* (1980) 68%; the *Tell-A-Graf User's Manual* (1981) 53%. Lesson: the very books and manuals that teach graphics present vibrating designs as exemplars, with computer-graphics manuals the most chartjunky of all.

### Bertin's "flirt with ambiguity" claim (p. 112)

Jacques Bertin (*Semiology of Graphics*) urged designers to exploit moiré resonance — "to flirt with ambiguity without succumbing to it." Tufte treats this as the strongest case for optical effects and rejects it: it is a clever idea, but no good examples can be found, because moiré vibration is an undisciplined ambiguity with an illusive, eye-straining quality that contaminates the entire graphic.

### Age-sex pyramid of the population of France, 1967 (p. 113)

A population pyramid (males left, females right, by year of birth) based on data from the Institut National de la Statistique et des Études Économiques, *Annuaire statistique de la France, 1968*, as redrawn in Shryock and Siegel, *The Methods and Materials of Demography* (1973). The original's dark grid camouflages the data profile — the dramatic notches from military losses and birth deficits of the two World Wars. Tufte's revision quiets the grid (light lines inside the silhouette only) and gives emphasis to the data. Lesson: dark grid lines carry no information, clutter the graphic, and can hide the data's own shape.

### Tukey and Tukey multiwindow plot of particle physics momentum data (p. 114)

From Paul A. Tukey and John W. Tukey, "Data-Driven View Selection; Agglomeration and Sharpening," in Vic Barnett, ed., *Interpreting Multivariate Data* (1981). An ingenious multiwindow display: a matrix of small X4-X7 scatterplots conditioned on levels of X1 and X3, with marginal plots along the edges and the unconditional scatter in the corner. The flaw: doubled grid lines around every window consume 18 percent of the area, and optical white dots appear at the grid intersections. Tufte's redrawing removes the doubled frames, eliminating the vibration while preserving the design's ingenuity. Lesson: even excellent designs are improved by erasing non-data grid ink.

### Marey's graphical train schedule, three grid treatments (pp. 115-116)

The classic Marey Paris-Lyon train schedule (diagonal lines plot each train against stations and time of day) is shown three ways. The original grid of closely spaced dark verticals is "very active," competing with the train lines. Thinning the grid lines "helps a little bit." The better treatment is a **gray grid**, which recedes so the train paths dominate. Lesson: when a graphic serves as a look-up table a grid genuinely helps reading and interpolation, but it must be muted relative to the data; a delicate gray grid may even promote more accurate data reconstruction than a dark one.

### The Big Duck (pp. 116-117)

A duck-shaped store building in Flanders, New York (photographed by Tufte, July 2000), gives the "duck" its name: the whole structure is itself decoration. Tufte quotes Robert Venturi, Denise Scott Brown, and Steven Izenour, *Learning from Las Vegas* (1977): modern architects who righteously abandoned ornament unconsciously designed buildings that *were* ornament, distorting the whole building into a duck; the passage revives Ruskin's claim that architecture is the decoration of construction and appends Pugin's warning, "It is all right to decorate construction but never construct decoration." Tufte transfers the lesson to data graphics: a graphic where data structures have become Design Elements purveying Graphical Style instead of quantitative information is a duck.

### "Age Structure of College Enrollment," American Education magazine (p. 118)

A 1970s display from the magazine *American Education*, one of a series of weird three-dimensional displays that "delighted connoisseurs of the graphically preposterous." Five bright colors, fake perspective, and a broken axis report, almost by happenstance, only five pieces of data (the under-25/25-and-over division within each year sums to 100 percent). Tufte's verdict: "This may well be the worst graphic ever to find its way into print." Lesson: fake perspective added to the data structure is high-fashion "Boutique Data Graphics" chartjunk found in annual reports, advertising, mass media, and muddled social science.

### California Water Atlas, "Applied Irrigation Water 1972" (p. 119)

From William L. Kahrl et al., *The California Water Atlas* (Sacramento, 1978, 1979). A handsome relief map of California with stacked colored block-grids per hydrologic basin, each block representing 5,000 acre-feet of water applied to a crop type. Tufte presents it as proof that "there are some superbly produced ducks" — beautiful craftsmanship does not stop a graphic from being dominated by Design Elements rather than efficient data presentation.

### "Type-Set Politics" bar chart and its table redesign (pp. 120-121)

From Arthur H. Miller, Edie N. Goldenberg, and Lutz Erbring, "Type-Set Politics: Impact of Newspapers on Public Confidence," *American Political Science Review*, 73 (1979). A computer-drawn bar chart of percent critical newspaper articles by issue area exhibits the **We-Used-A-Computer-To-Build-A-Duck Syndrome**: thin substance; clotted, crinkly all-uppercase sans serif lettering; pointlessly ordered cross-hatching; labels in computer abbreviations (INF, UNEM, GOVTPOW); optical vibration; and an overly busy vertical scale with more percentage markers than data points. Tufte's redesign (p. 121) replaces it with a plain table of the nine issue areas, article counts, and percent critical, sorted by count — because the information consists of a few numbers and a good many words, a table tells the story better than the computerized graphic. The chart evokes "Isn't it remarkable that the computer can be programmed to draw like that?" instead of "My, what interesting data."

### Other examples mentioned

- Jonathan Swift's indictment of 17th-century cartographers opens the chapter as an epigraph (p. 106): "With savage pictures fill their gaps / And o'er unhabitable downs / Place elephants for want of towns" — decoration substituting for data, the chapter's theme in three lines.
- Bridget Riley and Victor Vasarely, named as op artists who exploit moiré effects deliberately and successfully — in art, not data graphics (p. 108).
- The chapter's conclusion (p. 121) lists exemplary intriguing designs by way of contrast with chartjunk: Minard on Napoleon in Russia, Marey's graphical train schedule, the cancer maps, the *Times* weather history of New York City, the chronicle of the annual adventures of the Japanese beetle, and the new view of the galaxies (all discussed elsewhere in the book).
- Ad Reinhardt's 1955 Whitney Museum catalogue statement closes the chapter (p. 122) as an epigraph for quiet, clutter-free design: "Clarity, completeness, quintessence, quiet. No noise, no schmutz..."
