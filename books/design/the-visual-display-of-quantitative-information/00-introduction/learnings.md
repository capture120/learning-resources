# Introduction (and Introduction to the Second Edition)

The central argument: data graphics are not decoration or a mere substitute for small tables — at their best they are *instruments for reasoning about quantitative information*. Often the most effective way to describe, explore, and summarize a set of numbers, even a very large set, is to look at pictures of those numbers. Well-designed data graphics are usually the simplest and at the same time the most powerful of all methods for analyzing and communicating statistical information (p. 10).

## What a data graphic is (p. 10)

Definition: "Data graphics visually display measured quantities by means of the combined use of points, lines, a coordinate system, numbers, symbols, words, shading, and color."

- Abstract, non-representational pictures used to show numbers are a surprisingly recent invention — statistical graphics (length and area to show quantity, time-series, scatterplots, multivariate displays) were invented only in 1750–1800, long after logarithms, Cartesian coordinates, the calculus, and basic probability theory.
- The lateness is "perhaps because of the diversity of skills required": visual-artistic, empirical-statistical, and mathematical. (Tufte offers this as a conjecture, not a settled explanation.)
- William Playfair (1759–1823) developed or improved upon nearly all the fundamental graphical designs, seeking to replace conventional tables of numbers with the systematic visual representations of his "linear arithmetic."

## Core claims a designer should internalize (p. 10)

- Modern data graphics can do much more than simply substitute for small statistical tables.
- "At their best, graphics are instruments for reasoning about quantitative information."
- For describing, exploring, and summarizing numbers — even very large data sets — looking at pictures of the numbers is often the most effective method.
- Well-designed data graphics are usually the simplest *and* the most powerful method of analyzing and communicating statistical information. Simplicity and power are not in tension.

## Structure and purpose of the book (pp. 10–11)

- Part I reviews graphical practice in the two centuries since Playfair: graphical glories (Chapter 1, Graphical Excellence), lapses and lost opportunities (Chapter 2, Graphical Integrity... lapses), with Chapter 3 (graphical integrity and sophistication) accounting for the differences in quality of graphical design.
- Part II provides a *language* for discussing graphics and a *practical theory* of data graphics. The theory:
  - applies to most visual displays of quantitative information;
  - leads to changes and improvements in design;
  - suggests why some graphics might be better than others;
  - generates new types of graphics.
- The method emphasized: maximizing principles, empirical measures of graphical performance, and the *sequential improvement of graphics through revision and editing*. Treat graphic design as iterative editing, not a one-shot act.
- Insights into graphical design come from theories of what makes for excellence in art, architecture, and prose — not only from statistics.

## Scope and universality of the principles (p. 11)

- The design of statistical graphics is a *universal* matter — like mathematics — not tied to the unique features of a particular language. The descriptive concepts (a vocabulary for graphics) and the principles advanced apply to most designs.
- The book is about design and statistics, but also about communicating information through the *simultaneous presentation of words, numbers, and pictures* — integrate these, don't segregate them.
- Scale of impact: somewhere between 900 billion (9 × 10¹¹) and 2 trillion (2 × 10¹²) images of statistical graphics are printed each year, worldwide. The book's principles apply to most of them; some suggested changes are small, others substantial, with consequences for hundreds of billions of printed pages.
- Tufte's evidence for the scope of his ideas: he shows how frequently a principle applies to (a random sample of) news and scientific graphics.
- "Most of all, then, this book is a celebration of data graphics." (p. 11) The aim is that viewers and makers "will never view or create statistical graphics the same way again."
- The mechanism Tufte names for that change: it happens "in part because" the reader is about to see collected masterworks — "so many wonderful drawings, those of Playfair, of Minard, of Marey, and, nowadays, of the computer" (p. 11). Studying exemplary graphics is itself a way to learn design, alongside theory.

## Introduction to the Second Edition (pp. 8–9)

What the second edition changes: high-resolution color reproductions of William Playfair's graphics, color added to other images where appropriate, and all corrections accumulated over 17 printings of the first edition (p. 8).

Origins and lessons embedded in the book's history (pp. 8–9):

- The book began in 1975 when Tufte taught statistics to journalists at Princeton. The existing literature on statistical graphics was thin — "too often grimly devoted to explaining use of the ruling pen and to promulgating 'graphic standards' indifferent to the nature of visual evidence and quantitative reasoning." Lesson: graphic standards divorced from visual evidence and quantitative reasoning are not design guidance.
- John Tukey, the Princeton statistician, had opened up the field since the mid-1960s; his technical contributions made it clear that the study of statistical graphics was "intellectually respectable and not just about pie charts and ruling pens."
- *Self-exemplifying design*: Tufte designed the book so that "the physical object itself would reflect the intellectual principles advanced in the book." When publishers balked at an author governing design (and at wide, affordable readership), he self-published, laying out the book page by page with designer Howard Gralla. Actionable principle: the artifact carrying your argument should itself follow your design principles.
- A concrete consequence of self-publishing: graphics were integrated right into the text, "sometimes into the middle of a sentence, eliminating the usual separation of text and image" — one of the ideas *Visual Display* advocates. Place graphics where the argument needs them, not in a segregated figures section.
- On standards of work: "My view on self-publishing was to go all out, to make the best and most elegant and wonderful book possible, without compromise. Otherwise, why do it?" (p. 9)
- The payoff he names: fresh eyes for "the intellectual and aesthetic joy of visual evidence, visual reasoning, and visual understanding" (p. 9). These three terms — visual evidence, visual reasoning, visual understanding — frame the book's whole subject.

## Actionable takeaways

1. Treat a graphic as an instrument for reasoning, not as decoration or a table substitute. Design it to support description, exploration, and summary of the data.
2. When facing a set of numbers — especially a large one — default to looking at pictures of those numbers.
3. Aim for designs that are simultaneously simple and powerful; the best graphics are both.
4. Improve graphics the way you improve prose: through sequential revision and editing, guided by maximizing principles and empirical measures of performance.
5. Integrate words, numbers, and pictures into one simultaneous presentation; do not separate text from image.
6. Apply these principles universally — they are not genre-, field-, or language-specific.
7. Distrust "graphic standards" that ignore the nature of visual evidence and quantitative reasoning.
8. Make the artifact self-exemplifying: the object presenting your principles should obey them.
