# Epilogue: Designs for the Display of Information — Detailed Account

## Placement, form, and scope

The Epilogue is the final chapter of *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information* and occupies a single printed page, page 191. It follows immediately after Chapter 9, "Aesthetics and Technique in Data Graphical Design" (which ends on p. 190 with the conclusions about shaping graphics roughly 50 percent wider than tall), and it is followed only by the Index (section title page p. 193, entries beginning p. 194), the illustration-permissions list, and the Acknowledgments. The Epilogue serves as the closing statement for both halves of the book — Part I ("Graphical Practice," covering graphical excellence and graphical integrity) and Part II ("Theory of Data Graphics," covering data-ink, chartjunk, data-ink maximization, multifunctioning elements, data density, and aesthetics).

The chapter's full title, set in large italic display type across the top of the page, is "Epilogue: Designs for the Display of Information" (p. 191). The title's scope is notable: it says "the Display of *Information*," wider than the book title's "quantitative information" — the closing statement is offered for information design generally, not only for statistical graphics. The whole chapter runs to about 145 words.

The Epilogue contains:

- No figures or graphics of any kind.
- No footnotes, sidenotes, or citations.
- No epigraph.
- No data, numbers, tables, or examples.
- Exactly two paragraphs of body text plus one set-off closing line.

It is pure statement of philosophy — the only chapter in the book consisting entirely of words about design, with nothing displayed.

## Full text of the chapter

Because the chapter is so short, its complete text can be given verbatim. The body text begins well below the title, in the upper-middle portion of an otherwise mostly blank page (p. 191):

> Design is choice. The theory of the visual display of quantitative information consists of principles that generate design options and that guide choices among options. The principles should not be applied rigidly or in a peevish spirit; they are not logically or mathematically certain; and it is better to violate any principle than to place graceless or inelegant marks on paper. Most principles of design should be greeted with some skepticism, for word authority can dominate our vision, and we may come to see only through the lenses of word authority rather than with our own eyes.
>
> What is to be sought in designs for the display of information is the clear portrayal of complexity. Not the complication of the simple; rather the task of the designer is to give visual access to the subtle and the difficult—that is,
>
> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;the revelation of the complex.

The final phrase, "the revelation of the complex.", is not run into the paragraph. It is dropped to its own line and indented toward the center of the measure, set apart typographically as the book's last words. The second paragraph deliberately breaks mid-sentence at "—that is," (the em dash set closed, with no surrounding spaces) so that the reader's eye must travel down and inward to reach the conclusion — a small enactment of the book's own teaching that placement on the page carries meaning. The remainder of page 191, roughly its lower two-thirds, is blank white space.

## Paragraph 1: Design is choice — the status of the book's principles

The first paragraph addresses a question the whole book raises: what kind of authority do its principles have? Tufte builds the answer in four moves, one per sentence.

**1. The opening axiom.** "Design is choice." (p. 191). Three words, the shortest sentence in the chapter, placed first. Design is defined not as rule-following, taste, or technique but as the act of choosing among alternatives.

**2. The theory's two functions.** "The theory of the visual display of quantitative information consists of principles that generate design options and that guide choices among options." (p. 191). Note that the sentence names the book itself — "the theory of the visual display of quantitative information" echoes the book's title — and assigns the theory exactly two jobs relative to choice: (a) *generative* — principles produce candidate designs (e.g., erasing experiments that yielded the dot-dash-plot, the range-frame, the white grid; small multiples; data-based grids); and (b) *selective* — principles guide which candidate to choose (e.g., maximize data-ink, within reason; maximize data density, within reason; avoid chartjunk; prefer wider-than-tall shapes). The theory is thus an option-generator and a choice-guide, not a decision procedure.

**3. The limits of the principles.** "The principles should not be applied rigidly or in a peevish spirit; they are not logically or mathematically certain; and it is better to violate any principle than to place graceless or inelegant marks on paper." (p. 191). This single sentence contains three distinct claims, joined by semicolons:

- A claim about *application*: rigid or peevish (small-minded, fault-finding) application is the wrong temperament for using the principles.
- A claim about *epistemic status*: the principles are empirical and aesthetic generalizations, "not logically or mathematically certain" — they do not have the force of proof. This concedes explicitly what Part II's hedges ("within reason," attached throughout to the data-ink and erasing principles, e.g., pp. 96, 100) had implied.
- A claim about *priority*: grace and elegance trump every individual principle. "It is better to violate any principle than to place graceless or inelegant marks on paper" — the word "any" makes no exception, including for the data-ink principles that organize the entire second half of the book. The supreme criterion is the quality of the marks on paper, judged visually.

**4. Skepticism toward word authority, including Tufte's own.** "Most principles of design should be greeted with some skepticism, for word authority can dominate our vision, and we may come to see only through the lenses of word authority rather than with our own eyes." (p. 191). The term of art here is **"word authority"**: verbal doctrine about design, as opposed to direct visual judgment. The danger Tufte names is perceptual, not merely intellectual — verbal rules can "dominate our vision," so that we literally "come to see only through the lenses of word authority rather than with our own eyes." Since Tufte's own book is itself word authority about visual matters, the sentence is reflexive: the reader is instructed to greet *this book's* principles with some skepticism too, and to keep visual judgment sovereign over verbal doctrine. This closes the loop with the book's empirical method throughout — claims demonstrated by showing redesigns side by side rather than by argument alone.

The paragraph's overall argument structure: define design as choice → position the book's theory as serving choice (generating and guiding) → bound the theory's force (not certain; subordinate to grace and elegance) → install a permanent safeguard (skepticism toward all verbal design doctrine, lest words colonize sight).

## Paragraph 2 and the closing line: the revelation of the complex

The second paragraph states the end that all the choosing serves.

**The positive goal.** "What is to be sought in designs for the display of information is the clear portrayal of complexity." (p. 191). The object of information design is complexity itself, portrayed clearly. This restates, as a closing maxim, the standard set at the start of the book — graphical excellence as "complex ideas communicated with clarity, precision, and efficiency" (p. 51 in Part I) — but now stripped to a single phrase.

**The rejected counterfeit and the true task.** "Not the complication of the simple; rather the task of the designer is to give visual access to the subtle and the difficult—that is, / the revelation of the complex." (p. 191). The sentence works by antithesis between two parallel "-ation" words: *complication* (of the simple) versus *revelation* (of the complex). (Both words are set in ordinary roman type on the page; the body text of the Epilogue contains no italics at all.) Complicating the simple is the counterfeit of good design — it is what chartjunk, over-decorated displays, and puffed-up presentations of thin data do (the targets of Chapters 4-6). The genuine task is its mirror image: taking material that is genuinely "subtle and difficult" and giving the viewer "visual access" to it. "Visual access" is the operative phrase — the designer does not simplify the complex away, but opens a way for the eye to reach it.

**The set-off ending.** The book's final words, "the revelation of the complex.", stand alone on an indented line (p. 191). Typographically isolated and centered toward the middle of the page, the phrase functions as the book's summary in five words: the criterion against which every display, and every principle in the book, is finally to be measured.

## How the Epilogue reframes the whole book

Read against the preceding nine chapters, the one-page Epilogue performs three closing functions (all from p. 191):

1. **It demotes the principles from laws to instruments.** Everything in Part II that was phrased imperatively — maximize the data-ink ratio, erase non-data-ink, erase redundant data-ink, avoid chartjunk, maximize data density, mobilize every graphical element, shape graphics toward the horizontal — is recast as a generator of options and a guide to choice, defeasible whenever following it would produce "graceless or inelegant marks on paper."

2. **It immunizes the reader against the book itself.** The "word authority" warning instructs readers not to substitute Tufte's text for their own eyes. The final court of appeal in graphic design is looking, not reciting.

3. **It names the single end.** All principles, all redesigns, all the historical exemplars (Minard, Playfair, Marey, Lambert, the data maps) serve one purpose: clear portrayal of complexity — visual access to the subtle and the difficult — the revelation of the complex.

The Epilogue thus ends the book where Part I began (excellence as clarity about complex ideas), but with the theory's self-understanding made explicit: a set of fallible, skeptically held principles in service of choice, subordinate to grace, elegance, and sight.
