# Chapter 7 Examples: Multifunctioning Graphical Elements

### The blot of the blot map (p. 139)

The opening model of a multifunctioning element. A single shaded blot on a statistical map simultaneously does three jobs: it locates the geographic unit on the two-dimensional surface, it describes the shape of that unit, and its color or shading intensity indicates the level of the variable being displayed. Tufte's point: that is a great deal of information for a small patch of ink, and crucially the different pieces of information are not confounded or mixed together. This is the standard a multifunctioning element should meet.

### Stem-and-leaf plot of 218 volcano heights (p. 140)

John Tukey's stem-and-leaf display, here showing the heights of 218 volcanoes in units of 100 feet (stems 0–19, e.g. "19|3 = 19,300 feet"). The digits themselves form both the data record and the shape of the distribution, so every mark carries meaning. Tufte quotes Tukey's design rationale: "If we are going to make a mark, it may as well be a meaningful one. The simplest — and most useful — meaningful mark is a digit." The lesson: data measures can be built out of the data itself, increasing quantitative detail and dimensionality.

### The living histogram of student heights (p. 140)

A photograph (from Brian L. Joiner, "Living Histograms," *International Statistical Review*, 1975) of college students physically arranged in columns by their own height, standing on a lawn. The people are simultaneously the data and the data measure, and the resulting human histogram clearly shows a bimodal distribution (women's and men's heights). The data literally form the data measure.

### Ayres' chart of American divisions arriving in France (p. 141; revisited p. 155)

Designed by Colonel Leonard P. Ayres for *The War with Germany* (1919), his statistical history of World War I, made entirely with typewriter and rule. Each month from June 1917 to October 1918 gets a column built by stacking the numerical designations of the American divisions then in France. The data measure triple-functions, showing (1) the number of divisions in France each month, (2) which particular divisions were there, and (3) the duration of each division's presence (its number repeats across the months it stayed). The cumulative design depends on the fact that no divisions returned home before October 1918. Tufte returns to it on p. 155 as the exemplar of multiple viewing angles: the profile of the rising horizon reads as a time-series, the vertical reads as the composition of each month's bar, and the horizontal reads as each division's length of stay. Every drop of ink serves three display functions, yet the graphic is not a puzzle because each function has its own clear line of sight.

### Chernoff faces scatterplot (p. 142)

A scatterplot whose plotted points are Chernoff faces (Herman Chernoff, 1973) — cartoon faces whose features encode additional variables. The faces reduce well, staying legible even at individual areas of 0.05 square inches. The analyst first reads the standard X-Y scatter, then turns to the within-scatter detail, seeking clusters of similar faces over the X-Y plane; outlying faces inconsistent with their neighbors are "strangers" and should be identified by observation number or name. Tufte notes that with cartoon faces and numbers as data measures we reach "the limit of graphical economy of presentation, imagination, and, let it be admitted, eccentricity."

### "Easter Wings" by George Herbert (p. 143)

A shaped poem by George Herbert (1593–1633) that uses space — the length of each line — to depict quantity, 150 years before Playfair. The lines double-function: long lines describe wealth, plenty, largesse, and rising to flight; short lines tell of poverty and becoming "most thinne"; intermediate lines indicate transition and change. The text is simultaneously the words and the data measure of their meaning.

### Youden's typographical normal curve (p. 143)

A typographical composition by the statistician W. J. Youden in which a statement praising the normal law of error ("THE NORMAL LAW OF ERROR STANDS OUT IN THE EXPERIENCE OF MANKIND...") is set in lines of varying length so the text block itself forms the bell shape of the normal distribution. The words about the curve double-function as a picture of the curve.

### The lane-stripe "graphical pun" (p. 144)

Redrawn from A. R. Lauer, "Psychological Factors in Effective Traffic Control Devices," *Traffic Quarterly* (1951). The chart shows how 48 U.S. states once differed in their engineering standards for painting lane stripes on road pavement, ordered by the length of the painted mark — from short dashes with long gaps (California, Missouri) down to unbroken solid lines (Illinois through Utah). What is portrayed is the actual physical pattern painted on the road: the visual data are the data measure itself, a graphical pun.

### Magnetohydrodynamic calculation grids (p. 145)

From K. V. Roberts and D. E. Potter, "Magnetohydrodynamic Calculations" (*Methods in Computational Physics*, 1970). Two panels in which the grid is formed by the locations of measurement instruments; plain dots register a zero reading, in contrast with white background where no readings were taken, and arrows show measured flow. Erasing this grid would erase measured data — the rare grid that reports directly on the data, unlike most grid dots, ticks, and lines.

### Galton's table-graphic of parent and child heights (p. 145)

From Karl Pearson's *Life, Letters and Labours of Francis Galton*: the famous cross-tabulation of mid-parents' heights against adult children's heights, with the correlation ellipse, axes, and loci drawn over the counts. The arrangement of the data yields an internal grid — a rare example of data as grid. (This is the table from which Galton developed regression and correlation.)

### The Mitchell map grid comparison, 1783 vs. 1975 (p. 146)

From Cappon, Petchenik, and Long, *Atlas of Early American History* (1976). John Mitchell made his famous map of North America in the mid-eighteenth century, and the original boundaries of the United States were marked on it in 1783 (the Treaty of Paris boundary line is shown). A grid was constructed on Mitchell's map so that each labeled square corresponds, square by square, to a regular rectangular grid on a modern Mercator map. The relative stretching, compressing, and twisting of the distorted grid reveals the deformation — from ignorance or error — that Mitchell built into his map. Tufte's point: "Here the grid is the element of interest, rather than the map." The grid itself carries the message.

### Converse's religion-and-politics election surfaces (p. 147)

From Philip E. Converse, "Religion and Politics: The 1960 Election" (in *Elections and the Political Order*, 1966). Two three-dimensional surfaces compare the effect of religion (Protestant vs. Catholic) on the Democratic percentage of the two-party presidential vote, controlling for party identification, in 1956 and in 1960 (when a Catholic, Kennedy, ran). The grid presents the data on the surface of the "rock"; on the sides the grid is conventional. Reading the slopes along the religion direction shows no reliable religion effect in 1956 once party is controlled, but a systematic effect in 1960; reading the slopes in the other direction shows the persistent effect of party in both elections. Two analytical questions, two viewing directions on one data-bearing grid.

### Playfair's chart of the National Debt of Britain (p. 148)

William Playfair's "skyrocketing debt" graphic, charting Britain's national debt from the 1688 Revolution to the end of the American war. Although the implicit plotting coordinates are based on regular intervals, the published vertical grid lines are irregularly spaced, keyed to significant events: accessions of monarchs, the beginnings and ends of wars. The grid is tied to the data. Tufte's verdict: "The data-based grid is a shrewd graphical device, serving rather than fighting with the data. It is a technique underused in contemporary graphical work."

### Range-frame with range-labels (p. 149)

A three-step redesign of the horizontal axis. Start with a conventionally labeled frame running 0 to 40; erase it to a range-frame, which leaves lonely ticks and round numbers out on the tails; then invest that same ink in data by replacing the round end-numbers with the actual minimum and maximum realized in the data (4 and 37). With its greater precision and two fewer tick marks of non-data-ink, the range-frame with range-labels is superior to the range-frame with round-number labels, and both improve on the standard, passive frame.

### Thermal conductivity of tungsten (pp. 149–150)

From Ho, Powell, and Liley, *Thermal Conductivity of the Elements* (1974): a dense chart of many studies' measurements of tungsten's thermal conductivity versus temperature, with a heavy "recommended" curve. Each circled number identifies a particular study, but the studies are ordered alphabetically by the first author's last name — so the circled numbers fail to double-function. Had the list been ordered by date of publication, the same codes would also indicate the time order of the determinations ("1" the earliest study; "61c" the third study published in 1961), letting readers see which early studies got the right answer and track the movement of studies toward the recommended values. "This extra information requires no additional ink."

### Redesign of the JASA style-sheet histogram (pp. 150–151)

The exemplar histogram from the style sheet of the *Journal of the American Statistical Association* — a probability distribution over integer values of ω — has its coordinate labels far from the data, forcing the eye back and forth between the data path and the margins. Tufte's two-step redesign eliminates this eye-work by turning the coordinate labels into data measures. First, the X-axis grid increments (0, 1, 2, ... 15) are relocated upward to mark the path of the data, each number sitting at its own bar height. Second, since the issue is the probability at each integer, the round-number Y-scale is replaced by the exact values (.177, .114, .075, .052, .034, .025, ...), each positioned at its data height. The resulting Y-column resembles the dashes of a dot-dash-plot, the data-positioned numbers serving as dashes indicating the marginal distribution.

### Dot-dash-plot with data-based marginal numbers (p. 152)

An enhancement of Chapter 6's dot-dash-plot: the exact data values themselves are printed in the margins at their positions (20.3, 15.2, 14.6, ... down the Y margin; 81, 123, 182, ... along the X margin). These marginal numbers eliminate the standard frame and even a range-frame, replace the coordinate ticks, show the marginal distribution of both variables, and record the exact values of the two measurements for each observation. Tufte's qualification: this arrangement performs better for smaller data sets (about 30 observations or fewer) and when a fine level of detail is required.

### Carol Moore's annual-report design (p. 152)

A striking design with data-based labels by Carol Moore of Corporate Annual Reports, Inc. (reproduced in Walter Herdeg, *Graphis/Diagrams*, 1976): white curves and labels on a black field, with the numbers placed at the data. Cited as proof that data-based labeling can also be visually elegant.

### The two-variable color map of cardiovascular mortality (p. 153)

A U.S. Census Bureau map spreading 16 color shades (formed by crossing two four-color grids) over 3,056 counties, encoding both male cardiovascular disease mortality and household crowding (percent of households with more than 1.01 persons per room). Tufte concedes it is a feat of technological virtuosity but condemns it as a *puzzle graphic*: viewers must repeatedly run verbal decoding phrases through their minds ("Now let's see, purple represents counties where there are both high levels of... what does that mean anyway?"). It is experienced verbally, not visually. By contrast, in a non-puzzle graphic the translation of visual to verbal is quickly learned, automatic, and implicit. Tufte caps the point with Paul Valéry: "Seeing is forgetting the name of the thing one sees." A footnote adds that viewer comprehension of these two-variable color maps was empirically tested by Wainer and Francolini, "An Empirical Inquiry Concerning Human Understanding of Two-Variable Color Maps," *American Statistician* (1980) (p. 153, footnote).

### Vauthier's mountain-to-the-sea coloring method (p. 154)

Quoted from H. Gray Funkhouser's history of statistical graphics: L. L. Vauthier's coloring scheme used white for the greatest intensity (a mountain summit's eternal snow), then green (forests down the slopes), yellow (grain of the plains), and blue (water at sea level) for the minimum. "Ingenious in idea but not very satisfactory in practice" — the scheme demands a mnemonic phrase *about* phrases, illustrating that colors lack a natural visual ordering and that imposed orderings turn graphics into verbal puzzles.

### The galaxies map in ten shades of gray (p. 154)

A map of galaxy density (the million-galaxy map seen earlier in the book) rendered in ten shades of gray. Because grays have a natural visual hierarchy from light to dark, the ten shades communicate varying quantities effectively where ten colors would have produced a puzzle. The success of gray over spectacular color is Tufte's lead into the key principle: clarity amid complexity comes from graphical methods that organize and order the flow of information to the eye.

### Census Bureau population density map of the United States (pp. 155–157)

"Population Distribution, Urban and Rural, in the United States: 1970," prepared by the Bureau of the Census — "a glory of modern cartography," reproduced as a two-page spread. Each dot, except in urban centers, represents 500 people; the map arrays some 400,000 points on its implicit grid. It demonstrates the three viewing depths: from a distance, the overall national structure; up close, fine detail such as the corridors connecting major urban complexes, the effects of landforms (California's central valley, Appalachia's valleys and ridges, clusters along rivers), and small towns strung along highways "like a string of pearls"; and implicitly, the underlying geography behind the dots.

### Slopegraph of government receipts as share of GDP (pp. 158–159)

A table-graphic of "Current Receipts of Government as a Percentage of Gross Domestic Product, 1970 and 1979" for 15 countries (Sweden at the top moving from 46.9 to 57.4, Japan at the bottom from 20.7 to 26.6). Read vertically, it ranks the countries in each year, with names spaced in proportion to the percentages. Read across, the paired lines show how each country's number changed over the decade. Read down the collection of lines, the slopes are compared, and lines of unusual slope stand out from the overall upward pattern (Britain's is the only decline, 40.7 to 39.0). The information is "both integrated and separated: integrated through its connected content, separated in that the eye follows several different and uncluttered paths." Tufte closes the chapter on this example: analyzing the *viewing architecture* of a graphic helps in creating and evaluating designs that organize complex information hierarchically.
