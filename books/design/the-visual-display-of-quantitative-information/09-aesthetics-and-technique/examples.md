# Chapter 9 Examples: Aesthetics and Technique in Data Graphical Design

### Minard's Carte Figurative of Hannibal's campaign (pp. 176–177)

Charles Joseph Minard, the French engineer famous for his map of Napoleon's Russian campaign, published a companion "Carte Figurative" (1869, reproduced from his portfolio *Tableaux Graphiques et Cartes Figuratives de M. Minard, 1845–1869*) portraying Hannibal's fading elephant campaign from Spain through Gaul into Northern Italy. A flow-line whose width shows the shrinking army traces the route; Minard renders it in a light transparent color so the underlying map type shows through. Tufte opens the chapter with it as a model of graphical elegance: a refined, restrained use of color carrying more information, contrasted with "the garish tones too often seen in modern graphics." It prompts the chapter's framing question — what accounts for the quality of work by Minard, Playfair, and Marey? — answered by "simplicity of design and complexity of data."

### Atlas of Israel multiple pie chart map (p. 178)

A map from the *Atlas of Israel* (Department of Surveys, Ministry of Labour, Jerusalem, 1956–) covers the country with dozens of pie charts of varying sizes and shaded wedges. Tufte uses it as the decisive case against pie charts: one pie is bad ("a table is nearly always better than a dumb pie chart"), but several pies are worse, because the viewer must compare quantities located in spatial disarray both within and between pies. The verdict: given their low data-density and failure to order numbers along a visual dimension, pie charts should never be used. He cites Bertin, who calls multiple pies "completely useless."

### Sentence vs text-table (p. 178)

Tufte shows a clumsy sentence — "Nearly 53 percent of the type A group did something or other compared to 46 percent of B and slightly more than 57 percent of C" — then rearranges the same content as a *text-table* with the numbers aligned (Group A 53%, Group B 46%, Group C 57%). The lesson: sentences prevent comparison among more than two numbers, while typographic arrangement creates an effective comparison dimension. He then reorders the rows by data value (B 46, A 53, C 57) to show there are nearly always better sequences than alphabetical.

### New York Times supertable: "How Different Groups Voted for President" (p. 179)

A 410-number table Tufte himself designed for the *New York Times* (November 9, 1980) showing how demographic groups voted in the 1980 presidential election, with a column comparing Carter–Ford results from 1976. Comparisons between elections read across each line; within-election analysis runs down clusters of three to seven lines; horizontal rules divide the data into topical paragraphs; rows are ordered to tell a story about the elections. This *supertable* shows that tables excel when a presentation requires many localized comparisons — "One supertable is far better than a hundred little bar charts."

### New York Times forecasting graphic: "Some Winners and Losers in the Forecasting Game" (p. 180)

A *New York Times* graphic (January 2, 1979) arraying eight economic forecasters' year-ahead predictions for indicators like real GNP growth, inflation, and unemployment against the actual 1978 results shown in a black center panel; forecasters are stacked above or below depending on whether they over- or under-predicted. Tufte uses it to show that for sets of highly labeled numbers, a wordy data graphic — coming close to straight text — works well; it is a table of numbers "nicely organized into a graphic."

### Biderman on the segregation of graphics (p. 181)

Tufte quotes Albert D. Biderman ("The Graph as a Victim of Adverse Discrimination and Segregation," *Information Design Journal*, 1980): the exigencies of typography pushed graphics to an "adjunctive, segregated, and marginal position" in printed scientific work, producing intellectual marginality and an organizational segregation in which graphics decisions passed from the original analyst to commercial artists and audio-visual shops — "cosmeticians and merchandisers" rather than scientific communicators. The point supports Tufte's principle of data/text integration.

### Leonardo da Vinci's manuscript pages (p. 182)

A facsimile page from Leonardo's *Treatise on Painting* (Codex Urbinas Latinus 1270) in which small diagrams of optics and mountain atmospherics sit directly inside the flowing handwritten text, the words wrapping around the figures. Tufte presents it as the ideal of text/figure integration — "a gentle but thorough integration of text and figure, a quality rarely seen in modern work" — proof that the segregation of graphics from prose is a modern production artifact, not a necessity.

### The friendly vs unfriendly graphic comparison table (p. 183)

Not a single graphic but a two-column inventory defining the *friendly data graphic*: spelled-out words vs abbreviations, left-to-right vs vertical lettering, labels on the data vs legends and obscure codings, curiosity-provoking vs chartjunk-repellent, color-blind-safe palettes (avoid red/green for essential contrasts; blue is distinguishable by most color-deficient viewers, who are 5–10 percent of the audience), modest serif upper-and-lower-case type vs clotted all-caps sans serif. Backed by a quotation from Josef Albers (*Interaction of Color*, 1963): differentiated letterforms read more easily; all capitals are the hardest to read; sans-serif in text reflects "neither historical nor practical competence."

### Stockhausen's musical score (p. 184)

A page from Karlheinz Stockhausen's score for "Zyklus für einen Schlagzeuger" (*Texte*, vol. 2, Cologne, 1964). Its notation mixes symbols, lines, and annotations of varied weights yet hangs together. Tufte uses it as a positive example of visual balance: when relative proportions of graphical elements are balanced, an integrated quality and appropriate visual linkage between elements results.

### Tufte's own seats-and-votes graph (p. 184)

A scatterplot from Tufte's own 1973 article ("The Relationship Between Seats and Votes in Two-Party Systems," *American Political Science Review*), plotting Democratic share of seats against Democratic share of the vote in 1968. He critiques his own work as the counterexample to the Stockhausen score: heavy-handed, with clotted ink, poor lettering style, a puffed-up display of a small data set, coarse texture, and a mismatch between the drawing and the surrounding text. The lesson is the symptom list of proportional imbalance — and that thick, mechanical drafting-pen linework is clumsy compared to the hair-thin engraved lines of eighteenth- and nineteenth-century graphics.

### Burgoyne Diller painting / Mondrian poster (p. 185)

A poster for the Yale University Art Gallery exhibition "Mondrian and Neo-Plasticism in America" (1979), based on a 1941 painting by Burgoyne Diller: black orthogonal lines of clearly differing weights crossing on a pale field with one red rectangle. Tufte pairs it with a redrawn version in which all lines have constant width — the character of the design is visibly diluted. Lesson: the orthogonal intersection of lines of different weights is an effective aesthetic device, and uniform line weight flattens a design.

### Time-series with weighted data line (p. 186)

A small step-chart in which the horizontal data segments are drawn heavy and the vertical connectors thin. It translates the Diller lesson into data graphics: the heavier line should be a data measure, because contrast in line weight should represent contrast in meaning — greater meaning gets greater weight. Tufte notes this is a restatement of data-ink maximization.

### Horizon analogy sketches (pp. 186–187)

Schematic drawings supporting the horizontal-shape argument: a wide low frame vs a tall narrow one ("lesser height," "greater length"); a horizontally stretched wiggly time-series; and a comparison of a solid-shaded, high-contrast mountain-silhouette time-series against the same data as an unanchored thin line (the "floating snake"). The eye is practiced at detecting deviations from the horizon, so horizontally stretched series read more easily; shading, when used, should be calm and free of moiré effects.

### Labeling and cause-effect sketches (p. 187)

Two more schematic pairs: a wide plotting field whose labels fit naturally left-to-right "instead of" a tall field whose labels stack awkwardly; and an axis sketch labeled "effect" (vertical) vs "cause" (horizontal). They give the second and third reasons for horizontal graphics — ease of writing and reading labels, and giving the causal variable the longer axis so its workings can be elaborated in more detail.

### Tukey's counsel (p. 188)

A quotation from John W. Tukey's *Exploratory Data Analysis* (1977): most diagnostic plots — definite dependences that bobble around, or point spatters — are better made wider than tall, since wider-than-tall shapes help the eye follow left to right; smoothly-changing curves can stand being taller than wide, but a wiggly curve needs to be wider than tall.

### Scatterplot of Playfair's 89 graphics (pp. 188–189)

Tufte plots the physical dimensions (height vs length in inches) of all 89 graphics from six books by William Playfair, each point marking a graphic's upper right-hand corner, with a diagonal separating taller-than-wide from wider-than-tall. 92 percent are wider than tall; several exceptions are Playfair's skyrocketing government-debt displays, where the data's soaring shape justified a vertical format. A second version of the plot (p. 189) overlays the Golden Rectangle diagonal: about two-thirds of Playfair's graphics fall between proportions 1.4 and 1.8, with most exceptions more horizontal than the golden ratio prescribes. The empirical lesson: a master practitioner gravitated toward moderately horizontal shapes, deviating when the data demanded it.

### The Golden Section and Birkhoff's rectangles (p. 189)

A diagram derives the Golden Section — divide a line so a/b = b/(a+b), giving b = (√5+1)/2 = 1.618 when a = 1 — and draws the 1.0 × 1.618 Golden Rectangle. Tufte calls it a "venerable (fifth-century B.C.) but dubious rule," then shows George D. Birkhoff's point (*Aesthetic Measure*, 1933): at least five rectangles, with ratios 1, 1.414, 1.618, 1.732, and 2, each have some simple mathematical property on which aesthetic claims could rest. So the Golden Rectangle's geometry is not unique justification for any single proportion.

### Psychological studies of rectangle preference (p. 190)

Since 1860 psychologists have tested viewers' preferences for rectangle proportions. Even granting the implausible assumption that such studies bear on graphic design, the findings are hardly decisive: a mild preference near the Golden Rectangle, but preferred height/length ratios spread from 1:1.2 to 1:2.2, and responses are highly context-dependent. This grounds the chapter's closing rule: follow the data's suggested shape if it has one; otherwise make graphics about 50 percent wider than tall.
