# Chapter 8 Examples: Data Density and Small Multiples

### The 80 × 80 grid and the eye's resolving power (p. 161)

A small printed square shows an 80 × 80 grid over one square inch (about 30 × 30 over a square centimeter), yielding 25,281 distinct locations by the formula (2n−1)². Together with engraved measurement scales (50 increments per inch) and the fine print of the U.S. *Statistical Abstract* (185 actual characters per square inch), it demonstrates that the eye easily handles hundreds of distinctions per inch. Lesson: graphical thinness is a design failure, not a perceptual limit.

### Steel Products table, U.S. Statistical Abstract (p. 161)

A dense table ("Steel Products — Net Shipments, by Market Classes: 1960 to 1978") from the U.S. Bureau of the Census, *Statistical Abstract of the United States: 1979*. Tufte uses it as the fine-print exemplar: ordinary government typesetting routinely puts roughly 185 readable characters in every square inch, far more information than most graphics carry.

### OMB Social Indicators bar chart — 0.15 numbers per square inch (pp. 162-163)

A full-page bar chart of adult education participation from the Executive Office of the President, Office of Management and Budget, *Social Indicators, 1973* (originally printed in five colors). Its data matrix has four entries — really just the names and numbers for the two bars on the right, since the left bar is merely the total of the other two. Spread over 26.5 square inches, the data density is 0.15 numbers per square inch (0.02 per square centimeter), "which is thin indeed." Tufte's specimen of an overwrought, near-empty graphic.

### JASA style-sheet exemplar histogram — 3.8 numbers per square inch (p. 164)

The model graphic in the style sheet of the *Journal of the American Statistical Association*: a histogram of average probability with a small data matrix of 32 entries, at a lightweight 3.8 numbers per square inch (0.6 per square centimeter). Even the statistics profession's own published exemplar is data-thin.

### New York City's weather for 1980 — 181 numbers per square inch (p. 164)

A reduced reproduction of the New York Times weather-history graphic for 1980 (the same design praised in chapter 1): daily high and low temperatures against normals, plus precipitation and humidity panels across the full year. Even shrunken it "does very well" at 181 numbers per square inch (28 per square centimeter), showing both that dense designs exist in journalism and that they survive shrinking.

### Annual sunshine record, with positive/negative redesign (p. 165)

A chart from F. J. Monkhouse and H. R. Wilkinson, *Maps and Diagrams* (London, third edition, 1971), recording sunshine through a year at roughly 1,000 numbers per square inch (160 per square centimeter). In the original, dark marks represent sunshine. Tufte reverses the image so the light areas are the times when the sun shines: now "the visual metaphor corresponds appropriately to the data." Lesson: at high density, also get the visual metaphor right — light should depict light.

### Bertin's map of the 30,000 communes of France (p. 166)

From Jacques Bertin, *Semiologie Graphique* (Paris, second edition, 1973, p. 152): a map showing the location and boundaries of all 30,000 communes of France in 27 square inches (175 square centimeters). Recreating its data would take at least 240,000 numbers (30,000 latitudes, 30,000 longitudes, about six numbers describing each commune's shape), so the data density is nearly 9,000 numbers per square inch (1,400 per square centimeter). Demonstrates the map-like upper reaches of graphical density.

### The new map of the galaxies — the density record (p. 166)

A map locating 2,275,328 encoded rectangles on a two-dimensional surface of 61 square inches (390 square centimeters). Each rectangle represents three numbers — two by its location, one by its shading — yielding a data density of 110,000 numbers per square inch (17,000 per square centimeter). "That is the current record."

### Survey of publication practices, circa 1979-1980 (pp. 167-168)

Tufte's own empirical study: at least 20 graphics sampled from each of 21 scientific and news publications, tabulating data density and data-matrix size (median, minimum, maximum). Nature tops the densities (median 48 numbers per square inch); Pravda is last (0.2). Findings: performance varies enormously, a few data-rich designs appear nearly everywhere, yet the average published graphic is thin — about 50 numbers at 10 per square inch. The Wall Street Journal, The Times (London), and Asahi match the Journal of the American Statistical Association in density; most American papers and magazines, along with Pravda, trail the major papers of other industrialized countries.

### U.S. Geological Survey topographic quadrangle (p. 168)

The average USGS topographic quadrangle, measuring 17 × 23 inches, is estimated (Morris M. Thompson, *Maps for America*, 1979) to contain over 100 million bits of information — about 250,000 per square inch (40,000 per square centimeter). Detailed maps generally carry 100,000-150,000 bits per square inch. Maps set the benchmark statistical graphics have yet to reach.

### Bertin's page of 17 small graphics (p. 169)

A page from *Semiologie Graphique* (p. 214) titled "Problemes graphiques posés par les chroniques," where Bertin's crisp and elegant line work allows 17 small-scale graphics to share a single page with extensive explanatory text. Tufte's demonstration of the Shrink Principle: graphics can be shrunk way down, often to half their published size, with virtually no loss in legibility or information.

### Los Angeles air pollution small multiples (p. 170)

Twenty-three hours of Los Angeles air pollution — the hourly average distribution of reactive hydrocarbon emissions — organized into a grid of identical map frames, drawn from a computer-generated video tape by Gregory J. McRae of the California Institute of Technology (model published in *Atmospheric Environment*, 1982). The chapter's defining small multiple: frames of a movie laid out on the page. "The design remains constant through all the frames, so that attention is devoted entirely to shifts in the data."

### Melanoma distribution on head and neck (p. 171)

From Arthur Wiskemann, "Zur Melanomentstehung durch chronische Lichteinwirkung," *Der Hautarzt* (1974): dot maps on outline drawings of human heads showing the sites of 269 primary melanomas, first overall, then separately for men and women. Tufte notes the "data graphical arithmetic, similar to that of the multiwindow plot" — the overall panel is the visible sum of the men's and women's panels. Small multiples can decompose a whole into parts the eye can add back together.

### Twelve samples of random normal deviates (p. 172)

From Edmond A. Murphy, "One Cause? Many Causes? The Argument from the Bimodal Distribution," *Journal of Chronic Diseases* (1964): twelve silhouette histograms, each built from a sample of 50 random normal deviates. The wildly varied shapes — some looking bimodal — all come from the same normal distribution. Small multiples here teach a statistical lesson: how much sampling error alone can vary the look of a distribution.

### Age composition of herring catches, 1908-1913 (p. 172)

From Johan Hjort, "Fluctuations in the Great Fisheries of Northern Europe" (1914): six stacked distributions showing the age composition of the herring catch each year from 1908 to 1913. A tremendous number of herring were spawned in 1904, and that year-class begins to dominate the 1908 catch as four-year-olds, the 1909 catch as five-year-olds, and so on — a single cohort's peak marching across the frames. Small multiples as narrative: the index variable (year) makes the story.

### Chromosomes of man and the great apes (pp. 172-173)

From Jorge J. Yunis and Om Prakash, "The Origin of Man: A Chromosomal Pictorial Legacy," *Science* (March 19, 1982): a full-page array comparing the banded chromosomes of (left to right) man, chimpanzee, gorilla, and orangutan for every chromosome 1-22 plus X and Y. The striking similarities between humans and the great apes read directly off the repeated structure. Small multiples handle a genuinely complex comparative data set.

### Consumer Reports frequency-of-repair records (p. 174)

Redrawn from *Consumer Reports* 47 (April 1982): the frequency-of-repair charts for automobiles built 1976-1981, where each car model gets a panel of circles (from open "much better than average" to filled "much worse than average") arrayed by model year and trouble spot (air conditioning, brakes, engine, rust, and so on), plus overall trouble and cost indexes. Tufte calls it "a particularly ingenious mix of table and graphic, portraying a complex set of comparisons between manufacturers, types of cars, year, and trouble spots" — a visually similar small multiple in semi-graphic form.
