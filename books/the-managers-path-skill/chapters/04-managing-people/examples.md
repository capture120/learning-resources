# Chapter 4: Managing People — Examples and Anecdotes

## Marc Hedlund on the new-manager mindset (guest epigraph)
**Situation:** The chapter opens with a contributed observation from Marc Hedlund about how engineers approach their first management role.
**What happened / what was done:** Hedlund notes that new engineering managers think of the job as a promotion that grants them seniority on engineering tasks and questions. That mindset reliably keeps them junior managers, and unsuccessful leaders at that.
**Lesson:** "New manager" is an entry-level job with no seniority on any front. Accepting that is hard but is the best mindset with which to start leading.

## Marc Hedlund on skipping 1-1s (guest epigraph)
**Situation:** A second contributed line from Marc Hedlund introduces the section on team communication.
**What happened / what was done:** Hedlund compares regular 1-1s to oil changes: skip them and plan to get stranded on the side of the highway at the worst possible time.
**Lesson:** 1-1s are preventive maintenance. The cost of skipping them shows up later, suddenly, and at the worst moment.

## The CTO who resented 1-1s
**Situation:** A friend of the author, a CTO with long management experience, sheepishly admitted he didn't like holding regular 1-1s, because he had always resented being forced into 1-1s with managers he felt he didn't need.
**What happened / what was done:** He summed up his view as: "Regular 1-1s are like going to a psychiatrist when you're fine and discovering you have depression." The author defers to his experience as valid for him — every person and team needs different things.
**Lesson:** A few very seasoned leaders can get away without scheduled 1-1s, but unless you're a CTO with years and years of management experience, start by assuming you need them.

## The shared spreadsheet 1-1 agenda
**Situation:** A manager the author knew ran to-do-list-style 1-1s and wanted them to stay meaningful rather than artificial.
**What happened / what was done:** He kept a shared Google spreadsheet with a running list of discussion topics that both he and each report could edit. Whenever a thought came up during the week, either person added it, and they reviewed the list during the 1-1. Each party could also see what was on the other's mind before the meeting and prepare.
**Lesson:** A shared, continuously updated agenda keeps structured 1-1s substantive and lets both sides arrive prepared.

## Jane and Sanjay: the micromanager
**Situation:** Jane, a manager, gave her tech lead Sanjay a big project due at the end of the month. The timeline was fine, but Jane worried the deadline would slip.
**What happened / what was done:** Jane started attending standups she normally skipped, questioned the team directly about their blockers, combed through project tickets leaving comments, and reassigned some tickets to other team members. When she discovered Sanjay and the product manager had deprioritized a feature, she took over the project and told Sanjay she would manage the day-to-day. The project shipped successfully — and Sanjay told Jane he no longer wanted to be a tech lead. His engagement and hard work disappeared; he left early and said nothing in meetings. Her best team member became a low performer seemingly overnight.
**Lesson:** Micromanagement can ship a project while destroying the person you were supposed to be growing. Never giving up control undermines the very people you need to develop and reward.

## Sharell and Beth: the effective delegator
**Situation:** Sharell, Jane's colleague, gave Beth her first big project to run, also with a hard ship date.
**What happened / what was done:** Instead of sitting in on every meeting and tracking every detail, Sharell worked with Beth to determine which meetings Beth should attend and which details to escalate to Sharell. Beth felt confident running the project and knew Sharell had her back. When things got stressful near the end, Beth enlisted Sharell's help to cut scope and ship on time. Beth came out more confident, ready for bigger projects, and motivated to work harder for Sharell.
**Lesson:** Delegation is not abdication. Make the goals and responsibilities clear, stay available for support and escalation, and the delegate grows instead of burning out. The contrast with Jane shows both managers tried to delegate a high-priority project to train a new leader; only the one who actually ceded control succeeded.

## Jack, who hid his struggles
**Situation:** Jack was having a hard time with a project but never asked for help, and his manager only heard about the struggles late.
**What happened / what was done:** The right move was to tell Jack he needed to be more proactive about sharing progress, even if that meant admitting he was struggling. The manager could demand daily updates as a way of helping, but only for a brief period — the goal isn't to punish poor communication with micromanagement (which burdens the manager and undermines Jack's accountability), but to teach Jack what to communicate, when, and how. The accompanying warning: if a struggling engineer or project gets treated as a massive failure of the individual or manager, people feel blamed and start hiding information until it's too late.
**Lesson:** Treat the open sharing of information, good or bad, neutrally or positively. Intentionally hiding important information is a failure; getting stuck or making a mistake is an opportunity for learning.

## The 45-hour thought experiment
**Situation:** Managers who can't let go of details end up working ever-longer hours as their responsibilities grow, even if their team doesn't quit on them first.
**What happened / what was done:** The author proposes a forcing exercise: limit yourself to 45 working hours this week. With only 45 hours, would you really spend five of them nitpicking a junior developer's code? Would you pore over a smoothly running project hunting for minor errors? Or would you direct attention to bigger problems and to the future?
**Lesson:** Artificial scarcity exposes which control habits are worth nothing. Your time is too valuable to waste, and your team deserves a manager who trusts them to do things on their own.

## The 360 review for an engineer with no reports
**Situation:** The 360 review model collects feedback from a person's manager, teammates, direct reports, and regular collaborators, plus a self-review — but what does that look like for an individual contributor?
**What happened / what was done:** An engineer with no direct reports might solicit reviews from two other engineers on her team, the new hire she was mentoring, and the product manager she works with.
**Lesson:** 360 feedback adapts to any role: the reviewers are whoever regularly experiences the person's work, not just the org chart above and below them.

## The lone "sloppy work" reviewer
**Situation:** While assembling a review, a manager finds that exactly one peer reviewer described the person's work as sloppy, while no one else did.
**What happened / what was done:** Before delivering that feedback, the manager must ask: is the work actually sloppy, or does this reviewer simply hold higher standards than the rest of the team? Scattershot feedback includes both reviewers reaching for something to say and reviewers with uniquely harsh impressions.
**Lesson:** Use judgment as the feedback filter. Share what's valuable for the person to hear, but don't blindly relay every grudge that arrives in the peer review packet.

## Credential bias in "potential" (Ask the CTO)
**Situation:** A reader asks whether there's a good way to identify potential and what it really means for someone to have it.
**What happened / what was done:** The author calls out the pattern of inferring potential from inborn traits or credentials: "He went to a great school, so he has high potential." "She is very articulate, so she has high potential." Or, most bluntly, "He is handsome and tall and male, so he has high potential." These biases lead managers to keep extending the benefit of the doubt long after the evidence says the potential is an illusion. The counter-rule: someone who has been at the company long enough to be observed and has never shown reasonable performance probably doesn't have potential there, no matter the school, eloquence, or height.
**Lesson:** Potential must be tied to actions and value produced. Real potential shows itself quickly — extra effort, insightful suggestions, helping in neglected areas — even when assigned work is slow-going.

## The misplaced designer and the firefighter (Ask the CTO)
**Situation:** Within the same potential discussion: what to do with someone showing real potential but mediocre performance in their current seat.
**What happened / what was done:** The suggested fix is moving the person to where the potential can be realized. A person with a strong visual design sense who struggles with the day-to-day grind of coding tickets may do better in a UI/UX role. A great firefighter who hates planning may be better suited to an operations-focused team.
**Lesson:** Slightly-below-average performance plus visible potential often signals a role mismatch, not a person problem.

## The finance VP promotion packets
**Situation:** One of the author's most important promotions happened in finance, where public titles are few (Associate, Vice President, Managing Director, Partner) and the VP promotion is a critical leap signaling a long-term career at the firm. It's a complex, once-a-year process run by senior managers.
**What happened / what was done:** Her manager walked her through the game twice. First, for her own VP promotion, he showed her all the materials they'd gather to support the case: shipped projects, yes, but also signs of leadership and work that pushed beyond her immediate team. Later she prepared the packet for one of her own reports, gathering all sorts of evidence — including a letter the candidate had received commending him for serving as the floor fire warden. Both promotions succeeded, in the author's view at least partly because her boss and mentor knew exactly how the game was played.
**Lesson:** Promotions are won by managers who understand their company's process and assemble a deliberate, broad case — including evidence of leadership well outside the project list.

## Famous BigCo's up-or-out levels
**Situation:** A hypothetical company illustrates how early-career promotion pressure works.
**What happened / what was done:** Famous BigCo hires engineers out of college at level E2 (E1 is reserved for interns) and has a policy that an engineer showing no sign of advancing past E2 after two years has no future at the company. The policy applies through levels E2-E4; at E5, you can stay forever. A manager with a team of E2s and E3s therefore needs to keep them promotable every couple of years — usually straightforward, since the process moves them forward as long as the manager doesn't stand in the way and makes sure they're learning to estimate work, deliver roughly on those estimates, and learn from mistakes.
**Lesson:** Know which of your levels are "up or out." For early-career engineers, your job is to keep the promotion pipeline unobstructed and the evidence accumulating: independent project completion, on-call participation, engagement in planning.

## Jane the underperformer and her believable excuses
**Situation:** An employee (a different Jane) has been on the team a few months. She seemed slow during onboarding, but got the benefit of the doubt — the codebase was messy and there was lots of business jargon to learn. At the six-month mark, the record shows very little achievement, and what she did deliver was very late, very buggy, or both.
**What happened / what was done:** On paper the response is simple: tell Jane she isn't meeting expectations and set a strict deliverable. But Jane has excuses, and some are believable: the onboarding was bad, her first month was interrupted by the company party, her manager was on vacation for a week, and she had no one to ask questions. It starts to sound like the problem is the manager and the team, not Jane at all.
**Lesson:** This trap is why you give feedback early and often and keep records of it. Late feedback meets a pile of plausible excuses. Both extremes fail: managers who dismiss all excuses lose employee after employee to a team that genuinely fails at onboarding and coaching, while managers who accept every excuse let problems fester until the team is furious at the inaction.

## The surprise improvement plan that backfired
**Situation:** A great engineer bowed out of a project — with his manager's approval — to focus elsewhere. Someone in the organization complained about it.
**What happened / what was done:** The manager, who hadn't been paying attention to the situation despite having approved the engineer's focus, caved to the pressure and put the engineer on a surprise performance improvement plan. The plan did nothing except poison whatever goodwill the engineer had toward the manager and the company. He easily achieved the plan's goals and resigned soon after anyway.
**Lesson:** Don't put anyone on an improvement plan whom you wouldn't be happy to lose. Smart employees read a formal plan as a signal that the organization isn't a good fit, and they leave as quickly as possible regardless of whether they pass it.

## Coaching out the stuck employee (Ask the CTO)
**Situation:** A manager writes in: an employee has been at the company a couple of years doing OK work, but doesn't seem promotable on this team. Whenever he asks what he needs to do to reach the next level, he's told — and then he returns to his comfort zone. No amount of nudging changes anything.
**What happened / what was done:** The author identifies this as a common case: the employee has topped out in the organization and is losing energy. The advice is to coach him out. Make the situation explicit: he has been told repeatedly what the next level looks like, he hasn't been able to show he can work at that level, and this team isn't the right place for him to grow his career — so if he wants to progress, he needs to move on. This is not firing. Give him the chance to find a job in another part of the organization or at another company, and when he goes, let him go happily and preserve goodwill.
**Lesson:** Some people will happily cruise at a level forever, and if both sides are satisfied, that's fine. But when someone wants progression they can't achieve on your team, you owe them that truth. Like former couples who break up because they don't see a shared future, former employees who needed a different team to shine can part as friends.
