# Chapter 8: The Big Leagues — Core Learnings

## What this chapter is about

This chapter covers senior engineering leadership: the CTO, VP of Engineering, and other executive-level technology roles. It explains what these jobs actually consist of, how to set technology strategy, how to handle priority changes and bad news, how to work with executive peers and nontechnical bosses, and how your behavior echoes through the organization once you are the most senior person in the room.

## What the job is

The day-to-day of a senior manager varies enormously by company; running a 70-person startup engineering org is not the same job as managing thousands at a Fortune 500. But technical senior leaders share something specific: a willingness to embrace and drive change, question how things work now and try different approaches when the current way isn't working, and — because technology evolves quickly — build organizations that can evolve to keep up and follow through on the changes they push. Being a change agent alone is not enough.

Your first job is to be a leader. The company looks to you for what to do, where to go, how to act, how to think, and what to value; you help set the tone for interactions. People join because they believe in you, the people you hired, and the mission you helped craft.

A senior leader must be able to:

- Make hard decisions without perfect information and face the consequences.
- Understand the current business landscape and see into its many potential futures, planning months and years ahead so that the organization is best suited to handle those potential futures and capture opportunities as they come along.
- Understand organizational structure and how it shapes the work of teams, putting in place management that strengthens structure rather than undermines it.
- Play politics productively to move the organization and business forward, working well with peers outside engineering and seeking out their perspectives in addressing issues with a wide scope.
- Disagree with a decision and still commit to delivering on it.
- Hold individuals and organizations accountable for their output.

### Grove's four categories of management tasks

From Andy Grove's *High Output Management*, the daily work breaks into:

1. **Information gathering and sharing.** Meetings, email, one-on-ones, gathering perspectives. The strong senior leader synthesizes large amounts of information quickly, identifies the critical pieces, and shares them with the right people in a form they can understand.
2. **Nudging.** Reminding people of their commitments by asking questions instead of giving orders. A leader of a large team cannot forcefully steer it; nudging keeps the organization on track.
3. **Decision making.** Taking conflicting perspectives and incomplete information and setting a direction, knowing a bad call hurts you and possibly the whole team. This is one of the most draining parts of the job.
4. **Role modeling.** Showing people the company's values, showing up for your commitments, and setting the best example even when you don't feel like it.

Whatever your title — CTO, VP, general manager, head of engineering — your days are shaped by those four tasks.

## Models for thinking about tech senior leadership

Senior titles are muddy. Fournier flags that her view of the CTO job is opinionated and applies especially to product-focused startups — not a universal model for all companies. Rather than define every variant, think in terms of common roles that one or several people can play — some people can play several parts, some only one or two, and some companies won't need every role filled at all. They break down at large companies, where you often look at them division by division:

- **R&D:** experimentation, research, new technology generation. May own technology strategy or exist purely to find new ideas.
- **Technology strategy / visionary:** technology strategy meets product development; often manages product as well. Focused on using technology to grow the business and predicting technology's evolution in the industry. Unlike R&D, guided by business and technology trends rather than research potential.
- **Organization:** guides structure and people; owns staffing plans and org structure, ensuring projects are staffed accordingly. Often paired with Execution.
- **Execution:** makes sure things actually get done. Aligns roadmaps, plans work, coordinates large efforts, prioritizes, breaks roadblocks, resolves conflicts, makes decisions to keep teams moving.
- **Face of technology, external:** participates in the sales cycle for B2B software companies; speaks at conferences; supports the engineering hiring brand.
- **Infrastructure and technical operations manager:** responsible for technology infrastructure and operations; may be cost-, security-, or scaling-focused depending on company stage.
- **Business executive:** focused first on the business itself; understands the industry and other business functions, balances internal development needs against growth needs, owns high-level project prioritization.

Common combinations observed in practice:

- Business executive + technology strategy + organization + execution → CTO or Head of Engineering (VP/SVP)
- R&D + technology strategy + external face → CTO, Chief Scientist, Chief Architect, sometimes Chief Product Officer (usually at a company selling a software product)
- Organization + execution + business executive → VP of Engineering, General Manager
- Infrastructure manager + organization + execution → CTO/CIO, possibly VP of Technical Operations
- Technology strategy + business executive + execution → Head of Product / Chief Product Officer, sometimes CTO
- R&D + business executive → CTO or Chief Scientist, cofounder
- Organization + execution → VP of Engineering, sometimes Chief of Staff

The CTO role in particular changes wildly by company, but most strong CTOs command a strategic function, whether business-focused, technology strategy, or both.

## What a VP of Engineering is

The VP of Engineering role varies just as much as the CTO role does, based on the needs of the organization — but with one obvious difference: the VP usually sits at the top of the management career ladder for engineers, an experienced manager of people, projects, teams, and departments. As companies grow, the VP role often shifts from organizationally focused to business strategy, with multiple VPs acting as mini-CTOs for divisions while directors absorb the organizational management.

A great VP of Engineering (in the single-VP company most people will encounter):

- Runs day-to-day operations with a solid handle on process and detail, tracking several in-flight initiatives at once. Often described as having a good "ground game": able to drop into the details and make things happen at a low level. When both a CTO and VP exist, the VP usually pushes execution while the CTO focuses on larger strategy and technology's position in the company.
- Aligns the development roadmap to the hiring plan, often runs the hiring process, and coaches the engineering management team, working with HR on training and development.
- Owns (often entirely) organizational strategy: helping teams set goals tied to business deliverables, staying closely aligned with product, ensuring the roadmap is realistic and that business goals translate into achievable engineering goals. Needs strong business and product instinct and a track record of delivering big projects, including negotiating deliverables.

The role is both big and detail-oriented, which is why it's hard to hire for from outside (though most companies must): the person has to quickly understand the organization and earn trust, but engineers are reluctant to trust people without technical credibility, and senior managers rarely want to endure a hard technical interview for a mostly organizational role.

People who excel here are capable engineers who care deeply about their teams and prefer staying out of the spotlight in favor of building high-performing organizations. They want happy teams but tie that happiness to a sense of accomplishment, represent team health to other senior leaders, cultivate a collaborative culture, are very comfortable identifying gaps in processes, and stay calm while managing highly complex, detailed work.

## What a CTO is

Start with what the CTO is **not**: it is not an engineering role, not the top of the technical ladder, not the natural progression of an engineering career, and the CTO is not necessarily the best engineer in the company. People who love coding, architecture, and deep technical design would mostly not enjoy it.

At a small company or startup, "senior management" often implies that your title is CTO — yet the CTO is one of the least well-defined roles in the technical world. The challenge in defining it is that actual CTOs manifest it in many different ways: technical cofounders, the best of the early engineers, people who started with the title or were promoted into it over time, former VPs of Engineering; some focus on the people and processes of engineering, hiring, and recruiting, others on technical architecture or the product roadmap; some are the company's face to the external technical world; some have no direct reports while others manage the entire engineering organization. A first-cut definition — "the technical leader at the company's current stage of evolution" — is unsatisfying because it misses the hardest part of the job.

The working definition: **the CTO is the strategic technical executive the company needs in its current stage of evolution.**

- *Strategic*: thinks long term and helps plan the future of the business and the elements that make it possible.
- *Executive*: makes that strategy real and operational by breaking down the problem and directing people to execute against it.

What a CTO actually does:

- Cares about and understands the business, and shapes business strategy through the lens of technology. Executive first, technologist second. Without a seat at the executive table and an understanding of the business challenges, the CTO cannot guide technology to solve them. This can mean identifying where technology can create new or bigger lines of business aligned with overall company strategy, or simply ensuring the technology is always evolving to anticipate and enable the potential futures of the business and product roadmap.
- Identifies where the biggest technical opportunities and risks for the business are and focuses on capitalizing on them. If a CTO is focused on recruiting, retention, process, and people management, it's because that is currently the most important thing for the technology team — in contrast to the "chief nerd" notion that the CTO should focus on purely technical issues.
- Keeps significant management responsibility and influence. Not necessarily day-to-day management, but putting people behind the problems they believe will impact the business, and protecting the technology team from becoming a pure execution arm for other executives' ideas.

### The trap of giving up management

When the team grows large and the CTO hires VPs, many CTOs give up all management responsibility, sometimes not even having the VPs report to them. It is incredibly difficult to maintain influence and effectiveness as an executive with no reporting power. A leader with no power over business strategy and no ability to allocate people is at the mercy of influence with other executives — at worst a figurehead. The CTO without management authority must get things done purely by influence; if managers won't actually give people and time to the areas the CTO believes matter, the CTO is effectively powerless. Giving up management means giving up the most important power you ever had over business strategy — and you effectively have nothing but your organizational goodwill and your own two hands.

Advice for aspiring CTOs: it is a business strategy job first and foremost, and also a management job. If you don't care about the business your company runs, and you're not willing to take ultimate responsibility for a large team effectively attacking that business, CTO is not the job for you.

### Ask the CTO: Where do I fit (CTO vs. VP of Engineering)?

There are many ways to do both jobs, but questions help you find your pull:

- Toward CTO: Might you cofound a company someday? Do you want to oversee technical architecture and the processes for evolving it? Are you willing to deeply understand the business side in order to ground that architecture in the company's growth? Willing to do external events, speaking, selling to customers, recruiting senior managers and engineers? Willing to manage and mentor senior individual contributors?
- Toward VP of Engineering: Do you enjoy managing people and making engineering processes efficient? Do you like a broad view of the team's work and a hand in prioritizing it? Fascinated by organizational structure? Good at partnering with product managers? Willing to trade depth in technology details for focus on overall team effectiveness? Would you rather sit in roadmap planning than an architecture review?

Many people have a mix. Practical routes: the fastest path to CTO is to be a technical cofounder, though that guarantees the job only as long as you and the startup grow together. The fastest path to VP of Engineering is to get management experience at a larger organization, then join a growing startup. And remember the advice: wanting to be a CTO or VP of Engineering is like wanting to be married — it's not just the title, it's the company and the people that matter.

## Changing priorities

Senior management priority changes can sometimes arrive without warning. Leaders removed from the day-to-day forget that teams have long priority lists mapped out weeks or months ago, so they expect change to happen immediately. The result is the dreaded question: "Why aren't you working on the top priority?"

How to handle it:

- **Check the communication chain first.** Do you know what the top priority is? Do your teams? Do the developers on those teams? Often the failure is simple: the priority wasn't communicated clearly and urgently down the management chain, and nobody explicitly went through in-flight work to kill or postpone things to make room. Saying something is top priority is one thing; making the actual schedule tradeoffs to get people moving is completely different.
- **Treat the question as a signal of misalignment.** People above you don't share your detailed picture of what teams are doing and why. You don't need to constantly feed minute details upward, but being taken to task over priorities means you and the CEO have a misaligned understanding of reality. If the team genuinely needs to finish current work (stabilizing an outage-prone system, the final push of a long project) before shifting, you must communicate that clearly.
- **Push both up and down.** If a big project should finish before new work slots in, gather details on its value, status, and expected timeline, and be realistic. If someone above you has changed focus urgently enough to have this conversation, expect to compromise: cut parts of current work short or move people off it. Teams generally dislike being pulled off work for an "executive whim," especially when they believe their current work matters.
- **Drive the direction change actively.** The more senior you are, the more the job is making the organization move in the direction it needs to go, including changing direction. Communicate the new direction clearly; ask teams for the list of projects the change will impact so you can communicate it upward (this also forces your managers to actually plan for the new initiative); ask the initiative's originator for its goals and look for ways to combine them with work already in flight.
- **Repeat yourself — at least three times.** Never underestimate how many times and how many ways something must be said before it sinks in. You'll tell your senior team, hold an all-hands, send email. Do a little communication planning: anticipate questions, prepare answers, be precise about what changes, and sell the change as a good thing.
- **The three-times rule runs upward too.** When you want your boss to act, expect to raise it three times before he listens; the first two times the issue may resolve itself, and the third signals something bigger must happen. You may find yourself doing the same to your team — letting a degree of sustained struggle surface before stepping in. Not a policy to adopt deliberately, but a pattern that tends to happen.
- **Bigger orgs change priorities slower.** If you work for a growing startup with a founder CEO, this slowness will frustrate him. Manage it by proactively keeping the CEO informed about what's happening and why, showing you understand his priorities and naming the concrete steps you're taking to meet them.

## Setting the strategy

Most people don't know where to begin with strategy at the senior level. Get coaching (from your CEO, an executive CTO coach), solicit input from executive peers, and use senior engineers to pressure-test detailed problems — don't do it alone. The working process:

1. **Do a lot of research.** Study the team, the current technology, and the company. Ask engineers where their pain points are. Ask executives where they expect growth to come from. Identify current and future scaling challenges, the engineering team's productivity bottlenecks, and how the technology landscape relevant to you might change.
2. **Combine your research and your ideas.** Sit alone with a whiteboard and map the systems and teams, slicing them across attributes (customer-facing vs. internal-operations, backend vs. frontend). Look for the unique insight your position gives you — for example, realizing your technology models most of the business's data, giving you visibility into how data flows and where the axes of evolution lie.
3. **Draft a strategy.** Turn the map into actionable ideas to improve operational efficiency, expand features, and grow the business. Force yourself to consider the structure of the business, the needs of internal and external customers, and possible future evolutions.
4. **Consider your audience's communication style.** A board often reads the slide deck before the meeting so the meeting can focus on details; a sparse, presentation-style deck wastes the effort. Make board materials dense and informative on paper. Also avoid the underdeveloped plan: all system and architecture detail, few forward-looking ideas beyond 6–12 months, and no connection to the business drivers critical to the team's success.

Good technology strategy for a product-focused company "enables the many potential futures of the business." It is not just a reactive document accounting for current problems; it anticipates and enables future growth. It is not about deciding the product's direction — it is about enabling the larger roadmap to play out successfully. It covers architecture *and* team structure, grounded in an understanding of the business.

The hardest part is getting started. The second hardest is getting comfortable making guesses about the future with highly imperfect information. That discomfort is the difference between leading reactively (planning around the known environment) and leading in a forward-thinking way. Once the architecture vision is clear, leading gets easier: you can show the engineering team where the technology platform is going, not just what the product roadmap looks like — and the architecture can drive technology org strategy, which can in turn shape company org strategy.

## Delivering bad news

Layoffs, disbanding a team, unpopular policy changes, painful roadmap shifts: as the senior leader you are the messenger. Communication is the skill, and there are clear dos and don'ts:

- **Don't blast an impersonal message to a large group.** Email and chat (especially channels with commenting) are the worst way to deliver bad news; misunderstandings and animosity bubble without you there to guide the message. Second worst: everyone in one room at once. It feels efficient and prevents leaks, but it's still impersonal, you can't read everyone's reaction, and one or two deeply unhappy people can rile up the whole team before the message sinks in.
- **Do talk to individuals as much as possible.** Identify who will react most strongly and tailor the news to them. Give people one-on-one space to react, ask questions, and hear it straight from you. Make clear, as needed, that these are the marching orders and you need people on board even if they don't love the changes. For a whole-organization announcement: brief your managers first, give them talking points, let them share with their teams, then bring the whole group together.
- **Don't force yourself to deliver a message you can't stand behind.** If you can't deliver the news without betraying strong disagreement, get help: another executive, HR, or a trusted lieutenant (depending on team size). Senior leaders must learn to maturely handle decisions they disagree with, but that doesn't mean going it alone.
- **Do be honest about the likely outcomes.** The more you can commit to the direction yourself, the easier delivery becomes. For layoffs: acknowledge the process isn't fun, and that everyone needs the company to survive. For a disbanded team: name the team's accomplishments, the changes that make this the right path, and the new opportunities for learning and growth. Forthrightness builds trust and makes people more likely to tolerate unhappy news.
- **Do think about how you would like to be told.** When you resigned from a job or moved teams, you probably told people face-to-face, maybe wrote a farewell letter or had a going-away gathering. It can be OK to celebrate sad changes, done with grace. Apply the same care to hard news for your team.

## Ask the CTO: Managing a nontechnical boss

Reporting to a nontechnical manager (often the CEO) can be a culture clash. Best practices:

- **Don't hide information behind jargon, and be careful with details.** Your boss is probably very smart but may not have the patience for technical jargon, and is very unlikely to want to hear a ton of details about nuanced technical decisions. Learn to distinguish information that's valuable to communicate from information that isn't.
- **Expect to run your own 1-1s; come prepared with a list of topics.** Busy executives are hard to pin down; don't waste the time you get. If 1-1 time keeps slipping, send the agenda in advance to remind your boss you need her attention — and stay on good terms with the executive assistant who manages her calendar.
- **Bring solutions, not problems to be solved.** CEOs generally don't want to hear how things are failing, about your disagreements with peers, or your management troubles. If your CEO doesn't want to hear problems, accept that you won't get management coaching from him and find it elsewhere. That said, don't shy away from delivering bad news.
- **Ask for advice.** It sounds contrary to bringing solutions, but nothing shows respect like asking for someone's advice. Your boss may not want to solve your problems but will likely give feedback happily when framed as advice.
- **Don't be afraid to repeat yourself.** If an important issue seems forgotten, raise it again. You may need several attempts before getting traction; three times is often the magic number.
- **Be supportive.** Always ask if there is more you can do to help. Show you are there to support your boss and the company.
- **Actively look for coaching and skill development elsewhere.** You no longer have a manager; you have a boss. In a first senior leadership role you still need skill development: get a coach, ask for training, and build a peer group outside the company.

## Senior peers in other functions

Senior leaders, more than any other group, must practice **first-team focus**: dedicated first to the business and its success, secondly to their departments (Patrick Lencioni's *The Five Dysfunctions of a Team* covers this). Senior leadership is often the first time your peers all operate very differently from you, and having few or no fellow engineers on your first team can feel isolating.

Working well with cross-functional peers:

- **They own their areas; you own yours.** Give respectful deference on a peer's turf. If you disagree with her management style or how she applies her skills in places that don't directly affect your team, treat it like a good friend dating someone you don't love: stay out of it unless asked, and approach any disagreement you do raise with kindness.
- **Peer deference is a skill many people build earlier.** Many of us learn to let a peer own her specialty earlier in our careers, working with senior designers, product managers, or other business team members. If you haven't learned how to let a peer own her specialty, now is the time.
- **Disagree in the right venue.** The place for disagreement is one-on-one or in leadership team meetings, where you hash out strategy differences, company challenges, and direction-setting. If the numbers don't make sense, ask the CFO there. Expect to defend your own technical decisions and roadmap in the same setting.
- **Trust intent, not just ability.** Beyond respecting peers' ownership and expertise, put aside the idea that they act irrationally or self-interestedly when they disagree with you. Absence of trust is the fundamental dysfunction; what's needed is trust that peers are doing their best for the organization, not manipulating or undermining you.
- **Expect cultural clash.** The values that make a great CTO differ from those of a great CFO, CMO, or VP of Operations. Common clashes: extremely analytical people vs. creatively or intuitively focused people; those who embrace agility, change, and some disorder vs. those who push long-term planning, deadlines, and budgets. Learn to understand and trust styles across the spectrum.
- **Drop the "smartest people in the room" reflex.** Tech culture tells engineers they're the smartest in the room; peers who aren't analytically driven are not stupid. Throwing jargon at people who aren't familiar with it — and don't need to be — makes *you* look stupid to *them*. Learn to communicate the complexity of your work so an intelligent nontechnical peer can understand.
- **Honor the cone of silence.** Disagreements inside the leadership team don't exist to the wider team. Once a decision is made, commit and present a united front to engineering and everyone else. Letting go when you don't get your way — especially when your objections feel unheard — is hard, and it will happen; Fournier admits struggling frequently with hiding her own disagreements with peers. At this level you must decide whether to fall in line or quit; the middle ground of openly disagreeing with peers only makes things worse for everyone.

## The Echo

As the senior-most person, you are watched more closely than ever. People focus their attention on you, seek your approval, and avoid your criticism. The mindset shift from "one of the team" to "the person in charge" is especially hard if you came up through the team and grew it yourself. Your first team is now your executive peers; your reporting structure is your second team.

Why and how to detach:

- **Detach socially, with regularity.** Have a drink at the happy hour, then leave the team to socialize. Closing down the bar with your whole organization tends to end badly for everyone. Heavy socializing with your team outside work hours is a thing of the past.
- **Avoid (real and perceived) favorites.** If you don't detach, you'll be accused of playing favorites — and with strong social ties to people who report up to you, you probably *will* play favorites. A team that believes the leader plays favorites becomes unhappy, and your job gets harder.
- **Your words now carry weight.** A throwaway comment from you can change people's whole focus. That's bad unless you're aware of it and use it deliberately. If you keep a "buddy" image, reports can't distinguish their buddy thinking out loud from their boss telling them to focus on something.
- **Be deliberate about where you spend time.** Your mere presence changes the tone and structure of meetings; you suck the oxygen out of a room. Dropping into a one-off meeting and brainstorming enthusiastically can accidentally redirect a project. It's frustrating that you can no longer be just another contributor of ideas to be evaluated and rejected — but you aren't that person anymore.
- **You shape culture through modeled behavior.** People learn the behaviors you model and copy them. If you yell, they learn yelling is OK. If you openly make mistakes and apologize, they learn mistakes are OK. If you always ask the same questions about projects, people start asking them themselves. If you visibly value certain roles, ambitious people pursue them. Use this power for good.
- **Don't rant to friends in your reporting team.** You'll be part of hard, stressful business decisions you can't discuss broadly. Venting to reports you consider friends undermines their confidence by sharing worries they can't do anything to mitigate. Transparency that was harmless or even helpful at lower levels of management becomes incredibly damaging to team stability at this level.
- **Detach without dehumanizing.** Not being "one of the team" doesn't mean caring less; care *more* about people as individuals, at least in small ways. Ask about families, hobbies, interests. As you detach it's easy to start treating people like cogs — and people can tell. They give less, take fewer risks, and stop pushing through hard circumstances when they feel no one cares about them personally. Even superficial-seeming connection reinforces that you care about them beyond their current work output, and it grounds you without attaching you too strongly to individuals. You'll have to make hard calls as an executive; your team deserves a leader who can be kind while making them.

You are a role model. What kind of leaders do you want to develop? What legacy do you want to leave?

## Ruling with fear, guiding with trust

A leader can be technical, charismatic, decisive, and effective — and still accidentally create a culture of fear by being short-tempered, visibly annoyed, and openly angry when things go wrong. People then fear blame and public criticism, take fewer risks, hide their mistakes, and stop engaging — a vicious cycle of distrust and more mistakes. If you want an independent team capable of setting its own direction and pushing itself, you need people to take risks.

Where fear culture comes from: placing high value on being correct and following the rules, a strong affinity for hierarchy-based leadership, and coming from environments where open conflict was tolerated or encouraged. Engineering culture has a high tolerance for open debate, so leaders from heavily engineering-focused backgrounds may feel comfortable aggressively sparring over issues — but when you're the leader the dynamic changes, and people who would have fought back against you as a peer now feel threatened.

### Correcting a culture of fear

- **Practice relatedness.** A marker of fear culture is treating people impersonally. Diving straight into the issue, the status update, the problem — with no small talk and no personal relationship — leaves people scared to take risks in front of someone they think will reject them if they fail. Risk-taking requires belonging and safety. Take a little time for small talk; get to know people as humans and let them know you.
- **Apologize.** When you screw up, apologize honestly and briefly: "I'm sorry, I should not have yelled at you and I have no excuse for my bad behavior." A short apology that takes responsibility is all that's needed; going too long turns it into an excuse or distraction. Apologizing shows you know your behavior affects others, and role-models that mistakes are OK but you apologize when you hurt people. It doesn't make you weaker — it makes the whole team stronger.
- **Get curious.** When you disagree with something, stop and ask why. Not every disagreement is an undermining of your authority. Seeking more information often reveals you were reacting to something you didn't understand. Attacking what you disagree with teaches people to evade, shut down, and hide information from you. Turning disagreement into honest questioning opens the team up, gets the most information out, and produces the best decisions.
- **Hold people accountable without making them "bad."** Accountability doesn't start with responsibility and end with consequences. Ask: How do you measure success? Does the team have the capabilities needed to succeed? Are you providing feedback along the way? Leaders forget these and hope a junior team will achieve something just from a clear goal, or believe an experienced team should never need feedback. When you've made a person or team out to be "bad" for failing, ask whether you held yourself accountable for setting them up for success. When everything is clear and you've done your best, accountability comes with far less character judgment because everyone can see what happened.

A caution: fear culture is common in technology and survives best when things are otherwise going well. If you're feared but respected, the company is growing, and the problems are interesting, you might get along OK for a time — but lose any of those elements and people with better options leave. Fear-but-respect is not enough when the team is frustrated by other things around them. Soften your rough edges, practice caring about your team as humans, and get curious. Building a culture of trust takes time, and the results are worth it.

## True North

A core but sometimes-overlooked role of senior leadership: the senior leader of a functional area sets the baseline of what excellence looks like in that function — "True North," the core principles a person in that role must keep in mind.

- For a product leader: users and their needs first, measure and experiment as much as possible, push back on projects that don't address the team's stated goals.
- For a CFO: look at the numbers, at costs and potential value; make the numbers work in the company's favor; prevent accidental overspending; know when the team risks going over budget.
- For a technical leader: things are ready to go into production. Agreed-upon policies for review, operational oversight, and testing have been honored. You won't put something into production you don't believe is ready for users. You're creating software and systems you're proud of.

View it through risk analysis. Risk analysis doesn't mean never taking risks; things generally considered "bad" can be acceptable under certain circumstances — a single point of failure, known bugs and issues, inability to tolerate high load, losing data, undertested code, slow performance. There are companies and situations where every one of those is an acceptable risk. True North means all of these issues are *carefully considered* before code goes to production. Exceptions to the rules don't mean forgetting the rules exist.

True North is an underlying pull — a guiding instinct leaders develop over time and strive to develop in their teams. When teams internalize it, they can be trusted to follow the guidelines independently, without much direction or nudging.

Each function's True North differs, creating natural organizational tension: product managers may care more about user experience than production support burden; finance may care more about infrastructure cost than availability risk. This tension is healthy — it forces the organization to reckon with all the risks, not just the ones your function cares about.

When you examine your role as a leader, looking at the ways you set True North helps you understand your strengths and areas of ownership. If you consider yourself a technical leader, part of your job is setting True North for large facets of the critical technology — not making every decision, but guiding the standards by which decisions are evaluated. You may delegate True North for some areas (Fournier delegated mobile and UI-specific development), but push your senior technical staff to articulate what the standards look like there.

True North leaders rely on accumulated wisdom to make fast decisions without time to dig into details. To become one, you *must* spend enough time early in your career honing those instincts: stay technical, follow through with projects, languages, or frameworks long enough to learn far more than their basics, and keep learning new things even when your day-to-day no longer involves writing code.

## Assessing your own experience

- At this level your coaching and mentoring likely come from outside the company — you no longer have a manager, you have a boss. Do you have a professional coach, provided by work or paid for yourself? It's a good investment even if your job doesn't pay for it: a coach gives guidance and direct feedback, and unlike your friends, she's paid to listen to you talk.
- Beyond a coach, how is your support network of peers outside the company? Do you know other senior managers at companies in your area? A peer group shows you what the job looks like elsewhere and gives you a place to share experiences and get advice.
- Do you particularly admire any technology senior managers? What do you admire about them? What could you be doing to be more like them, if anything?
- Think back to the last time you needed to change priorities for part or all of your team. What went well and what didn't? How did you communicate the change, and what was the reaction? If you did it again, what one thing would you do differently?
- How well do you understand where your business is going for the foreseeable future? Do you understand the technology strategy that will get you there? What are the critical areas of focus for the team — feature velocity, performance, technical innovation, hiring — that need to evolve to reach the company's goals? Where are the bottlenecks or opportunities for technology evolution to push the business forward?
- How is your relationship with the other members of the senior leadership team? Which relationships are good and which are bad? What could you do to improve the bad ones? How well do you understand their priorities, and how well do they understand yours?
- If your team were asked which executives you got along with and which you hated, could they answer without hesitation? When the CEO or leadership team makes a decision you disagree with, can you leave that disagreement behind and support the decision to the rest of the company?
- Are you behaving like a role model for your team? Would you be happy to learn people were emulating your behavior daily? When you sit in on meetings with your team, do you dominate the conversation or are you more interested in listening and observing?
- When was the last time you asked someone you don't talk to regularly about life outside work? The last time someone emailed in sick, did you take a minute to wish them a quick recovery?
- What are the fundamental principles you want your senior engineers to consider when they evaluate work and make decisions? Or, if you're focused more on organization than technology, what are the fundamental management principles you want your managers to follow when leading their teams?
