# Managing Managers — Examples and Anecdotes

## The email to the leadership coach
**Situation:** Fournier, newly responsible for managing managers, found herself overwhelmed and unsure how to scale herself.
**What happened / what was done:** She wrote her leadership coach asking how to manage managers without it consuming all her time: what processes would get appropriate communication out of them, how to help with problems she wasn't in the room to see — with unreliable witnesses — and why she was spending all her time two levels deep in people problems.
**Lesson:** At this level answers are even less at your fingertips than before. Information arrives through an extra layer of abstraction, and figuring out where to spend your time to maximize leverage is the central challenge of the role.

## Ask the CTO: the open-door policy that produced silence
**Situation:** A CTO told the team they had an open-door policy, even held office hours, yet nobody came, and problems kept surfacing that no one had raised.
**What happened / what was done:** The diagnosis: open-door policies basically never work. It takes an extremely brave engineer to risk telling the boss — let alone the boss's boss — about problems, and that assumes the engineer can articulate them. Even on teams the leader built personally, with high trust, some problems never get escalated; they show up later as departures, delays, and exploded failures. The risk compounds with distance, culminating in the classic clueless executive who relies on office hours instead of 1-1s and direct team contact, then wonders why retention and delivery suffer while skilled managers manage up and hide problems. The prescribed fix: proactively ferret out problems, keep 1-1s open for real conversation, and hold skip-level meetings.
**Lesson:** Passive availability is not information flow. Predicting problems is part of the senior manager's job, and being blindsided reflects on you.

## Skip-level lunches with whole teams
**Situation:** Fournier's organization grew too large for quarterly 1-1s with every individual.
**What happened / what was done:** She held skip-level lunches with entire teams a couple of times per quarter, buying lunch and talking about whatever was going on. People act differently in groups and rarely complained about their managers there, so many lunches were little more than chats about random technical matters. Still, she got a sense of where each team believed its focus needed to be, and answered detailed questions about company strategy, other areas' work, and upcoming projects.
**Lesson:** Group skip-levels trade individual coaching depth for efficient familiarity — and that familiarity later made people more willing to bring sensitive topics to her office hours.

## Skipping skip-levels with a team she used to manage
**Situation:** Fournier already had personal relationships and shared history with teams she had previously managed directly, so she felt she didn't need to do extra work to keep in touch.
**What happened / what was done:** She skipped the deliberate skip-level process with those teams. The approach worked for a short period, but as the teams slowly changed, the relationships changed — and, as Fournier warns generally, even unchanged team members won't always come to you with problems they have with their manager.
**Lesson:** Old closeness doesn't persist on its own. Maintain skip-level practices even with teams you know well.

## Three accountability scenarios: roadmap churn, the errant tech lead, firefighting
**Situation:** Three common situations where it's tempting to excuse a manager. First, a team feels unproductive with unstable systems and attrition while the product organization keeps changing goals and declaring urgent mandates. Second, a tech lead has gone down a rabbit hole redesigning a core system; the design doc is barely started and work piles up, but he insists the problem can't be rushed. Third, a manager inherited a team drowning in constantly breaking legacy systems and support requests from other teams, with a stalled migration roadmap.
**What happened / what was done:** In every case the answer is the same: yes, the manager is accountable. With roadmap churn, the manager should show product the damage, refocus on what matters, and escalate upward if that fails. With the rabbit-holed tech lead, the manager must pull him out, make the design process transparent, and bring in senior people from other teams as mentors or collaborators. With firefighting, the manager should build a plan for attacking the causes of the fires, triage the support burden, refuse some requests, or ask for more people. The manager's boss then supplies what the manager lacks: clout against product, senior partners, headcount approvals, support-load shifts.
**Lesson:** Mitigating circumstances don't dissolve accountability for team health and productivity. A good manager surfaces clear problems before they become raging fires; the senior manager helps find the solutions.

## Good manager, bad manager: Marcus and Maria
**Situation:** Two contrasting department heads. Marcus is everyone's best friend — endless 1-1s with everyone down to the newest junior hires, infinite listening time, promises to make every problem right. Maria is less loved: available on request but distant, brusque at times, no patience for office gossip.
**What happened / what was done:** Under Marcus, nothing actually changes — the colleague people complained about still got promoted, the product team still railroads engineering, the goals still make no sense, but he's so busy nobody can blame him. Under Maria, the roadmap shrank to fewer objectives that make sense, the difficult colleague apparently got feedback and started listening, meetings run better, the team is focused for the first time in ages — and she goes home at a reasonable hour. Marcus is a people pleaser: deeply averse to making people he cares about unhappy, he says yes to everything and burns himself out.
**Lesson:** Being beloved and available is not the same as being effective. The manager who makes hard calls and fixes problems beats the manager who absorbs them.

## The manager who jumped on every issue himself
**Situation:** A people-pleasing manager insisted on personally handling every single issue brought before the team — including tedious work like resolving data problems caused by product bugs — to shield the team from unpleasant work.
**What happened / what was done:** Because the manager didn't actually have focus for the task, issues were resolved slowly. Worse, the team lost transparency into the problems their customers faced, so they struggled to prioritize fixing the root causes.
**Lesson:** Shielding a team from unpleasant work makes that work drag on and reduces the team's ability to solve problems for good.

## Why people pleasers don't create safety
**Situation:** One might assume a people-pleasing manager creates a team that feels safe to be vulnerable and fail.
**What happened / what was done:** The opposite proves true. The manager's own fear of failure and rejection blocks healthy failure. The externally focused pleaser shuts down honest conversation through evasion and, when needed, emotional manipulation that rests on being the person everyone likes so much. The team pleaser promises unrealistic things, and the team ends up bitter toward the manager or the company for failing to meet the inflated expectations. The remedies: help the pleaser feel safe saying no, externalize roadmap decisions to strong partners or agile processes, use promotion criteria that sit outside the manager's discretion, and show the person the behavior and its costs while honoring the selfless values underneath it.
**Lesson:** Niceness driven by fear corrodes honesty. Structure and awareness, not blame, fix the people pleaser.

## The overworked new manager
**Situation:** A common pattern among first-time managers: coming in early, staying late, writing emails all weekend.
**What happened / what was done:** The overwork usually means the new manager never handed off her old responsibilities and is attempting two jobs at once. The fix is explicit: make clear you expect her to hand off some of her old work, and help her identify opportunities to do so.
**Lesson:** Constant overwork in a new manager is a diagnostic signal of failed delegation, not dedication. Many people never learn to let go of tasks without being told.

## The control freak revealed by skip-levels
**Situation:** Some new managers take to the job with gusto because they believe the title is the key to realizing authority — taskmasters who make all decisions and assign specific work.
**What happened / what was done:** A skip-level meeting with the more senior members of such a manager's team reveals their frustration at having no ability to make decisions themselves. Control freaks also fight with peers in product management and adjacent tech teams rather than collaborating, and often hide their activities from their own boss for fear of losing control — skipping 1-1s and evading questions about what the team is working on. Notably, this impulse often stays hidden during baby-step trials like mentoring or managing tiny teams, emerging only once the person feels they have the true authority of title.
**Lesson:** Managers who neglect the job are bad; managers who seize it as a license for control can be worse. Watch new managers closely and deliver strong corrective feedback within the first six months if needed.

## The warehouse-software expert at the logistics startup
**Situation:** A hypothetical hire: a manager with deep expertise building enterprise-scale warehouse software looks great on paper to run warehouse technology at a logistics startup that lacks that domain expertise.
**What happened / what was done:** The hire fails culturally if he's used to shipping software once every six months and working only with remote development teams uninvolved in product ideation, while the startup needs someone who works with teams that ship frequently, embraces modern development practices, and inspires creative product-centric engineers.
**Lesson:** Domain expertise is overvalued in manager hiring; cultural and process fit matter more. It's easier to gain industry knowledge than to retrain someone who can't work in your culture.

## The org drowning in junior engineers
**Situation:** A hypothetical trigger for hiring a manager from outside: the organization is struggling after hiring ten engineers, each with fewer than three years of experience. None of the existing engineers who might be qualified wants a management role, and none has much management experience anyway, so promoting one would require heavy training while everyone drowns in people problems.
**What happened / what was done:** This is the point where you decide to hire in a new manager to take over some of the team — and run into the reason people resist outside management hires: we're barely capable of determining whether an engineer writes good code in a team setting, and coding is at least a skill candidates can demonstrate. Management interviews need their own deliberate two-part process: skills first, then culture fit.
**Lesson:** Hire a manager from outside when the organization is drowning and no willing, qualified internal candidate exists — but treat the interview as a designed evaluation, not a leap of faith.

## Big-company and startup managers crossing cultures badly
**Situation:** Managers moving between companies with very different norms around hierarchy and autonomy.
**What happened / what was done:** Fournier saw managers from big companies who treated their peers well but treated underlings and lower-level staff as less than human, causing massive friction in the startup. She also saw startup managers, used to acting on whatever they thought most important, struggle in environments requiring sign-off from other parties. She notes the crossover can work — she herself moved from a big company to a startup — but the fit must be evaluated, not assumed.
**Lesson:** Hierarchy and sign-off norms are the most obvious cultural element to screen in a management hire; decision-making styles (servant-leadership versus marching orders, collaboration versus loudest-voice-wins) likewise make or break the fit.

## Growing startups importing seasoned managers
**Situation:** Growing startups frequently hire seasoned managers and executives to round out the inexperience of the existing team, knowingly using the hire to change the culture of an area.
**What happened / what was done:** Sometimes this works incredibly well; sometimes it's a massive failure. Either way, attrition reliably happens around these bearers of a new and different culture.
**Lesson:** Hiring a manager to hasten culture change is a legitimate tactic, but expect attrition around the change agent and proceed with caution.

## Andy Grove on cultural values as decision-making (High Output Management)
**Situation:** Cited insight from Andy Grove's book explaining why culture fit predicts a new manager's success.
**What happened / what was done:** Grove describes cultural values as the mechanism people use to make decisions in highly complex, uncertain, or ambiguous circumstances where group interest must outrank self-interest. His observation: most new hires act in self-interest until they get to know their colleagues, and only then shift to group interest. A new hire dropped into a highly complex, uncertain job therefore tends to fail unless they quickly settle into the cultural norms and use the company's values to align their decisions.
**Lesson:** Screen for managers who already gravitate toward your company's existing values; they make the shift to group-interest decision-making fastest.

## Ask the CTO: inheriting operations and QA
**Situation:** A division leader who had only ever managed software development teams became responsible for operations and QA teams as well.
**What happened / what was done:** The warning: this is not a small leap. Each function requires tracking different details, and without experience you don't know which to watch, so problems surface too late — especially on long-timeline projects where lack of progress hides easily, and especially when you've hired a manager over work you don't deeply understand, who can go the wrong way for a long time unnoticed. The advice: adopt the curiosity of a mentee. Ask the manager to teach you her work as if she were your mentor, whether it's QA, design, product management, or technical operations, making clear the goal is to appreciate what she does. Then deliberately devote significant time to the new areas, especially at first, resisting the pull toward comfortable territory. If you treat the unfamiliar areas as uninteresting, guilt over ignoring them plus natural aversion will keep you from facing their problems even when people clearly flag them.
**Lesson:** Trust-and-delegate without understanding misses problems for far too long. Grit your teeth, learn each area, and practice asking for details until you can sense what the team actually does.

## Debugging the team that feels slow
**Situation:** A worked example: business partners and the product manager complain a team is slow, and the team visibly lacks the energy of its peers.
**What happened / what was done:** The investigation follows a debugging discipline. Form a hypothesis about how the team got into this state, probing as minimally invasively as possible — team problems resemble performance issues more than hard failures. Check the data the way you'd read logfiles: chats, emails, tickets, code reviews, check-ins, calendars; look for time-eating production incidents, sickness, code-review bickering over style, vague or mis-sized tickets, purely transactional chat, meeting overload, missing 1-1s. Observe the team by sitting in their meetings: boredom, one voice dominating, over-scripted agendas, and conflict being shut down all signal trouble. Ask the team what their goals are and why; if they can't answer, their leaders aren't connecting them to purpose, and a team doing only engineering-sponsored projects probably doesn't understand the value of the product work it's neglecting. Check team dynamics: people who never talk to each other and work only on independent projects aren't really a team. Finally, jump in to help the struggling manager rather than standing back — it teaches the manager and can expose foundational problems, such as missing senior business leadership, that no manager could fix alone.
**Lesson:** Treat organizational dysfunction with the same rigor as a systems outage: hypothesis, data, observation, questions — and a relentless pursuit of why.

## The poor debugger's log statement
**Situation:** An analogy from engineering: a developer adds a log statement to concurrent code while hunting an error, finds the error no longer reproduces, and assumes it's fixed.
**What happened / what was done:** That's a lazy habit — a sign of a poor debugger who is satisfied with the unknown. The great debugger relentlessly pursues why, digging through layers of code, logfiles, and system settings even for problems that happened only once. Fournier concedes that obsessively debugging one-off issues is not always a great use of time — but it shows a mind that won't be satisfied with the unknown, especially an unknown that might page you at two in the morning.
**Lesson:** The same relentlessness separates great engineering managers from lucky ones: managers who don't dig for root causes rely on charm and luck and never learn from their mistakes.

## Schrödinger's cat in the standup
**Situation:** A manager investigating a struggling team starts sitting in its meetings and watching its standups.
**What happened / what was done:** The observation itself changes the team's behavior — like Schrödinger's box, where the act of observing causes an outcome — and may hide the very problem being hunted, the same way adding a log statement can make a concurrency bug magically disappear for a while.
**Lesson:** You cannot observe a team without changing it. Factor your own presence into what you see.

## Being asked "why is it taking so long?" when nothing is late
**Situation:** Engineering managers regularly get asked why something is taking so long — sometimes when the project is genuinely over plan, but often when nothing has gone wrong, because leadership disliked the original estimate or never asked for one.
**What happened / what was done:** The defense is offense: aggressively share estimates and updates even when no one asks, especially for critical or multi-week projects. When the question comes anyway despite clear communication and an on-schedule project, it usually means someone is stressed or being pushed to deliver faster than was ever promised. Patient reminders that things are on track, empathy for the person under pressure, and offers to help in other ways shift the focus from blame to action.
**Lesson:** Blame under stress is not a rational exercise. Proactive estimate communication prevents most of it; empathy handles the rest.

## Cutting scope at the deadline
**Situation:** A project approaches an important deadline with too much left to build.
**What happened / what was done:** The senior manager works with managers, tech leads, and the business to cut scope, playing tiebreaker on which features are essential versus cuttable, and taking the fall for cutting someone's favorite idea when the larger project requires it. The trap to avoid: giving up only technical quality, which just slows the team down after launch.
**Lesson:** Cut product features alongside technical nice-to-haves, and absorb the political cost of the cuts yourself.

## The VP who wanted search improved faster
**Situation:** A VP wants the search experience improved to grow active users by next quarter and offers more engineers to speed the work.
**What happened / what was done:** The director knows the search system is mid-rewrite, so added engineers can't usefully modify it. Instead she directs the team to prioritize exposing the new API earlier, letting the product team finally run the tests they'd been asking for, and explains to the VP what is and isn't possible while keeping the team focused on work that makes the higher-level goal achievable.
**Lesson:** Staying technically current lets a senior manager translate business asks into feasible specific requests instead of acting as a mere go-between relaying messages up and down — which is not a value-add role.
