## Introduction

The chapter opens with one of the most memorable quotes from Arthur Miller's 1949 play *Death of a Salesman*: Uncle Ben's description of his path to wealth — "When I was seventeen, I walked into the jungle, and when I was twenty-one I walked out. And by God I was rich."

Two decades in, he is still learning more from each project he works on. The book has aimed to accelerate the reader's learning path, but his experience is that there remains a great deal left to learn, despite what the book has hoped to accomplish.

Because the reader is already familiar with his general suggestions on creating strategy, this chapter provides focused advice on creating your own strategy to get better at strategy. It covers four things:

1. Where to find strategies you can learn from.
2. How to diagnose the strategies you've found to ensure you learn the right lessons.
3. How to perform and practice strategy within your organization, whether or not you have organizational authority.
4. Operational mechanisms to hold yourself accountable for developing a strategy practice.

With that preamble, the chapter sets out to write the book's final strategy: your personal strategy for developing your strategy practice. (Note the structure of the chapter itself mirrors the book's framework: exploration, diagnosis, policy, operations.)

## Exploring Strategy Creation

Ideally, you could begin improving your engineering strategy skills by reading publicly available examples to learn from others' experience. Unfortunately, there simply aren't many easily available. The three most useful places to look are:

1. **Public resources** on engineering strategy, such as companies' engineering blogs.
2. **Private and undocumented strategies** available through your professional network.
3. **Learning communities** that you build with others, including ongoing learning circles.

Each of these is explored in its own subsection below.

### Public Resources

- The book's Appendix collects a number of public engineering strategy resources, including some blog posts that are adjacent to the topic.
- You can go a long way by searching and prompting your way into these resources, including the LLM companion created for this book.
- Warning: as you read public strategies, it's important to recognize that they are often misleading, as discussed in Chapter 23. Everyone writing in public has an agenda, and that agenda often means they'll omit important details to make themselves, or their company, come off well.
- Decision rule: make sure you read between the lines rather than taking public strategies too literally.

### Private Resources

- Ironically, Larson has found it much easier to find privately held strategy resources than public ones.
- While private recollections are still prone to inaccuracies, the incentives to massage the truth are less pronounced in private than in public.
- Strategies are often **oral histories**, and they are shared freely among peers both within and across companies. As you build your professional network, you can usually get access to a company's engineering strategy on any topic by just asking.
- There are brief exceptions to this openness: even a close peer won't share a sensitive strategy before its existence becomes externally obvious — but they'll be glad to share it after it does.
- People tend to overestimate how much information companies can keep private anyway. Even reading a company's recent job postings can expose a surprising amount about that company.
- Surprisingly few organizations formally collect their strategies into a repository, but their tenured members tend to collect the stories informally. These people are the company's **strategy archaeologists**, and you can learn a great deal by consulting them.
- You can also become a strategy archaeologist yourself, whether or not you're a tenured member of your company:
  - You can learn a tremendous amount by starting to build your own strategy repository.
  - As you start collecting strategies, you'll interest others in contributing theirs as well.
  - Over time, you can foster a culture of documentation where one didn't exist before.
  - Even better, building that culture doesn't require any explicit authority — "just an ongoing show of excitement."
- Other private sources exist as well, such as:
  - Attending the "hallway track" at conferences.
  - Organizing dinners where people share stories with a commitment to privacy.

### Working in Community

Larson's final suggestion for seeing how others work on strategy is to form a **learning circle**. He did this when he first moved into an executive role, and he has now been running it for more than five years. What surprised him the most is how much he has learned from it.

There are four reasons why ongoing learning circles are exceptional for sharing strategy:

1. **Bidirectional discussion** allows much more learning and understanding than unidirectional forms of communication, like conference talks and documents.
2. **Groups** allow you to learn from others' experiences and questions, rather than having to guide all of the learning yourself.
3. **Continuity** allows you to see a strategy at its inception, during the rollout, and after it's been in practice for some time.
4. **Trust is built slowly**, and you'll only get the full details about a problem when you've already successfully held trust about smaller things. An ongoing group makes this sort of sharing feasible, whereas a transient group does not.

Trade-off: although putting one of these communities together requires commitment, they are the best mechanism Larson has found for learning how others work on strategy.

Many people get stuck on the question of how they can get invited to an existing learning circle, but that's almost always the wrong question to ask. If you want to join a learning circle, **make one**. That's how Larson got invited to his.

## Diagnosing Your Prior and Current Strategy Work

Collecting strategies to learn from is a valuable part of improving, but it's only the first step. You also have to determine what to take away from each strategy. For example, you have to determine whether Calm's approach to resourcing Engineering-driven projects (Document 21-2, earlier in the book) is something to copy or something to avoid.

Method:

- Apply the strategy rubric from Chapter 23 to each of the strategies you've collected.
- Even just by splitting a strategy into its various phases, you'll learn a lot.
- Applying the rubric to each phase will teach you more.
- Each time you do this to another strategy, you'll get a bit faster at applying the rubric, and you'll start to see interesting, recurring patterns.

As you split a strategy into phases, apply the evaluation rubric, and dig in, here is the handful of questions to ask:

1. How long did it take to determine that this strategy's initial phase could be improved? How high was the cost to fund that initial phase's discovery?
2. Why did the strategy reach its final stage and get repealed or replaced? How long did it take to get there?
3. If you had to pick only one, did this strategy fail in its approach to exploration, diagnosis, policy, or operations?
4. To what extent did this strategy outlive the tenure of its primary author? Did it get repealed quickly after their departure, did it endure, or was it perhaps replaced during their tenure?
5. Would you generally repeat this strategy, or would you strive to avoid repeating it? If you did repeat it, what conditions seem necessary to make it succeed?
6. How might you apply this strategy to your current opportunities and challenges?

Decision rule: it's not necessary to work through all of these questions for every strategy you're learning from. Pick the two that seem most interesting for a given strategy.

## Policy for Improving at Strategy

For improving your strategic abilities at a high level, there are two key policies to consider:

1. **Implementing strategy.**
2. **Practicing implementing strategy.**

As for more detailed options, here are some worth considering — each framed as an if-then conditional:

- **If your company's existing strategies are not working:** debug one and work to fix it. If you lack the authority to work at the company scope, decrease altitude until you find an altitude you can work at. Perhaps setting Engineering organizational strategies is beyond your circumstances, but strategy for your team is entirely accessible.
- **If your company has no documented strategies:** document one to make it debuggable. Again, if operating at a high altitude isn't attainable for some reason, operate at a lower altitude that is within reach.
- **If your company or team has an effective strategy with low adoption:** see if you can iterate on operational mechanisms to increase adoption. Many such mechanisms require no authority at all, such as low-noise nudges or the model-document-share approach.
- **If existing strategies are effective and have high adoption:** see if you can build excitement for a new strategy. Start by asking which problems Staff-plus engineers and senior managers believe are important. Once you find one, you have a valuable strategy vein to start mining.
- **If you don't feel comfortable sharing your work internally within the company:** try sharing your proposals with just a few trusted peers, perhaps within a learning circle that you create or join. You can even go further and only share proposals with trusted external peers.

Operating rules for these options:

- Trying all of these at once would be overwhelming, so pick one in any given phase.
- If you can't gain traction, try another approach until something works.
- It's particularly important to recognize in your diagnosis *where* things are not working. Perhaps you simply don't have the sponsorship you need to enforce strategy, so you need to switch toward suggesting strategies instead. Once you acknowledge this, you'll find something that works.

### What If You're Not Allowed to Do Strategy?

If you're looking to find a reason why you can't do strategy in your current environment, you'll always unearth one, as seen in Chapter 3. "Don't believe the hype: you can always do strategy work."

If you believe your current role prevents you from engaging in strategy work, Larson has found two useful approaches:

1. **Lower your altitude.** There's always a scale where you can perform strategy, even if it's just your team, or even just yourself. "Only you can forbid yourself from developing personal strategies."
2. **Practice strategy work rather than performing it.** Organizations can only absorb so much strategy development at a given time, so sometimes they won't be open to you doing more. But only you can stop yourself from practicing.

## Operating Your Strategy Improvement Policies

As the book's refrain goes, even the best policies don't accomplish much if they aren't paired with operational mechanisms to ensure the policies actually happen and, if they don't, to debug why. It's tempting to overlook operations in discussing personal habits, but that would be a mistake: our habits profoundly impact us in the long term, yet they're easiest to neglect because others rarely inquire about them.

Recommended mechanisms:

1. **Track.** Clearly track the strategies you've implemented, refined, documented, or read. Maintain a document, spreadsheet, or folder that makes it easy to monitor your progress.
2. **Review quarterly.** Review your tracked strategies every quarter: are you working on the expected number and in the expected way? If not, why not? Ideally, review them in community with a peer or a learning circle. The reason: "It's too easy to deceive yourself, but much harder to trick someone else."
3. **Debug with a trusted person.** If your periodic review shows that you're simply not doing the work you expected to do, sit down for an hour with someone that you trust — ideally someone equally or more experienced than you — and debug what's going wrong. Commit to doing this before your next periodic review.

Tracking your personal habits can feel odd, but it's worthwhile. Larson has been setting and tracking personal goals for some time — for example, as discussed in his 2024 year in review — and has benefited greatly from it.

### Too Busy for Strategy

Many companies convince themselves that they're in too much of a rush to make good decisions. Larson admits he has certainly gotten stuck in this view at times himself, although at this point in his career he recognizes that he has the tools to create time for strategy, and an obligation to do strategy rather than inflicting poor decisions on the organizations he works in.

His advice for creating time:

1. If you're not tracking how often you're creating strategies, start there.
2. If you haven't worked on a single strategy in the past six months, then start with one.
3. If implementing a strategy has been prohibitively time-consuming, focus on practicing a strategy instead.

If you try all those things and still aren't making progress, then accept your reality: you don't view doing strategy as particularly important. Spend some time thinking about why. If you're comfortable with your answer, then maybe this is a practice you should come back to later.
