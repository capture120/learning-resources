# Chapter 7: Diagnosis

This chapter explains the diagnosis step of strategy writing: understanding the constraints and challenges your strategy must address before deciding how to address them. The single most important takeaway: every failed strategy Larson has seen failed because of a lazy or inaccurate diagnosis — "It's very challenging to fail once you have a proper diagnosis, and it's almost impossible to succeed without one." Diagnosis is the foundation; done well, the later steps of writing strategy often feel like an afterthought. Precisely because it's the foundation, Larson finds it the most intimidating phase of strategy work — a somewhat unavoidable reality.

## Core ideas

### Diagnosis is strategy's foundation

Diagnosis is the attempt to correctly recognize the problem the strategy needs to solve *before* deciding on the policies to address it. Its central discipline is slowing yourself down from jumping to solutions before fully understanding the nuances and constraints of the problem.

Why this matters: after the fact, many effective strategies are so obvious they're boring, and most ineffective ones are so clearly flawed their authors look lazy, because operating a strategy reveals the reality around it. While writing, you don't yet know that reality (e.g., whether colleagues will actually adopt a new API-specification approach). Diagnosis is how you recognize reality up front instead of discovering it through failure.

### Diagnosis is evaluation; exploration is not

Where exploration (the prior step) was an evaluation-free activity, diagnosis is all about evaluation: How do teams feel today? Why did that project fail? Why did the last strategy go poorly? What distractions must be overcome?

### A strong diagnosis is a web of evidence, not a stated judgment

Not all evaluation is equal. A directly stated judgment is easy to dispute. An effective diagnosis is hard to argue against because it is "a web of interconnected observations, facts, and data." Even readers who dislike your conclusions will find the weight of evidence hard to shift.

### Diagnose by doing, not only by speculating

Strategy testing (Chapter 8) exploits the fact that it's easier to diagnose by doing than by speculating: use a recursive diagnosis process until you have real-world evidence the strategy is working.

### Data's role in diagnosis

If everyone shares the same data and the same assumptions about how it will change, evaluating the strategy becomes vastly simpler. Data is the mechanism for supporting or critiquing the perspectives you've gathered: to an impartial reader, "data will speak louder than passion."

Expect missing data: in strategy work it's common that much of the data you want doesn't exist. If the data already made the decision easy, you'd probably have decided already rather than running a structured thinking process. Accepting missing data is essential — it's also why many good strategies never get shared. (Chapter 8, on refining strategy, covers tools for building confidence in low-data environments.)

### Controversial truths must be included, reframed politely

Some diagnoses contain elements that can't be said bluntly (e.g., a specific leader is a bad hiring manager). Omitting them makes the strategy nonsensical to readers and "particularly hard to evaluate, copy, or recreate." The resolution: include the full diagnosis, but reframe the difficult part into a palatable, precise, factual statement that avoids casting blame too narrowly. "Strategies are always more about realities than ideals." Trying to force explicit accountability inside the strategy writeup would undermine the strategy work itself.

### Blockers are diagnosis, not impossibility

When something seems to make strategy impossible (e.g., "executives change their minds too often"), that insight is almost certainly true — but reframe it as a condition the strategy must address (e.g., "If we don't find a way to show concrete progress quickly and use that to excite the executive team, our strategy is likely to fail"). "There are never reasons why strategy simply cannot succeed — only diagnoses you've failed to recognize." Difficulties are part of the problem to be solved, not obstacles blocking progress.

### Self-awareness about your own role

Every problem of today is partially rooted in the decisions of yesterday. If you've been with the organization for any duration, you are directly or indirectly responsible for a portion of the problems your diagnosis should recognize. Recognizing the impact of your prior actions demonstrates self-awareness — and suggests your next strategy's success will be rooted in that self-awareness about your prior choices. Don't be afraid to name failures in your past work. Key distinction: "changing your mind without new data is a sign of chaotic leadership; changing your mind with new data is a sign of thoughtful leadership."

## Actionable guidance

### The five-step diagnosis process

1. **Braindump.** On a blank page, write your best understanding of the circumstances informing the strategy. Set it aside.
2. **Summarize exploration.** On a new page, review your exploration. Pull in every diagnosis from similar situations (internal or external) that resonates. Tag each as fitting your circumstances perfectly or needing adjustment. Set it aside.
3. **Mine for distinct perspectives.** On another page, talk to stakeholders and colleagues likely to *disagree* with your early thinking. The goal is to understand their views, not to agree with them. (Rumelt's *The Crux* anchors diagnosis in "testing, adjusting, and changing the frame, or point of view.")
4. **Synthesize into one internally consistent perspective.** Gathered perspectives may not mesh: people can explicitly differ on what the underlying problem even is (typical of the common tension between platform and product engineering teams). Represent each perspective competently — even ones you disagree with — so you can later evaluate your proposed approach against each. Don't crown one viewpoint as correct; readers should see detailed perspectives without sensing the author's biases. Hold one author accountable for representing all views rather than synthesizing by committee.
5. **Test drafts across perspectives.** Sit with the people you expect to disagree most fervently. Iterate until they agree you've accurately captured their perspectives. They needn't agree with every viewpoint, only that others genuinely hold those views.

### Decision rules and heuristics

- Form your diagnosis before deciding how to solve it.
- Try especially hard to capture perspectives you initially disagree with.
- Supplement intuition with data where you can; accept that sometimes the data you need will be missing.
- Aim for directionally correct, not perfectly correct. You're producing "the right crumbs" for refinement; perfecting details prematurely anchors you in one perspective.
- If a stakeholder says your data doesn't capture their full reality, either collect more data (preferred) or document their disagreement that the data is comprehensive. Fulfill one or two data requests per stakeholder, then hold the line — don't let data requests stall progress indefinitely.
- If you're confident a perspective is true, include a data narrative supporting it. If you believe a perspective is overstated, include the data a reader needs to reach that conclusion themselves.
- Include data *analysis* inline with a link to full original sources, rather than making readers interpret raw data. Linking sources somewhat prevents inevitable requests for different cuts as the document travels.
- When you hit "say something awkward about the company or a person" versus "omit a critical piece of diagnosis," choose neither: include the full diagnosis reframed as precise, factual, nonjudgmental statements.
- When you find a reason strategy seems difficult or unlikely to work, treat it as an important piece of the diagnosis to write down.
- Recognize your own prior decisions' contribution to current problems in the diagnosis itself.
- If the five steps feel overly mechanical, adapt them — there's no perfect way to understand complex problems. But if uncertain or skeptical of your own track record, start with the structured approach.

## Pitfalls and misconceptions

- **Skipping or rushing diagnosis.** The urge to "get to the solution already" is the canonical failure mode; every failed strategy Larson has seen traces to lazy or inaccurate diagnosis.
- **Author bias bleeding through synthesis.** If the author's opinion shines through strongly, the whole diagnosis becomes suspect.
- **Committee-owned synthesis.** A common issue: a group jointly owning synthesis creates fractured perspectives rather than a unified one; assign one accountable author.
- **Stating bare judgments instead of evidence webs.** Direct judgments invite dispute; interconnected facts and data resist it.
- **Omitting controversial diagnosis elements.** This makes the strategy hard to understand and particularly hard to evaluate, copy, or recreate (the bar-raiser program made little sense without its hidden diagnosis).
- **Casting blame narrowly.** Naming an individual as the problem undermines the strategy work; use professional, factual framing instead.
- **Treating blockers as proof strategy is impossible** rather than as conditions the strategy must address.
- **Endless data collection.** Letting stakeholder data requests delay progress indefinitely.
- **Perfectionism in early drafts.** Polishing details anchors you in one perspective prematurely.
- **Changing your mind without new data** — chaotic leadership, as opposed to changing it with new data.
