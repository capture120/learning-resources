# Chapter 5: Analytic geometry

**The chapter's epigraph** (p. 69), which the rest of the chapter then inverts:

> "The geometrical mind is not so closely bound to geometry that it cannot be drawn aside and transferred to other departments of knowledge. A work of morality, politics, criticism, perhaps even eloquence will be more elegant, other things being equal, if it is shaped by the hand of Geometry."
>
> — *Bernard le Bovier de Fontenelle*, 1729, 'Preface sur l'Utilite des Mathematiques et la Physique', translated by F. Cajori

Read it next to what Tao actually does and you get a nice joke at the reader's expense. Fontenelle is boasting that geometrical *thinking* can be exported out of geometry into politics and rhetoric; Tao spends the chapter importing algebra, combinatorics and arithmetic *into* geometry. The shared moral is the one that matters: the compartment a way of thinking was born in is not the compartment it has to stay in.

> This is the chapter where geometry gets *refused*. Every problem here has a geometric statement — a regular polygon in a circle, a rectangle cut into rectangles, line segments in the plane, a boy in a swimming pool — and in not one of them does Tao reach for a classical geometry theorem. The chapter's opening sentence sets the policy: these are "problems involving geometrical concepts and objects, but whose solution requires ideas from other branches of mathematics: algebra, inequalities, induction, and so on" (p. 69). The headline move is *coordinatize*: "One nice trick that sometimes works very well is to rewrite the geometry problem in terms of vectors in order to use the laws of vector arithmetic" (p. 69). But the four problems actually fan out into four different escape routes from geometry: **vectors and the dot product** (5.1), **a two-colouring plus a connectivity/blocking argument** (5.2), **a monovariant — a numerical quantity that only ever decreases** (5.3), and **a game-theoretic strategy argument with explicit arithmetic** (5.4). The deeper lesson of the chapter is therefore about *tool selection*: read the shape of the objective, decide which branch of mathematics has the machinery that matches that shape, and translate the problem into that branch's language before you start pushing symbols.

## Background you need first

You have Calculus 1 and basic linear algebra; that is nearly enough for this chapter. Here is the rest, with tiny examples.

**Points as vectors.** Writing $A_1, \dots, A_n$ for both *points* and *vectors from the origin to those points* is the whole trick of analytic geometry. If $A = (3,1)$ and $B = (1,4)$ then the displacement from $B$ to $A$ is the vector $A - B = (2,-3)$, and the distance between the points is the length of that vector. Notation: $\lvert AB\rvert$ means "the distance from $A$ to $B$", and $\lvert AB\rvert^2$ is its square.

**Squared length is a dot product.** For any vector $v$, $\;v\cdot v = \lvert v\rvert^2$. Example: $v = (2,-3)$, $v\cdot v = 4+9 = 13 = \lvert v\rvert^2$. This one line is the bridge Tao uses in Problem 5.1, because the dot product obeys ordinary algebra (it is bilinear and symmetric), so
$$(A-B)\cdot(A-B) = A\cdot A - 2\,A\cdot B + B\cdot B,$$
exactly like $(a-b)^2 = a^2 - 2ab + b^2$. Lengths themselves obey no such rule — that is why squares are so much friendlier than lengths.

**Regular polygon inscribed in the unit circle.** $n$ equally spaced points on the circle of radius 1. Put the centre at the origin and every vertex is a *unit* vector: $A_i \cdot A_i = 1$ for all $i$. For $n=4$ the vertices are $(\pm 1, 0), (0,\pm 1)$; the side length is $\sqrt2$ and the diagonal is $2$.

**The one formula that lets you check any of Tao's small cases** (not in the book, but you will want it). Vertices at angles $2\pi(k-1)/n$, so $A_k = (\cos\frac{2\pi(k-1)}{n}, \sin\frac{2\pi(k-1)}{n})$. The chord subtending $j$ steps around the polygon has length
$$\lvert A_1 A_{j+1}\rvert = 2\sin\!\Big(\frac{j\pi}{n}\Big).$$
(Why: the two endpoints are separated by a central angle of $2\pi j/n$; drop a perpendicular from the centre to the chord and you get two right triangles with hypotenuse $1$ and half-angle $\pi j/n$.) Check Tao's numbers: $n=3$, $j=1$: $2\sin 60° = \sqrt3$ ✓. $n=4$: $2\sin45° = \sqrt2$ and $2\sin90° = 2$ ✓. $n=6$: $2\sin30° = 1$, $2\sin60° = \sqrt3$, $2\sin 90° = 2$ ✓.

And now you can do the case Tao deliberately skipped. **$n=5$**: the two distinct lengths are $2\sin36° \approx 1.1756$ and $2\sin72° \approx 1.9021$, so
$$X = 4(\sin^2 36° + \sin^2 72°) \approx 4(0.3455 + 0.9045) = 4(1.25) = 5,$$
which is exactly the formula's prediction $X = n = 5$ for odd $n$. This is worth doing yourself for two reasons: it confirms the final answer on a case that was *not* used to generate the conjecture (the strongest kind of check), and it shows concretely what "not as simple to evaluate unless you know a bunch of sines and cosines" cost him — the two irrational lengths only collapse to the integer $5$ after you know that $\sin^2 36° + \sin^2 72° = 5/4$, which is not a fact anyone has memorized.

**Centroid.** The average of the vertices, $\frac1n(A_1+\cdots+A_n)$ — the "balance point". For a *regular* polygon the centroid is the centre of the circumscribed circle, so $A_1+\cdots+A_n = 0$ when the origin is at that centre. Tao proves this rather than assuming it (p. 74).

**Cross product** (needed only for Exercise 5.1). For 3-D vectors, $u\times v$ is the vector perpendicular to both, with length equal to the area of the parallelogram they span. Example: $(1,0,0)\times(0,1,0) = (0,0,1)$, length 1 = area of the unit square.

**Moment of inertia and Steiner's theorem** (an aside on p. 74). In physics, the moment of inertia of a set of unit point masses about a point $P$ is $\sum_i \lvert P A_i\rvert^2$ — a sum of squared distances, exactly the quantity $Y$ in Problem 5.1. Steiner's *parallel-axis theorem* says: moment of inertia about any point $P$ = moment of inertia about the centroid $+$ (total mass) $\times$ $\lvert P - \text{centroid}\rvert^2$. In symbols, $I_P = I_{\text{cm}} + M d^2$. This is exactly the algebraic identity Tao derives, dressed in mechanics vocabulary. You do not need it, but noticing that a piece of algebra is a known physical law is itself a technique (#26).

**Triangle inequality.** For any triangle, the sum of the lengths of two sides exceeds the third: $\lvert AB \rvert + \lvert BC\rvert > \lvert AC\rvert$ (strictly, if the three points are not on a line). Equivalently: a detour is longer than going straight. This is the entire engine of Problem 5.3.

**"No three collinear."** No three of the given points lie on a single straight line. This is a *genericity* hypothesis: it rules out degenerate pictures where two segments overlap along a stretch instead of crossing at a point.

**Monovariant (also called a "monovariant" or "termination measure").** Programmers will recognize this immediately: it is a **loop variant**. To prove a loop terminates you exhibit a quantity that strictly decreases every iteration and cannot decrease forever (it is a non-negative integer, or it ranges over a finite set). An *invariant* never changes; a *monovariant* only ever moves one way. Problem 5.3 is a pure monovariant problem: find a real number attached to the configuration, show every legal move strictly decreases it, note there are only finitely many configurations, conclude the process must halt.

**The game of Hex** (invoked on p. 77). Two players colour hexagonal cells of a rhombic board, one trying to build a connected chain of their colour from the left edge to the right edge, the other from top to bottom. Its famous theorem: **when the board is completely filled, exactly one of the two players has a winning chain** — you cannot block your opponent without simultaneously completing your own connection. Problem 5.2 uses exactly this "either red connects one way or green connects the other" duality.

**The game of Sprouts** (invoked on p. 78, in square brackets). Start with dots; a move joins two dots (or a dot to itself) with a curve that crosses nothing, then places a new dot on that curve. Tao's own vocabulary and count, worth having verbatim because the definition is the whole trick: "every time a move is made (joining two points and placing a third) the number of available **exits** (an exit is an unused edge from a point: each point starts with three exits) goes down by one (two are used up by the line and one is created by the new point). This shows that the game cannot last forever, because one will run out of exits." So: exits are the resource, each dot is born with three of them, a move spends two and mints one, net $-1$ per move, and the supply is finite. Note that the monovariant here is a *count of unused capacity*, not a geometric measurement — evidence for Tao's dart-board point that you are allowed to invent the quantity.

**Reading Tao's difficulty stars.** `(*)` and `(**)` on problems and exercises are his difficulty ratings; unstarred is easiest. In this chapter: Problem 5.1 unstarred, Exercise 5.1 `(**)`, Problem 5.2 `(*)`, Problem 5.3 unstarred, Problem 5.4 unstarred, Exercise 5.2 `(*)`, Exercise 5.3 `(**)`.

## Technique 1: Recast the geometry into another branch of mathematics

- **What it is.** Refuse to solve a geometry problem with geometry. Translate the configuration into vectors, coordinates, complex numbers, algebra, inequalities, combinatorics, or induction — whichever branch owns the machinery that matches your objective.
- **When to reach for it.** Whenever the statement is geometric but the *objective* is arithmetic or algebraic in flavour (a sum, a squared length, a parity, a count, an integrality claim), or when the configuration is too general to have a classical theorem attached to it.
- **Tao's own words.** The chapter is defined by it: these are "problems involving geometrical concepts and objects, but whose solution requires ideas from other branches of mathematics: algebra, inequalities, induction, and so on" (p. 69). And: "One nice trick that sometimes works very well is to rewrite the geometry problem in terms of vectors in order to use the laws of vector arithmetic" (p. 69).
- **Seen in action.** Problem 5.1 → vector algebra. Problem 5.2 → a two-colouring and a connectivity argument (combinatorics/topology), plus the single arithmetic fact that integers add to integers. Problem 5.3 → a monovariant and the triangle inequality. Problem 5.4 → strategy analysis plus straightforward distance arithmetic.
- **Why it works.** Classical synthetic geometry has few *general-purpose* manipulation rules; algebra has many. Once the objective is an equation you can add, expand, substitute, and collect terms mechanically. The programming analogy: this is a *reduction* — you convert an instance of problem A into an instance of problem B for which you already own a solver.
- **Failure mode.** The translation can cost more than it buys: Tao explicitly notes that plain coordinate geometry on Problem 5.1 "will give a slow-but-steady solution involving trigonometric sums" (p. 71) — correct but painful. Choose the *variant* of the translation that matches the objective (see Technique 3).

## Technique 2: Let the form of the objective choose the tool

- **What it is.** Look at the algebraic *shape* of the thing you must compute and match it against the tool that has an identity of that shape.
- **When to reach for it.** At the very start, before any computation.
- **Tao's own words.** "We are summing the squares of lengths, not lengths themselves. This immediately puts us out of the realm of pure geometry and into analytic geometry: it suggests vectors, or coordinate geometry, or perhaps complex numbers (these are essentially all the same approaches anyway)" (p. 71).
- **Seen in action.** *Squares of lengths* → $\lvert v\rvert^2 = v\cdot v$ → dot products → vectors. In Problem 5.3, *"the segments get more separated"* → hard to quantify; *"the segments get shorter"* → total length → triangle inequality.
- **Why it works.** Squared lengths are polynomial in the coordinates; lengths are not (they carry a square root). Any objective that is polynomial in the coordinates can be pushed around by algebra. This is why the problem asks for the sum of *squares* — the problem setter chose the exponent that makes an elegant solution exist, which is itself a hint.
- **Cost out the alternatives.** Tao lists three translations and grades them in one sentence: coordinates = slow but sure; vectors = dot product available; complex numbers = complex exponential available. Choosing among tools *explicitly*, rather than grabbing the first, is the technique.

## Technique 3: Express the objective as an equation

- **What it is.** Convert whatever the problem asks for — a set, a maximum, a description in words — into a formula with an equals sign in it.
- **When to reach for it.** Essentially always.
- **Tao's own words** (p. 71, and one of the most quotable lines in the book):

  > "Equations are solid mathematics. Not as inspirational as pictures and ideas, but the easiest to manipulate. In general, one would always express the objective as an equation of some sort. Some possible exceptions, though, are in combinatorics or graph theory."

- **Seen in action.** In Problem 5.1 the objective is a *set* $L$ of distinct lengths; sets are hard to manipulate. Tao's exact framing of the goal — note that the target is a *degree* of equation-ness, not a binary — is: "But we can soon rephrase the question into a form which is **more reducible to an equation**." He then converts it into the explicit finite sum $\lvert A_1A_2\rvert^2 + \cdots + \lvert A_1A_m\rvert^2$. Two intermediate reformulations do the work: restricting to segments from a single vertex (Technique 4), and then restricting to a semicircle. Neither is an equation; together they make an equation possible.
- **Why it works / what it buys you.** Pictures and slogans generate ideas but you cannot do arithmetic on them. An equation can be halved, doubled, expanded, and substituted into.
- **Failure mode.** Tao names it himself: combinatorics and graph theory. Problems 5.2 and 5.3 in this very chapter are the exceptions — there the productive representations are a *colouring* and a *monotone quantity*, not an equation.

## Technique 4: Choose a canonical representative to eliminate a "distinct values" condition

- **What it is.** When you must sum/count each *value* once, don't chase the values; find a set of *objects* that realizes each value exactly once, and sum over the objects.
- **When to reach for it.** The statement says "distinct", "different", "the set of all possible...".
- **Tao's own words.** "It is almost impossible to try to deal with the problem directly, because we are not summing the squares of the lengths of all diagonals, only all diagonals of distinct length" (p. 71).
- **Seen in action.** Problem 5.1: fix one vertex $A_1$ and take only the segments from $A_1$ to vertices in the upper semicircle. Every distinct length occurs exactly once in that list; nothing is double counted, nothing is missed.
- **Why it works.** It replaces a quotient (values modulo equality) by a concrete index set $i = 2,\dots,m$ that you can put into a $\sum$.

## Technique 5: Symmetrize the sum, then add a zero

- **What it is.** If a sum stops at an unnatural place, extend it to the natural place by exploiting a symmetry that pairs the terms, and pay for it with a factor of $\tfrac12$ plus a correction term. If a term is missing at the start, add it in when it is zero.
- **When to reach for it.** Your index runs $2 \le i \le m$ where $m$ is some ugly expression like $n/2 + 1$, and you can see a reflection symmetry.
- **Tao's own words.** "It is a bit inconvenient that the sum ... stops at $A_m$, rather than the more natural $A_n$. But (as with Problem 2.6) we can 'double up'" (p. 72). And: "We can tidy this up, and add the quantity $\lvert A_1A_1\rvert^2$ (since it is zero) for sake of symmetry."
- **Seen in action.** The reflection $i \mapsto n+2-i$ fixes $A_1$ and swaps the two halves of the polygon, so $\lvert A_1A_i\rvert = \lvert A_1A_{n+2-i}\rvert$; hence the sum over half the polygon is half the sum over all of it (up to the one self-paired term when $n$ is even).
- **Why it works.** Uniform index ranges are what let you apply general identities. A sum from 1 to $n$ can be attacked; a sum from 2 to $\lceil (n+1)/2\rceil$ cannot.
- **Failure mode.** Doubling up introduces bookkeeping: here, the long diagonal when $n$ is even gets counted twice, producing the $+2$ correction in equation (17). Symmetrizing without auditing the fixed points of the symmetry is a classic off-by-one bug.

## Technique 6: Introduce a prettier auxiliary quantity and compute that instead

- **What it is.** Don't compute $X$; define a cleaner $Y$ from which $X$ follows, and compute $Y$.
- **When to reach for it.** Your target has case splits, ugly endpoints, or a shape no identity matches.
- **Seen in action.** Problem 5.1 defines $Y = \lvert A_1A_1\rvert^2 + \cdots + \lvert A_1A_n\rvert^2$ (equation (18), p. 72). Tao then lists the three payoffs in bullet form — and *writing down why the change of variable is a good idea* is part of the technique:
  1. Knowing $Y$ gives $X$ immediately, via (16) and (17).
  2. "$Y$ has a prettier form than $X$, and thus is hopefully easier to compute."
  3. Computing $Y$ needs no odd/even case split, "which may save us some work".
- **Why it works.** You are choosing your battlefield. All the parity ugliness is confined to the *bridge* between $X$ and $Y$ — two lines of bookkeeping done once — and the actual mathematical work happens on the clean object.
- **Programming analogy.** This is refactoring: push the special cases out to a thin wrapper and keep the core loop uniform.

## Technique 7: Choose the origin to maximize the number of simple relations

- **What it is.** Coordinates are free. Spend that freedom to make as many quantities as possible equal to $0$ or $1$.
- **When to reach for it.** Any time you coordinatize.
- **Tao's own words.** "We can choose the origin of our coordinate system to be wherever we want, but the most logical choice would be to put the origin at the centre of the circle. (The second best choice would be to make $A_1$ the origin.)" (p. 73)
- **Seen in action.** With the origin at the centre, every vertex is a unit vector, so $A_1\cdot A_1 = \cdots = A_n\cdot A_n = 1$ — $n$ free facts. The alternative choice ($A_1$ at the origin) makes one term vanish but destroys the other $n-1$ relations. Tao *ranks* the options rather than just picking one.
- **Why it works.** Every symbol that becomes $1$ or $0$ is a symbol you never have to carry through the algebra.

## Technique 8: Work backwards from the conjectured answer to a sufficient claim

- **What it is.** Once you have guessed the answer, expand your expression until the difference between "what I have" and "what I want" is a single identifiable claim. Then prove that claim.
- **When to reach for it.** After conjecturing (Technique: guess-then-prove).
- **Seen in action.** Expansion gives $Y = 2n - 2A_1\cdot(A_1 + A_2 + \cdots + A_n)$. The conjecture is $Y = 2n$. So: "We can now achieve this guess if we can show that the vector sum $A_1+A_2+\cdots+A_n$ is zero" (p. 73). The whole problem has collapsed into one lemma.
- **Why it works.** It converts an open-ended computation into a specific, checkable sub-goal. Note that the sub-goal is *sufficient*, not obviously necessary — you don't need the weaker/stronger distinction, you just need one route home.

## Technique 9: Exploit symmetry — and then make the symmetry argument rigorous

- **What it is.** If a configuration is unchanged by a transformation, then any quantity built from it is also unchanged; a quantity that is fixed by a non-trivial rotation is often forced to be zero.
- **Tao's own words.** "Always look for ways to exploit symmetry" (pp. 73–74).
- **Seen in action, twice over.** First the informal version: the vectors "'pull' in all directions with equal strength, so the net result has to be 0"; or, the centroid of a regular polygon is its centre. Then, acknowledging that "One may not be happy with the hand-waving symmetry argument", the rigorous version (p. 74):
  1. Let $v = A_1 + \cdots + A_n$.
  2. Rotate the whole picture about the origin by $360°/n$. This permutes the vertices (each $A_i$ becomes $A_{i+1}$), so the *set* of vertices is unchanged, so the *sum* $v$ is unchanged.
  3. Therefore rotating $v$ by $360°/n$ gives back $v$.
  4. But a non-zero vector genuinely moves when you rotate it by an angle that is not a multiple of $360°$. Hence $v = 0$.
- **Why it works.** Step 4 is a *fixed-point* argument: the only vector fixed by a non-trivial rotation is the zero vector. This pattern ("the object is invariant under a symmetry, therefore it lies in the tiny set of symmetric objects") recurs throughout mathematics.
- **Failure mode / the meta-lesson.** Tao does not throw away the hand-waving argument — he keeps it as the *idea* and supplies the rigorous version as the *proof*. He also notes two other routes to the same lemma (trigonometry, complex numbers). Having three proofs of your lemma is insurance.

## Technique 10: Reinterpret the algebra in another domain

- **What it is.** Notice that your expression is a known quantity in physics (or economics, or probability) and import that field's theorems.
- **Seen in action.** "the sum of squares $Y$ is basically the moment of inertia about $A$" — that is, about $A_1$ — "and then one can use Steiner's theorem of parallel axes ... to move the rotation point to the centroid" (p. 74; he cites Borchardt, 1961, p. 370). Steiner's theorem instantly relates "sum of squared distances from $A_1$" to "sum of squared distances from the centre" plus a correction, which is exactly the computation just performed by hand.
- **Why it works.** Physicists have already done the bookkeeping for sums of squared distances. Recognizing your object under another name gives you their lemmas for free.

## Technique 11: The monovariant (find the quantity that only goes one way)

- **What it is.** To show a process cannot continue forever, attach a number to each state such that (i) it moves strictly in one direction with every legal move, and (ii) it cannot move in that direction indefinitely.
- **When to reach for it.** Any question of the form "can this be done indefinitely / must this terminate / can we return to the start?".
- **Tao's own words** (p. 78) — the recipe, in two bullets, quoted in full because every clause does work:
  - "(a) Find some characteristic of the system that can be represented as a number. **It could be number of intersections, number of line segments, perhaps a sum of carefully chosen point scores (like a dart board).** It must reflect 'outeredginess'; that is, **it should be higher when all the edges are scattered on the rim.**"
  - "(b) This characteristic must increase (or perhaps remain steady, but this is a lot weaker) every time our procedure is used."

  Two things in (a) are easy to skim past and shouldn't be. First, the **dart-board** image: Tao is telling you that the numerical characteristic need not be anything canonical — you are allowed to *invent* a scoring function, assigning made-up point values to made-up features, exactly as a dart board assigns 20 to one wedge and 1 to its neighbour for no intrinsic reason. That licence to invent is what makes the search feasible. Second, the clause "**it should be higher when all the edges are scattered on the rim**" is an *operational test*: it converts the un-measurable word 'outeredginess' into a check you can actually apply to a candidate quantity — draw the end configuration you expect, draw a tangled starting configuration, and see whether your number really is bigger in the first. Coin a word for the intuition (Technique: English first), then immediately give the word a test.
- **And the termination clause**, in Tao's phrasing (p. 77–78): "this cannot happen indefinitely because there are only a finite number of configurations the system can reach. Eventually the 'outeredginess' should reach at a maximum and the procedure will stop." Followed by the parenthetical that is the whole idea in eight words: "(That is, things stop when they cannot go any further.)"
- **Mind the sign.** Tao's framework asks for a quantity that *increases*, and the quantity he ends up using — total edge length — *decreases*. This is not a contradiction and it is worth being explicit about, because a careful reader will trip on it: 'outeredginess' was the increasing thing, and total length is (a proxy for) its negative. A monovariant is any quantity that moves strictly one way, and $Q$ increases strictly iff $-Q$ decreases strictly, so you may set the recipe up in whichever direction is convenient and flip at the end. What you may *not* do is let the quantity sometimes rise and sometimes fall — that is exactly why 'number of intersections' was rejected.
- **Seen in action.** Problem 5.3, with total edge length as the monovariant. Also the *Sprouts* aside (exits decrease by one per move).
- **Why it works.** It replaces reasoning about an unbounded sequence of moves with reasoning about a single move. Same reason a loop variant works in program verification: local decrease + well-foundedness = global termination.
- **How to *find* one.** Tao's advice is bluntly empirical: "The best technique is just guessing something simple and hope that it works." Then he narrows the search space by two clever meta-arguments (next technique).

## Technique 12: Narrow the search for a monovariant by symmetry and by what the operation touches

- **What it is.** Instead of guessing blindly, reason about what *kind* of quantity could possibly work.
- **Seen in action** (p. 79, after the solution is already found — Tao is explaining why it was findable):
  1. "Because we are changing two line segments each time, any characteristic under consideration should be in terms of individual line segments, rather than intersections or other properties." — Match the granularity of your measure to the granularity of the move.
  2. "Now there are really only three properties of individual line segments: length, position, and orientation." — Enumerate the possibilities exhaustively; the list is short.
  3. Position and orientation are ruled out by symmetry, and Tao's reason is worth having in full including the leading clause: "**Position and orientation type characteristics fail to give nice results because those properties cannot really decrease or increase:** for example, it is unlikely that, after each operation, the total orientation (whatever that is) goes clockwise. If that were so, why clockwise and not counter-clockwise? There is no real distinction between clockwise and counter-clockwise, while there is a definite difference between longer and shorter." (Concretely: reflect the entire configuration in a mirror. Every length is unchanged, every intersection is still an intersection, the rewrite rule still applies the same way — but every clockwise rotation has become counter-clockwise. A quantity that reflection negates cannot be one that always increases, since the mirror-image process would then always decrease it, and the mirror-image process is a legal process. Lengths, by contrast, survive reflection untouched, so nothing stops them from having a preferred direction of travel.)
  4. Conclusion: "one almost is forced to use 'total length' ideas."
- **Why it works.** A monovariant must break a symmetry of the problem, because "increase" and "decrease" are different. So look at which of your candidate quantities has a *built-in asymmetry*. Length does (there is a shortest configuration, no longest direction); angle does not (the problem has no preferred handedness).
- **Transferable form.** *If your problem is invariant under a symmetry, then no quantity that is also invariant under that symmetry can be your monovariant.* This is a genuinely powerful search heuristic and it is stated nowhere else in the book as clearly as here.

## Technique 13: Encode unknown binary choices as a colouring

- **What it is.** When each of many objects independently has one of two properties and you don't know which, paint them two colours and reason about the *pattern* of colours rather than the individual choices.
- **When to reach for it.** A case analysis that would branch $2^k$ ways.
- **Seen in action.** Problem 5.2: "imagine that any rectangle with a horizontal integer edge is coloured green, and any rectangle with a vertical integer edge can be coloured red. (Rectangles with both horizontal and vertical integer edges are a bonus: they can be assigned either colour.)" (p. 76)
- **Why it works.** It converts an intractable enumeration into a single geometric question about two-coloured regions — for which there is a known answer (Hex duality). Note the handling of the overlap case: objects with *both* properties are given a free choice, which only ever helps.
- **Programming analogy.** You have replaced exponential branching with a structural argument about the whole assignment — like replacing brute-force SAT enumeration with an argument about the formula's structure.

## Technique 14: Solve the lower-dimensional / simplest non-trivial case, then mine it for mechanism

- **What it is.** Attack a stripped-down instance, then ask not "what was the answer?" but "which ingredients did the proof use?"
- **Tao's own words.** "Examples are really only useful when they give some insight into how the general problem works." (p. 75)
- **Seen in action.** Problem 5.2 twice:
  - The **1-D analogue** (segments instead of rectangles) is trivial and does *not* generalize — but it yields one clue: "we have to use the fact that the sum of integers is an integer" (p. 75). That is the entire arithmetic content of the final proof.
  - The **two-rectangle partition** is solved in five lines; scanning that proof gives the two ingredients that structure the general argument: (a) you must split into cases, (b) you need a *chain* of rectangles that "add up".
- **Failure mode.** A special case that only gives you the answer, not the method, is nearly worthless. Tao's parenthetical is a warning as much as a slogan.

## Technique 15: Proof by contradiction, then convert "impossible" into positive structure

- **What it is.** Assume the thing you want is impossible; ask *why*; the obstruction you are forced to name is usually the other half of the statement.
- **Tao's own words.** "No direct proof seems to be available, so let us try proof by contradiction." (p. 76)
- **Seen in action.** Suppose no green chain joins the two vertical edges. Why not? "Because there are not enough green rectangles: the red rectangles must have blocked the greens. But the only way to block the green rectangles from reaching the vertical edge is by a solid barrier of red rectangles. But a solid barrier of red rectangles must join the two horizontal edges." (pp. 76–77) The failed green connection *manufactures* a red connection.
- **Why it works.** "Not connected" is a negative, unusable statement; "separated by a barrier" is a positive, constructive one. Converting a non-existence assumption into an existing obstruction is the standard way to make a contradiction argument productive rather than vacuous.

## Technique 16: Separate the intuitive core from the formal write-up, and flag the gap honestly

- **What it is.** When your key step is visually obvious but technically fiddly, state the intuition, then record — explicitly — that a formal proof takes work, and sketch how.
- **Seen in action.** Problem 5.2, p. 77: "(Incidentally, while the essence of the above paragraph is a very intuitive statement, actually proving it formally and topologically requires some work. Briefly: the set of all green areas can be divided into connected subsets. Assuming that none of these subsets straddle both vertical edges, consider the union of the left vertical edge together with all green connected subsets that touch on the left vertical edge. Then a small strip touching the boundary of this set on the outside will be coloured red, and this red strip defines a red set of rectangles which will straddle the two horizontal edges.)"
- **Why it works.** It keeps the reader (and you) from confusing "I can see it" with "I have proved it", while not letting the technicalities smother the idea.
- **Companion move: sweep up the edge cases.** Tao then explicitly disposes of the three fiddly configurations in a chain (p. 77): superfluous rectangles ("easily ditched"), rectangles touching only at a corner ("also not a problem"), and backwards-going chains — "we are subtracting integers instead of adding them on, but the grand total will always be an integer". Naming and dismissing your edge cases in one sentence each is far better than pretending they don't exist.

## Technique 17: In a two-outcome problem, pick the branch with the cheaper proof obligation

- **What it is.** Before proving anything, work out which of "yes" and "no" would be *easier to prove*, and try that one first.
- **Tao's own words** (p. 80):

  > "Surely it is easier to prove that one strategy works, than to prove that all strategies do not work. So, let us assume the boy can escape. It looks the easier of the two options: always tackle the soft options first—you may then be able to dodge a load of hard work later on. (This is not laziness, but practicality. As long as the job is done well, the easy way is of course better than the hard way.)"

- **When to reach for it.** Existence-vs-universal asymmetry: "does there exist a winning strategy?" is answered yes by *one* witness and no only by defeating *everything*.
- **Supporting move — dimension counting.** Circumstantial evidence for the same choice: the boy "can move in two dimensions, while the teacher is effectively limited to one" (p. 80). Counting degrees of freedom is a cheap way to guess who wins.
- **But grade the evidence.** Tao opens that whole paragraph with "**Circumstantial evidence is a bit uncertain**" — he is explicitly *not* claiming the dimension count is an argument. It is a bet, and he says so. Marking a step as heuristic rather than proved is not modesty for its own sake: it tells you which claims you will have to go back and discharge, and stops you from building a proof on a hunch you have forgotten was a hunch. (Compare the "not a strong conjecture at present" hedge in Problem 5.1 and the "hand-waving symmetry argument" label on p. 74. Tao does this constantly.)
- **And name the method you are committing to.** "But the first possibility is easier **without becoming too trial-and-errorish: we would have to intelligently guess a strategy, and then prove that it works.**" The existence branch is only cheap if you have a way of generating good guesses; otherwise "exhibit one witness" degenerates into an unbounded search. So the real comparison is not *existence vs universality* but *the cost of a good guess vs the cost of a universal argument*.
- **Companion.** "Now, to solve this question, we should first decide for ourselves what the answer is going to be. (You cannot really search for a solution unless you know what you are searching for.)" (p. 80)

## Technique 18: Prune the strategy space with common sense before computing anything

- **What it is.** In a game or optimization problem, eliminate whole families of strategies by soft arguments, so the computation you eventually do is small.
- **Seen in action** (Problem 5.4, pp. 80–81). Tao's own framing of the move: "Before we start guessing strategies, let us use some common sense to eliminate some bad strategies and isolate some promising tactics." Six prunings, each with a one-line justification:
  1. **Always move at maximum speed** — "even if some obscure advantage is gained by slowing down, the teacher can simply slow down to match."
  2. **Never stop** — "the teacher can wait until the boy moves again. (Stalemate is not a victory from the boy's point of view.)"
  3. **The teacher stays on the pool edge** — "why go away from the edge? It would only slow the teacher down." (This is the *assume a competent adversary* move: "we may assume that the teacher is no pushover".)
  4. **Straight lines** — "since the boy is trying to reach the edge of the pool quickly (**or, at least quicker than the teacher could**), so presumably straight lines, being the shortest (and hence the fastest) way to move, are part of the answer—although sudden turns and twists could conceivably be used to the boy's advantage." Two things here: the parenthetical quietly restates the objective as *relative* speed, not absolute (this is a race, not a time trial); and note he does not over-prune — he keeps the possibility of a turn, and the turn is exactly what wins.
  5. **The strategy must be adaptive** — "the strategy should not be wholly predetermined, but depend in part on the actions of the teacher: after all, if the teacher knows that you will wriggle around for a while until you reach corner $B$, for example, then the teacher could simply run to corner $B$ and wait for the boy to arrive—**if he follows his rather foolishly pre-determined plan.**" The pointed adverb is doing work: against an adversary, a fixed plan is not a strategy at all, because announcing it is equivalent to playing it.
  6. **And one pruning that happens before all of these** (p. 81, just after the summary): "Obviously the boy will have to move away from the teacher: **heading straight for $A$ is not a clever move.**" Stating the worst option out loud is nearly free and it orients the whole search — every candidate from here on heads away from $A$.
- **Why it works.** Each pruning is an argument about the *opponent's* best response, not about the position. It cuts an infinite-dimensional strategy space down to something you can enumerate: "The boy's best strategy would involve straight line bursts at top speed. Also, the strategy could be flexible to account for the actions of the teacher" (p. 81).
- **Failure mode.** Over-pruning. If Tao had pruned "sudden turns" as well, he would have lost the problem — the winning strategy is precisely a straight burst *followed by a right-angle turn*.

## Technique 19: Reduce the objective, then discard the data you have used

- **What it is.** Replace the goal by a simpler sufficient goal; once a hypothesis has done its work, note explicitly that it is no longer relevant.
- **Seen in action.** "The boy can run faster than the teacher, so this means that once he is on land and unintercepted, he can escape. So his first goal is to get out of the pool. Now that we have shown that, the boy's running speed is now irrelevant." (p. 80)
- **Why it works.** The problem now has one fewer parameter. Consciously retiring a hypothesis prevents you from re-deriving things you already know and stops you from looking for cleverness where none is needed.

## Technique 20: Use a threat to force the opponent's moves — and don't cash it

- **What it is.** Make a move your opponent *must* answer. The answer relocates them; then switch objectives.
- **Tao's own words** (pp. 81–82), two lines that are the heart of the solution:

  > "the boy is forcing the teacher's moves just by threatening to move in one direction. Being able to control, to some degree, the enemy moves can be a potent tool."

  > "The threat of being able to go to $M$ was enough to keep the teacher where he is now, but threat and actuality are different things."

- **When to reach for it.** Adversarial problems where one agent has less freedom than the other; also, more generally, any argument where you can pin down an unknown by considering what would happen if you *did* something.
- **Why it works.** It converts a two-player problem into a one-player problem: after the forcing phase, the opponent's position is *known*, not chosen.
- **Companion: retry a failed move in a better configuration.** Dashing straight for the edge fails at time zero (Technique 21 below shows the arithmetic). The *same* dash succeeds after the forcing phase, "unlike the first time we considered the boy dashing for the edge, the teacher is badly placed" (p. 82). The move was never bad; the position was.

## Technique 21: Test candidate strategies numerically, and read the near-miss

- **What it is.** Compute the two travel times. If your candidate loses by a wide margin, the idea is wrong; if it loses by a hair, the idea is nearly right and the problem's constants are on a knife-edge.
- **Seen in action.** Problem 5.4: the far-corner dash loses $0.707$ to $0.667$ — decisive failure, a 6% margin. The edge-midpoint dash gives an exact tie, $0.5$ vs $1.5/3 = 0.5$. Tao's reading of the tie: "The teacher **almost** lets the boy escape when he heads for the edge: if the teacher was only a fraction slower, then the boy would escape. This suggests that either
  - the teacher's speed is the barest minimum needed to stop the boy;
  - the teacher's speed is the barest maximum which allows the boy to escape."

  — two options that are, note, *not* the same statement, because they differ on what happens exactly at speed 3. Whichever holds, "the teacher's speed seems to be on a knife-edge here", so some cleverer strategy might tip the balance.
- **Explore both sides of the knife-edge.** Tao then perturbs the constant in each direction to see what each regime looks like, which is how he learns what kind of idea he needs: "If the teacher was marginally slower, the boy would automatically escape if he simply headed for the edge. If the teacher was significantly faster, it is possible that the teacher could **stalk** the boy, moving clockwise when the boy moves clockwise, and so on." So the two neighbouring regimes have *qualitatively different* solutions — a plain dash on one side, a stalking/angle-matching strategy on the other. (The stalking regime is exactly Exercise 5.2, where the teacher is $6\times$ as fast.) Perturbing a constant to see which regime you are in is a cheap way to find out what shape your argument must have.
- **Tao's own words.** "Common sense fails to reach a verdict; we have to do some computation." (p. 81)
- **Why it works.** Exact ties in a competition problem are never coincidences; they mark the boundary the problem was designed around. A tie is an invitation to find the extra $\varepsilon$ of advantage.

## Worked problems

### Problem 5.1 (Australian Mathematics Competition 1987, p. 14) — A regular $n$-gon is inscribed in a circle of radius 1. Among all the segments joining two of its vertices, collect the set $L$ of *distinct* lengths that occur. Compute the sum of the squares of the elements of $L$.

**Tao's exact wording** (p. 69): "A regular polygon with $n$ vertices is inscribed in a circle of radius 1. Let $L$ be the set of all possible distinct lengths of all line segments joining the vertices of the polygon. What is the sum of the squares of the elements of $L$?" (Note that $L$ includes the polygon's *sides* as well as its diagonals — "all line segments joining the vertices" — which is why the $n=3$ answer is $3$ and not $0$.)

**The data / the objective.** Tao's framing: name the target $X$ ("let us give 'sum of squares of the elements of $L$' a shorter name, such as '$X$'; our job is to compute $X$") and classify the question. "This is what might be called a 'feasible' problem: It is not a 'prove this' or 'is this' problem, but an evaluation of a number that could be found, by say a straightforward application of trigonometry and perhaps Pythagoras' theorem" (p. 69). Consequence: for every specific $n$ the answer is computable, so *data is available*.

**The chain of moves.**

1. `[name the objective]` Call the sum of squares $X$.
2. `[classify the question type]` It is an evaluation problem, so brute-force small cases are possible and the answer is a formula in $n$.
3. `[compute small cases]` $n=4$: a square in the unit circle has side $\sqrt2$ and diagonal $2$, so $X = (\sqrt2)^2 + 2^2 = 6$. $n=3$: equilateral triangle, only one length, $\sqrt3$, so $X = 3$.
4. `[skip the expensive data point]` "$n = 5$ is not as simple to evaluate unless you know a bunch of sines and cosines, so let us skip it and try $n = 6$ instead." $n=6$: "The side length is 1, the small diagonal is $\sqrt3$, and the long diagonal (the diameter) is 2. Hence in this case $X = 1^2 + \sqrt3^2 + 2^2 = 8$." Two remarks. (i) This is triage, not laziness: $n=6$ costs three memorized lengths and $n=5$ costs a trig evaluation, and they buy roughly the same amount of information, so you buy the cheap one. (ii) Note also that $n=5$ would have been *more* informative in one respect — it is the only odd case besides $n=3$ he could easily reach, and the odd/even split turns out to matter. He got away with it because $n=3$ alone happened to pin the odd formula. See the Background section above for the $n=5$ computation done out ($X = 5$), which is a genuinely independent check on the final answer.
5. `[degenerate case, flagged]` $n=2$: the "polygon" is a diameter, $X = 2^2 = 4$ — recorded in the table as "2?  4?" because "it is a little dodgy to talk about a two-sided polygon."
6. `[tabulate]` The table (p. 70) is $(n,X) = (2?,4?), (3,3), (4,6), (6,8)$.
7. `[admit the data is inconclusive]` "This small table does not give us too many clues about what the general answer should be." **Dead end — but a productive one:** it tells him to change representation rather than keep computing.
8. `[draw the picture; label for general $n$]` An octagon inscribed in a circle, vertices labelled $A_1$ (left), then $A_2, \dots, A_8$ going clockwise around the top: $A_3$ at the top, $A_5$ at the right, $A_7$ at the bottom. He notes the choice explicitly: for a fixed $n$ you might use $A,B,C,\dots$, but "for the general $n$ case it is probably going to be more convenient to label the vertices $A_1, A_2, A_3, \dots, A_n$."
9. `[initial observations]` (a) Parity of $n$ matters: "It could make a difference if $n$ is odd or even. If $n$ is even, we have this long diagonal to contend with. In fact when $n$ is even we have $n/2$ diagonals, and when $n$ is odd we have $(n-1)/2$ diagonals." (A reading note: Tao's word "diagonals" here means *distinct lengths*, counting the side as one of them — for the square, $n/2 = 2$ counts the side $\sqrt2$ and the diagonal $2$. Check: $n=8$ gives $4$; $n=5$ gives $2$; $n=6$ gives $3$, matching the three lengths $1,\sqrt3,2$ he just computed.) (b) `[meta-guess]` The answer might always be an integer — and Tao immediately discounts his own evidence: "This is not a strong conjecture at present, because we are dealing with the very special square, hexagon, and equilateral triangle which have square-root type measurements. However, it gives us a bit of hope that the general solution would not be too nasty." Note the shape of that sentence: name the conjecture, name the reason it is weak, then name what it is still good for. A conjecture you distrust is still worth writing down as a *morale* signal about how ugly the final answer is allowed to be.
10. `[let the objective's form choose the tool]` Observation (c): we are summing **squares** of lengths. That "immediately puts us out of the realm of pure geometry and into analytic geometry: it suggests vectors, or coordinate geometry, or perhaps complex numbers". `[cost out the tools]` Coordinates: slow but sure, trigonometric sums. Vectors: dot product. Complex numbers: complex exponential.
11. `[express the objective as an equation]` Observation (d): the "distinct lengths" phrasing blocks a direct attack. `[canonical representative]` Restrict to segments emanating from the single vertex $A_1$, and further to the upper semicircle: for the octagon those are $\lvert A_1A_2\rvert, \lvert A_1A_3\rvert, \lvert A_1A_4\rvert, \lvert A_1A_5\rvert$ — the four distinct lengths, each hit exactly once. (The second figure, p. 71, is the same octagon with these four segments drawn in from $A_1$: to the adjacent vertex $A_2$, then $A_3$, then $A_4$, then the horizontal diameter to $A_5$.)
12. So $X = \lvert A_1A_2\rvert^2 + \cdots + \lvert A_1A_m\rvert^2$ where $m = (n/2)+1$ for even $n$ and $m=(n+1)/2$ for odd $n$. (The text on p. 71 misprints this as "$m = (n+1/2)$"; the boxed restatement on p. 72 has it right.)
13. `[restate the problem in a box]` p. 72 gives the cleaned-up statement in full — a small act of hygiene that makes the next steps legible.
14. `[symmetrize / double up]` The sum stopping at $A_m$ is awkward. By the reflection symmetry through the diameter at $A_1$, $\lvert A_1A_i\rvert = \lvert A_1A_{n+2-i}\rvert$. So $X$ equals one half of the sum over *all* the vertices — with a correction, because for even $n$ the diameter $\lvert A_1A_{n/2+1}\rvert^2 = 4$ is its own mirror image and gets counted twice.
15. `[add a zero term]` Insert $\lvert A_1A_1\rvert^2 = 0$ "for sake of symmetry", giving the clean pair of formulas
$$X = \tfrac12\big(\lvert A_1A_1\rvert^2 + \lvert A_1A_2\rvert^2 + \cdots + \lvert A_1A_n\rvert^2\big) \quad (16)\ \text{for } n \text{ odd},$$
$$X = \tfrac12\big(\lvert A_1A_1\rvert^2 + \lvert A_1A_2\rvert^2 + \cdots + \lvert A_1A_n\rvert^2\big) + 2 \quad (17)\ \text{for } n \text{ even},$$
the $+2$ being $\tfrac12 \times 4$, the halved long diagonal. (In the displayed "doubling up" line on p. 72 the last term is printed as $\lvert A_{n+2-m}\rvert^2$, evidently a misprint for $\lvert A_1A_{n+2-m}\rvert^2$.)
16. `[auxiliary quantity]` Define $Y = \lvert A_1A_1\rvert^2 + \lvert A_1A_2\rvert^2 + \cdots + \lvert A_1A_n\rvert^2$ (18), and compute $Y$ instead of $X$, for the three reasons listed above (Technique 6).
17. `[re-tabulate the old data]` "We can revisit our previous table of the small cases $n = 3,4,6$ and compute $Y$ in these cases (**for instance, by using (16) and (17)**)", giving $(n,X,Y) = (2?,4?,4?), (3,3,6), (4,6,8), (6,8,12)$. `[invert the bridge, don't redo the work]` That parenthetical is a small but real technique. He does *not* go back to the polygons and re-measure distances; he runs the bridge formulas backwards. From (16), $n$ odd: $Y = 2X$, so $n=3$ gives $Y = 2\cdot 3 = 6$. From (17), $n$ even: $X = \tfrac12 Y + 2$, so $Y = 2(X-2)$, giving $n=4 \to 2(6-2) = 8$, $n=6 \to 2(8-2) = 12$, and the dodgy $n=2 \to 2(4-2)=4$. Cost: four subtractions. The general moral: after a change of variable, your old data transfers for free — *and* the transfer exercises the bridge formulas, so if you had made an algebra slip in (16) or (17) the new column would look wrong. Re-tabulating is simultaneously data collection and a unit test.
18. `[conjecture, then prove]` "$Y = 2n$"; hence $X = n$ for odd $n$ and $X = n+2$ for even $n$. "This is probably going to be the right answer, but we still have to prove it."
19. `[squares → dot products]` Only now does the vector machinery come out, and Tao says why at the moment he reaches for it: "Now it is time to use vector geometry, **as it gives us a number of useful tools to manipulate expressions such as (18)**." The tool is chosen to fit the expression he has spent three pages constructing — not the other way round. Because "the square of a length of a vector $v$ is simply the dot product $v \cdot v$ of $v$ with itself", $\lvert v\rvert^2 = v\cdot v$, so
$$Y = (A_1-A_1)\cdot(A_1-A_1) + (A_1-A_2)\cdot(A_1-A_2) + \cdots + (A_1-A_n)\cdot(A_1-A_n),$$
"where we now think of $A_1,\dots,A_n$ as vectors rather than points."
20. `[choose the origin]` Origin at the centre of the circle ⟹ every $A_i$ is a unit vector ⟹ $A_i\cdot A_i = 1$ for all $i$. (Second best: $A_1$ at the origin.)
21. `[expand]` Using bilinearity, $(A_1-A_i)\cdot(A_1-A_i) = A_1\cdot A_1 - 2A_1\cdot A_i + A_i\cdot A_i = 2 - 2A_1\cdot A_i$. In words: the squared distance between two points on the unit circle is $2$ minus twice the dot product of their position vectors. So $Y = (2-2A_1\cdot A_1) + (2 - 2A_1\cdot A_2) + \cdots + (2-2A_1\cdot A_n)$.
22. `[collect terms]` $Y = 2n - 2A_1\cdot(A_1 + A_2 + \cdots + A_n)$.
23. `[work backwards to a sufficient claim]` The conjecture $Y=2n$ holds iff the bracket vanishes: it suffices to prove $A_1 + \cdots + A_n = 0$.
24. `[exploit symmetry — informal]` The unit vectors "'pull' in all directions with equal strength, so the net result has to be 0"; equivalently, the centroid of a regular polygon is its centre. "Always look for ways to exploit symmetry."
25. `[upgrade to rigour]` Set $v = A_1 + \cdots + A_n$. Rotating the picture by $360°/n$ about the origin permutes the vertices, hence leaves $v$ unchanged; but a rotation by a non-multiple of $360°$ moves every non-zero vector. Hence $v=0$.
26. `[note alternative proofs]` Trigonometry or complex numbers would also do it (the vertices are the $n$-th roots of unity, whose sum is $0$ by the geometric-series formula).
27. `[reinterpret in physics]` $Y$ is the moment of inertia of the vertices about $A_1$; Steiner's parallel-axis theorem transfers the reference point to the centroid, which is another route to the same conclusion.

**The resolution.** $Y = 2n$; therefore
$$X = n \ \ (n \text{ odd}), \qquad X = n+2 \ \ (n \text{ even}).$$
Sanity check against the table: $n=3 \to 3$ ✓; $n=4 \to 6$ ✓; $n=6 \to 8$ ✓; and even the dodgy $n=2 \to 4$ ✓. `[verify on an unused case]` Better still, check the case that was *never* fed into the conjecture: $n=5$, which the Background section computes as $4(\sin^2 36° + \sin^2 72°) = 5$, matching $X=n$ ✓. Checking against data you used to guess is weak evidence (it is what you fitted); checking against data you withheld is strong evidence, and it costs one extra evaluation. And the weak conjecture from step 9(b) — that the answer is always an integer — turned out to be exactly right, despite all those square roots in the individual lengths. That is the whole point of squaring.

**Transferable lesson.** The problem's *phrasing* (a set of distinct lengths) was hostile and its *objective* (a sum of squares) was friendly. Tao spent roughly two thirds of the write-up converting the phrasing into the objective's natural language, and the actual mathematics — expand, collect, kill the symmetric sum — took four lines. Reformulation is the work; computation is the reward.

### Exercise 5.1 (\*\*) — Prove that the area of a unit cube's projection on any plane equals the length of the cube's projection on the perpendicular of that plane.

**Tao's exact wording** (p. 74): "Prove that the area of a unit cube's projection on any plane equals the length of the cube's projection on the perpendicular of this plane." ("The perpendicular of this plane" means the line through the origin perpendicular to the plane — i.e. the normal direction. "Projection" is orthogonal projection, so "shadow cast by light coming in perpendicular to the screen" is a fair mental image, and the one I use below.)

**The data / the objective.** Project the unit cube orthogonally onto a plane $P$: you get a hexagon (or square) of some area. Project the same cube onto the line perpendicular to $P$: you get a segment of some length. Claim: those two numbers are equal. Note the free bit of weirdness that makes the statement worth proving: an *area* is being equated to a *length*, which is only dimensionally sane because the cube has side $1$.

**Tao gives only a hint, and the hint *is* a list of techniques** (p. 74):

1. `[recast as vectors]` "There is a neat vector solution, but it involves a good track keeping of cross products and the like."
2. `[choose a good coordinate system]` "First of all, select a good coordinate system".
3. `[pick friendly vectors]` "then choose the most friendly vectors you can find."
4. `[express the objective as an equation, then manipulate]` "Then write down the objective and manipulate it, using cross products, dot products, and the huge number of pairs of perpendicular vectors to your advantage."
5. `[exploit unit lengths]` "Also exploit the fact that many vectors $v$ have unit lengths (so that $v\cdot v = 1$)." — the same trick as putting the origin at the circle's centre in Problem 5.1.
6. `[rewrite the finished proof]` "Eventually, once you have solved it, you can rewrite the proof and then see how a resolution by vectors can be abstractly neat."

**Why the hint is shaped that way.** The cube's three edge directions are mutually perpendicular unit vectors; the plane has a unit normal. So every quantity in sight is a dot or cross product of unit vectors, many of them perpendicular — a configuration in which vector algebra collapses long expressions to short ones.

*Sketch (my reconstruction, not in the book — included so you can see why the statement is even true).* Let $\mathbf n$ be the unit normal of the plane and let the cube's edge directions be the standard basis $\mathbf e_1,\mathbf e_2,\mathbf e_3$. The shadow on the perpendicular line has length $\lvert \mathbf n\cdot\mathbf e_1\rvert + \lvert \mathbf n\cdot \mathbf e_2\rvert + \lvert \mathbf n \cdot \mathbf e_3\rvert$, because the extreme corners of the cube in direction $\mathbf n$ are reached by walking along whichever of the three edges have a positive component. The shadow on the plane is bounded by the projections of the three faces facing $\mathbf n$, and the projected area of a face with unit normal $\mathbf e_i$ is $\lvert \mathbf n\cdot \mathbf e_i\rvert$. Summing the three visible faces gives the same expression. Hence equality. `[check small/degenerate cases first — Technique 5 from Problem 5.1]` Two concrete checks before you trust any of that. **Case $\mathbf n = (0,0,1)$** (project onto the floor $z=0$): the shadow on the floor is the unit square, area $1$; the shadow on the $z$-axis is a segment of length $1$. Equal ✓, and the formula gives $\lvert 0\rvert+\lvert 0\rvert+\lvert 1\rvert = 1$ ✓. **Case $\mathbf n = (1,1,1)/\sqrt3$** (project along the main diagonal): the shadow on the plane is a regular hexagon, and both quantities equal $3\cdot(1/\sqrt3) = \sqrt3 \approx 1.732$ ✓. These two cases also show why the answer is not a constant — the shadow's area ranges from $1$ (face-on) up to $\sqrt3$ (corner-on), so the theorem is asserting that a *varying* area tracks a *varying* length exactly, which is much more surprising than either being fixed.

**Transferable lesson.** The *last* item in Tao's hint is the least expected and the most valuable: once the computation works, go back and rewrite it. The first correct proof is rarely the illuminating one.

### Problem 5.2 (\*) — A big rectangle is cut into finitely many smaller rectangles (sides parallel to the big one). Every small rectangle has at least one side of integer length. Prove the big rectangle also has a side of integer length.

**Tao's exact wording** (p. 74): "A rectangle is partitioned into several smaller rectangles. Each of the smaller rectangles has at least one side of integer length. Prove that the big rectangle has at least one side of integer length."

*Two things the book leaves implicit, and you should notice them yourself.* (i) There is no explicit hypothesis that the small rectangles have sides **parallel** to the big one — but every figure has them parallel, the whole argument assumes it, and the statement is in fact false without it (a $1\times 1$ square can be cut into tilted rectangles whose "integer side" is measured along a diagonal direction, and a big rectangle with both sides irrational can be built this way). I have therefore put "sides parallel to the big one" into the heading above. (ii) "Several" means *finitely many*; an infinite partition would break the "sum of integers is an integer" step. This is exactly the vagueness Tao himself complains about two paragraphs later — "the question also has traces of topology, combinatorics, and worse, mainly because of the word 'partition'. It is a bit too general." Part of reading a competition statement well is spotting which of your convenient assumptions are actually stated and which you are importing.

The chapter shows a sample partition (p. 74): a big rectangle divided into about eight sub-rectangles of assorted sizes — a tall one running the full height down the right edge, a wide one across the top left, and a cluster of smaller ones of assorted shapes filling the middle and lower left, none of them lining up into a grid. Nothing about the partition is regular; that irregularity is the difficulty. (In particular the internal cut lines do not run all the way across — that is precisely what stops you from just slicing the big rectangle into strips and inducting.)

**The data / the objective.** Tao's framing, in order: "This is a pleasant-looking problem, so presumably there should be a pleasant solution. But the conclusion is a bit odd: if all the small rectangles have one integer side (or maybe more), why should the big rectangle have one?" (pp. 74–75)

**The chain of moves.**

1. `[aesthetic heuristic]` Short surprising statement ⟹ expect a short surprising proof. This sets the expectation that no heavy machinery is needed.
2. `[ask what is odd about the conclusion]` Interrogate the claim before trying to prove it. Why should a property of the parts transfer to the whole?
3. `[solve the lower-dimensional analogue]` In 1-D: a long segment cut into segments of integer length obviously has integer length, "so the length of the big segment is a sum of integers which is an integer, of course."
4. `[the analogue fails to generalize]` **Dead end acknowledged:** "This one-dimensional case does not immediately offer any help to the two-dimensional case clearly." `[harvest the ingredient]` But it leaves the clue: "we have to use the fact that the sum of integers is an integer." That is the only arithmetic the final proof will contain.
5. `[invent terminology]` Tao's connective is worth keeping because it links the abbreviation back to the clue: "One way we can use this fact immediately is to get some convenient notation: an 'integer side' means a side of integer length." The phrase you are about to write forty times gets a name *now*, before the argument starts, not after it has become unreadable. (Compare naming the objective $X$ in Problem 5.1 — same move, different object.)
6. `[diagnose the hard word]` "the question also has traces of topology, combinatorics, and worse, mainly because of the word 'partition'. It is a bit too general."
7. `[simplest non-trivial case]` Take the partition into just two rectangles by a single vertical cut. (Figure, p. 75: a rectangle with one vertical line, dividing it into a wider left piece and a narrower right piece.)
8. `[case analysis with an immediate-win branch]` The left piece has *some* integer side, horizontal or vertical — we don't know which. If it is **vertical**, then since the left piece spans the full height of the big rectangle, the big rectangle's vertical side is an integer and we are done. So we may assume it is **horizontal**.
9. Same argument for the right piece: assume its horizontal side is an integer. Then the big rectangle's width is the sum of two integers, hence an integer. Case closed.
10. `[mine the example for mechanism]` "But how did it work? (Examples are really only useful when they give some insight into how the general problem works.)" Two ingredients: **(a)** a case split, because each sub-rectangle can have a vertical or a horizontal integer side; **(b)** the only way to get a vertical integer side for the big rectangle is a "chain" of small rectangles with vertical integer sides that "add up" across it.
11. `[picture the chain]` The figure on p. 76 is the *same* eight-piece partition redrawn with a subset of the pieces shaded grey. The shaded pieces form a connected staircase that starts on the left edge, steps rightwards and downwards through the middle of the figure, and finishes on the tall shaded rectangle that occupies the whole right edge — so the shaded pieces' horizontal widths, laid end to end, span the full width of the big rectangle. Tao's caption: "here is an example, where the shaded rectangles have a horizontal integer side, so the big rectangle has to have a horizontal integer side as well." The figure is doing two jobs: it makes "chain" concrete, and by *re-using the earlier picture* it shows the chain is not a special feature of a contrived partition. (Reusing one running example instead of drawing a fresh one is itself a small expository technique — the reader does not have to re-parse a new configuration.)
12. `[write down a vague strategy]` Boxed, p. 76: *find either a chain of horizontal-integer-side rectangles or a chain of vertical-integer-side rectangles that "add up" into an integer side of the big rectangle.* Note the scare quotes — the strategy is deliberately imprecise at this stage.
13. `[name the remaining obstacle]` "But we have to find such a chain for every possible partition. Partitions are very ugly things. And each rectangle has a choice of a horizontal integer side, or a vertical integer side. Some rectangles may have both. Now how could we possibly find a system that will work for all these possibilities?"
14. `[justify why chains work]` If small rectangles with horizontal integer sides "link" from one end to the other, the big edge's length is the sum of the small edges' lengths. Tao's gloss: "if you stack some blocks on top of each other, the total height of the structure is the sum of the height of the blocks."
15. `[encode the choices as a colouring]` Green = has a horizontal integer side; red = has a vertical integer side; rectangles with both may be either (a bonus). Every small rectangle is now green or red, and the goal is: *a green chain connecting the two vertical edges, or a red chain connecting the two horizontal edges.*
16. `[proof by contradiction]` "No direct proof seems to be available, so let us try proof by contradiction." Suppose the two vertical edges are **not** connected by greens.
17. `[turn impossibility into structure]` Why not? Because reds blocked them — and a block must be a *solid barrier* of reds. But a solid barrier of reds necessarily runs from one horizontal edge to the other. So the reds connect the horizontal edges. Either way we have the chain we need.
18. `[recognize the pattern]` "Anyone who is familiar with the game Hex may see comparisons here."
19. `[flag the formal gap and sketch its closure]` The parenthetical on p. 77 (quoted in Technique 16): decompose the green region into connected components; assume none straddles both vertical edges; take the union of the left edge with all green components touching it; a thin strip just outside the boundary of that union must be red, and it straddles the two horizontal edges.
20. `[sweep up edge cases]` Superfluous rectangles in a chain — ditch them. Rectangles touching only at a corner — not a problem. Backwards-going chains — "we are subtracting integers instead of adding them on, but the grand total will always be an integer."

**The resolution.** Colour every small rectangle green (horizontal integer side) or red (vertical integer side). By the Hex-type blocking argument, either the greens form a left-to-right chain or the reds form a top-to-bottom chain. In the first case, adding and subtracting the horizontal integer sides along the chain shows the big rectangle's *width* is an integer; in the second case the same argument on the reds shows its *height* is an integer. Either way the big rectangle has an integer side. ∎

Notice what the proof never does: no coordinates, no areas, no clever integral. The only arithmetic fact used is the one harvested from the 1-D warm-up in step 4. (Tao's remark that "the question also has traces of topology" is honest — this is the one problem in the chapter whose key step is genuinely topological.)

**Transferable lesson.** When each of many objects independently makes an unknown binary choice, do not enumerate the $2^k$ possibilities: colour them and reason about the global shape of the colouring. And when the sub-case is solved, ask what the *proof* used, not what the answer was.

### Problem 5.3 (Taylor 1989, p. 8) — Finitely many points in the plane, no three on a line. Some are joined in pairs by segments, with each point on at most one segment. Repeatedly: take two segments $AB$ and $CD$ that cross, delete them, and draw $AC$ and $BD$ instead. Can this go on forever?

**Tao's exact wording** (p. 77): "On a plane we have a finite collection of points, no three of which are collinear. Some points are joined to others by line segments, but each point has at most one line segment attached to it. Now we perform the following procedure: We take two intersecting line segments, say $AB$ and $CD$, and remove them and replace them with $AC$ and $BD$. Is it possible to perform this procedure indefinitely?"

Two things to read carefully in that statement. First, *why $AC$ and $BD$ specifically?* Four points in general position admit exactly three ways of pairing them up, and exactly one of the three is the crossing one. So if $AB$ and $CD$ cross, then $A,B,C,D$ are the corners of a convex quadrilateral with $AB$ and $CD$ as its two **diagonals**, and the other two pairings — $\{AC, BD\}$ and $\{AD, BC\}$ — are the two pairs of **opposite sides**, both of which are non-crossing. The rule picks one of them. (Sanity check against Tao's figure, with $A$ upper-left, $B$ lower-right, $D$ lower-left, $C$ upper-right: the convex quadrilateral read round its boundary is $A, C, B, D$; $AC$ is its top side and $BD$ its bottom side, and they clearly miss each other.) The choice of *which* non-crossing pairing does not matter for the argument — the triangle-inequality step below works for either, because in any convex quadrilateral each pair of opposite sides is together shorter than the pair of diagonals. Knowing that the rule was one of two interchangeable options is worth a moment, because it tells you the specific letters in the statement are not carrying hidden information. Second, "Is it possible to perform this procedure indefinitely?" is a *yes/no* question about an unbounded process, which is the standard advertisement for a monovariant.

**The data / the objective.** A "matching" (each point used at most once) plus a rewrite rule that un-crosses two crossing segments. The question is whether the rewrite process can run indefinitely.

**The chain of moves.**

1. `[check the process is well defined]` "First of all, one should check that this procedure does not lead to any degenerate or ambiguous situations, in particular we do not want to create segments of zero length, or have two segments coincide with each other. This is why we have the condition 'each point has at most one line segment attached to it'." And the discipline: "it is easy to verify but should be done nevertheless (it could be a tricky question!)." — Every hypothesis in a problem statement is there for a reason; find the reason.
2. `[experiment, then decide the answer]` "After trying a few examples it seems that the assertion is plausible" — i.e. the process must stop. Observed behaviour: "The line segments seem to all hide away in the outer edges after a while and no longer intersect."
3. `[English first, mathematics second]` "That is easy to say in English, but how do we say that in Mathematics?" He coins the informal word **'outeredginess'** for the property he can see but not yet measure.
4. `[monovariant framework]` The plan: 'outeredginess' must increase with every move; it cannot increase forever "because there are only a finite number of configurations the system can reach. Eventually the 'outeredginess' should reach at a maximum and the procedure will stop. (That is, things stop when they cannot go any further.)" Formally, he needs
   - **(a)** a numerical characteristic of the configuration reflecting outeredginess. `[widen the candidate list]` Tao immediately names the *kinds* of thing that qualify: "It could be number of intersections, number of line segments, perhaps a sum of carefully chosen point scores (like a dart board)." The dart-board clause grants permission to invent a scoring function from nothing. `[operational test]` And he attaches a check the characteristic must pass: "it should be higher when all the edges are scattered on the rim."
   - **(b)** that characteristic must strictly increase (or at worst stay level, "but this is a lot weaker") with every move.
   `[mind the sign]` Note that the quantity he eventually settles on, total length, *decreases* — it is a proxy for the negative of outeredginess. Setting the framework up as "increase" and delivering a "decrease" is harmless (strict decrease of $Q$ = strict increase of $-Q$); what matters is that it moves one way only.
5. `[analogy from games]` The bracketed *Sprouts* aside: an "exit" is an unused edge at a point, each point starts with three, and each move (join two points, place a third on the new curve) uses up two exits and creates one — so the exit count falls by exactly one per move and "the game cannot last forever, because one will run out of exits." A fully worked example of the framework in a setting the reader may already know, and a demonstration that the monovariant can be an *invented bookkeeping quantity* rather than a natural geometric measurement.
6. `[guess simple candidates]` "There is no unique solution: several kinds of characteristics would satisfy (a) and (b). But we only need one. The best technique is just guessing something simple and hope that it works."
7. `[kill candidates]` **Dead end 1:** number of points — never changes. **Dead end 2:** number of lines — never changes. (An *invariant* is useless here; you need a *monovariant*.) **Dead end 3:** number of intersections — "looks promising, but the number of intersections does not always decrease with each application (although it should decrease in the long run)".
8. `[counterexample to kill a candidate]` The figure on p. 78 makes Dead End 3 concrete. On the left: segments $AB$ and $CD$ crossing in an X ($A$ upper-left to $B$ lower-right, $D$ lower-left to $C$ upper-right), with three short unrelated segments drawn above the crossing, none of them meeting $AB$ or $CD$. On the right: the same points after the swap, now joined as $A\!-\!C$ (a long near-horizontal segment across the top) and $D\!-\!B$ (a long near-horizontal segment along the bottom). The new segment $AC$ now cuts across all three of those short segments. "One point of intersection has become three." So intersection count can go *up*.
9. `[ask what actually decreases]` "After all, what decreases when one changes two intersecting lines into two non-intersecting lines? Somehow the lines become more separate."
10. `[reject the meaningful-but-uncomputable candidate]` **Dead end 4:** "sum of distances between line segments" — captures 'separation' but "this is not easy to do". `[prefer the computable]` "But in a similar spirit, we can eventually happen across 'sum of lengths of line segments'; not only do the line segments become more separate, they become *shorter* as well."
11. `[prove monotonicity via the triangle inequality]` "(The triangle inequality—the sum of two sides of a triangle is always longer than the third—shows that quite nicely.)"
12. `[finiteness closes the argument]` The total length strictly shrinks with every move, "and hence the operation cannot cycle or go on forever (as there are only a finite number of possibilities for the edges connecting the vertices, which are fixed)".
13. `[explain retrospectively why total length was findable]` The three-part narrowing argument of Technique 12: the move acts on individual segments, so the measure should be built from individual segments; segments have only length, position, and orientation; position and orientation have no preferred direction (clockwise versus counter-clockwise is not a real distinction) whereas "there is a definite difference between longer and shorter"; therefore "one almost is forced to use 'total length' ideas."

**The resolution.** Let $P$ be the crossing point of $AB$ and $CD$. Then
$$\lvert AC\rvert + \lvert BD\rvert < (\lvert AP\rvert + \lvert PC\rvert) + (\lvert BP\rvert + \lvert PD\rvert) = \lvert AB\rvert + \lvert CD\rvert,$$
by the triangle inequality applied to triangle $APC$ and to triangle $BPD$ (and the middle equality is just "$P$ lies on both segments", so $\lvert AP\rvert+\lvert PB\rvert = \lvert AB\rvert$ and $\lvert CP\rvert + \lvert PD\rvert = \lvert CD\rvert$). *Where the strictness comes from, spelled out:* the triangle inequality is strict unless the three points are collinear, so we need $A, P, C$ not collinear. Suppose they were. $P$ lies on the line $CD$, so the line $AC$ would then be the line $CD$, putting $A$ on line $CD$ — i.e. $A$, $C$, $D$ collinear, which the hypothesis "no three of which are collinear" forbids. Same argument for $B, P, D$. **So the "no three collinear" condition is not decoration: it is precisely what upgrades $\le$ to $<$, and without a strict decrease the whole monovariant argument collapses** (a non-strictly-decreasing quantity is compatible with an infinite loop). This is a second instance of the discipline from step 1 — every hypothesis is in the statement for a reason. In words: the two un-crossed segments are together strictly shorter than the two crossed ones — going straight beats detouring through the crossing point. So the total length of all segments strictly decreases at every step. Since the vertex set is fixed and finite, only finitely many configurations of segments exist, so only finitely many total lengths are possible; a strictly decreasing sequence through a finite set must terminate. Hence the procedure cannot be performed indefinitely. ∎

(Programmer's reading: total length is a loop variant taking values in a finite set, so the loop provably terminates. Note also that this argument gives no bound on *how long* the process runs — only that it stops. That is typical of monovariant proofs and is usually all you need.)

**Transferable lesson.** Three separate lessons, all explicit in the text. (i) "Can this go on forever?" is almost always a monovariant question. (ii) When choosing the monovariant, prefer the quantity that is *easy to compute* over the quantity that best expresses your intuition — sum of lengths beats sum of separations. (iii) You can *derive* rather than guess the monovariant: it must be built from whatever the move touches, and it must break a symmetry that the problem does not have.

### Problem 5.4 (Taylor 1989, p. 34, Q2) — A boy is at the centre of a square swimming pool; his teacher, who cannot swim, is at one corner. The teacher runs three times as fast as the boy swims, but the boy runs faster than the teacher. Can the boy escape? (Both are infinitely manoeuvrable.)

**Tao's exact wording** (p. 79): "In the centre of a square swimming pool is a boy, while his teacher (who cannot swim) is at one corner of the pool. The teacher can run three times faster than the boy can swim, but the boy can run faster than the teacher can. Can the boy escape from the teacher? (Assume both persons are infinitely manoeuvrable.)"

"Infinitely manoeuvrable" is doing real work and is worth unpacking, since it is the hypothesis that makes the problem clean: both agents may change direction instantaneously, with no turning radius and no acceleration penalty. That is what licenses the boy's abrupt 90° turn in the middle of the pool, and equally what licenses the teacher to reverse along the edge without cost. Without it the problem becomes a much messier optimal-control question. Note also what is *not* assumed: nothing about either player knowing the other's plan in advance — both simply see where the other is, continuously.

The figure (p. 79) is a square with corners labelled $A$ top-left, $B$ top-right, $C$ bottom-left, $D$ bottom-right, and $O$ marked with a small $\times$ at the centre (the boy). "So the boy starts at $O$, and the teacher starts at one of the corners—say $A$." `[exploit symmetry to fix a label]` That "say $A$" is a WLOG: all four corners are equivalent under the square's symmetry group, so naming one costs nothing.

*A note on the labelling — and how to diagnose this kind of clash.* The prose on pp. 80–82 uses the letters inconsistently with the figures. It calls the corner "furthest away from $A$" $C$ (in the figures that corner is $D$); it says the teacher runs "$A\to B\to C$ or $A\to D\to C$" (impossible with the figure's labels, since $A$ and $D$ are diagonally opposite); and it calls the midpoint of the right-hand edge "the midpoint $M$ of $B$ and $C$" and later "the midpoint $M$ of $BC$" (in the figures the right edge is $BD$).

The diagnosis is clean once you see it: **the prose is internally consistent under a *cyclic* labelling** $A$ top-left, $B$ top-right, $C$ bottom-**right**, $D$ bottom-**left** — going round the square. Under that reading everything checks out: $C$ really is diagonally opposite $A$; $A\to B\to C$ (along the top, then down the right) and $A\to D\to C$ (down the left, then along the bottom) really are the two two-side routes to it; $BC$ really is the right-hand edge, so its midpoint $M$ is the natural first target; "stranded in the $AB$ edge" is the top edge; and "the opposite $CD$ edge" is the bottom edge. Meanwhile **the two figures use a *grid* labelling** — $A$ top-left, $B$ top-right, $C$ bottom-left, $D$ bottom-right — which is what you would write if you were reading the square like a matrix. Somebody switched conventions between drawing and writing.

Because the figures are unambiguous and the arithmetic works out identically either way, I use the figures' grid labels below: $A$ top-left, $B$ top-right, $C$ bottom-left, $D$ bottom-right, $M$ = midpoint of the right edge $BD$, and "the opposite edge" = the bottom edge $CD$. `[technique]` The general lesson: when a text's letters stop making sense, do not assume you have misunderstood the mathematics — first check whether two different naming conventions have collided, then pick whichever one the numbers confirm and say out loud which you are using.

**The data / the objective.** Side length normalized to 1 (`[normalize units]`, p. 80). The teacher's speed is 3, the boy's swimming speed is 1. "Can the boy escape?"

**The chain of moves.**

1. `[decide the answer first]` "we should first decide for ourselves what the answer is going to be. (You cannot really search for a solution unless you know what you are searching for.)"
2. `[choose the cheaper proof obligation]` If the boy escapes, we need to exhibit *one* strategy. If the teacher wins, we must find "a strategy that would defeat every possible move the boy could make" — "a bit grim mathematically". `[dimension counting]` And the boy has two dimensions of freedom to the teacher's one, which suggests the boy. `[grade your own evidence]` Tao is careful to label the strength of this reasoning before he leans on it: "**Circumstantial evidence is a bit uncertain**" — he is not claiming to have shown the boy wins, only that the boy is the cheaper hypothesis to test. `[intelligent guess + verification]` And he names the shape of the work he is signing up for: "the first possibility is easier **without becoming too trial-and-errorish: we would have to intelligently guess a strategy, and then prove that it works.**" Guess-then-verify is a *method*; flailing through strategies is not, and the distinction is what makes the soft option respectable. So assume the boy escapes: "always tackle the soft options first".
3. `[reduce the objective and discard the used hypothesis]` Since the boy outruns the teacher on land, escaping = reaching the pool edge without being intercepted. "Now that we have shown that, the boy's running speed is now irrelevant."
4. `[prune the strategy space]` "Before we start guessing strategies, let us use some common sense to eliminate some bad strategies and isolate some promising tactics." The six common-sense prunings of Technique 18: boy always at top speed; never stops (stalemate is not a win for him); teacher hugs the edge (and is "no pushover"); straight lines are the fast way to move, though turns are explicitly *not* ruled out; the strategy must react to the teacher rather than being a "rather foolishly pre-determined plan"; and heading toward the teacher is out. Summary (p. 81): "The boy's best strategy would involve straight line bursts at top speed. Also, the strategy could be flexible to account for the actions of the teacher." Note the two words "bursts" and "flexible" — between them they already contain the answer (a burst, then a reassessment, then another burst), three paragraphs before it is found.
5. `[discard the obviously bad move first]` Before any arithmetic, one candidate is thrown out on sight: "Obviously the boy will have to move away from the teacher: **heading straight for $A$ is not a clever move**." Trivial, and Tao says it anyway — because writing down the worst option orients the search ("away from $A$") and costs one line.
6. `[test the obvious strategy]` **Dead end 1: swim to the far corner** — "the intuitive response then is to head straight for $C$, being the furthest away from $A$." The boy swims half a diagonal, $\sqrt2/2 \approx 0.707$. The teacher has **two** equally good routes, "$A\to B\to C$ or $A\to D\to C$, crossing two lengths of the pool to reach the boy's exit": distance $2$ either way, at speed 3, so "the teacher, being three times faster, will reach $C$ when the boy has only travelled $2/3 \approx 0.667$ units of length. So this approach does not work: the teacher arrives first." `[notice the symmetric target]` The fact that the teacher has two routes of *equal* length is not incidental — a corner is the one target the boy can pick that gives the teacher a free choice of side, which is the worst possible thing to hand a pursuer. Aiming at an edge point off-centre, by contrast, commits the teacher to one direction. That asymmetry is what the winning strategy will exploit.
7. `[reconsider the target]` "Sneakier manoeuvres are needed than simply running away. After all, people trying to leave a pool head for the edges, not for the corners."
8. `[test the next strategy]` **Dead end 2: swim to $M$, the midpoint of the right-hand edge.** The boy swims $1/2 = 0.5$. The teacher runs $A \to B \to M$, distance $1 + 0.5 = 1.5$, taking time $1.5/3 = 0.5$. Dead heat: "The teacher, being three times as fast, will just barely catch the kid as he is climbing out of the pool."
9. `[read the near-miss]` An exact tie is a signal. Either the constant 3 is "the barest minimum needed to stop the boy" or "the barest maximum which allows the boy to escape". "This complicates matters a bit, because the teacher's speed seems to be on a knife-edge here." He also notes what happens away from the knife-edge: marginally slower, and the naive edge-dash already wins; significantly faster, and the teacher could simply *stalk* the boy, "moving clockwise when the boy moves clockwise" (which is exactly the situation of Exercise 5.2).
10. `[stop hand-waving]` "Common sense fails to reach a verdict; we have to do some computation."
11. `[discover the forcing mechanism]` "If the boy makes for an edge, the teacher has to run continuously just to keep even. In other words, the boy is forcing the teacher's moves just by threatening to move in one direction. Being able to control, to some degree, the enemy moves can be a potent tool. Can we use it?"
12. `[phase 1 — swim toward $M$ and pin the teacher]` While the boy swims from $O$ toward $M$ at full speed, the teacher "cannot afford to do anything other than run towards $B$ and then towards $M$." If he deviates, the boy simply completes the trip and gets out first. So when the boy has travelled a distance $d = \lvert OX\rvert$ to an intermediate point $X$, the teacher must be at the point $Y$ on the top edge with $\lvert AY\rvert = 3\lvert OX\rvert$: any less and the boy could finish the run to $M$ and win; any more is impossible at speed 3.
13. `[don't cash the threat]` "Now is there any real need to continue all the way towards $M$? The threat of being able to go to $M$ was enough to keep the teacher where he is now, but threat and actuality are different things."
14. `[phase 2 — retry the failed move in the better configuration]` "Now that the teacher is stranded in the $AB$ edge, why does not the boy make a dash for the opposite $CD$ edge? It only takes half a length to reach the edge, and unlike the first time we considered the boy dashing for the edge, the teacher is badly placed."

**The figure (p. 82), described precisely.** A unit square, $A$ top-left, $B$ top-right, $C$ bottom-left, $D$ bottom-right. A tick labelled $Y$ sits on the top edge, roughly at its midpoint. At the centre, $O$ is marked with an $\times$, and a short horizontal segment runs from $O$ rightwards to a point labelled $X$. A tick labelled $M$ sits on the right-hand edge at its midpoint. So the boy has swum a short way east of centre; the teacher has been dragged from the corner $A$ to the middle of the top edge; the bottom edge is now wide open.

**The resolution, with the arithmetic.** Put coordinates $A=(0,1)$, $B=(1,1)$, $C=(0,0)$, $D=(1,0)$, $O=(0.5,0.5)$, $M=(1,0.5)$. Phase 1: the boy swims east to $X = (0.5+d,\,0.5)$; the teacher is forced to $Y = (3d,\,1)$ on the top edge. (Check that the threat is exactly binding: the boy's remaining distance to $M$ is $0.5-d$, and the teacher's remaining distance along the perimeter is $(1-3d)+0.5 = 1.5-3d$, which at speed 3 also takes time $0.5-d$. Dead even — the teacher may not deviate.) Phase 2: the boy turns 90° and swims straight south, a distance of $0.5$, exiting at $(0.5+d,\,0)$, arriving at time $0.5$ after the turn. The teacher must reach that same point along the perimeter, and has two routes:

- clockwise via $B$ and $D$: $(1-3d) + 1 + (0.5-d) = 2.5-4d$;
- anticlockwise via $A$ and $C$: $3d + 1 + (0.5+d) = 1.5+4d$.

At speed 3 he needs a distance under $3 \times 0.5 = 1.5$ to arrive first. The anticlockwise route is $1.5+4d > 1.5$ always, so that side is hopeless for him. The clockwise route beats $1.5$ only when $2.5-4d < 1.5$, i.e. $d > 1/4$. **So the boy escapes provided he turns while $d = \lvert OX\rvert < 1/4$** — for instance at $d = 1/6$, which is what the figure shows (it puts $Y$ at the middle of the top edge, i.e. $3d = 1/2$). Concretely with $d=1/6$: the boy needs time $0.5$; the teacher's best route is $2.5 - 4/6 \approx 1.833$, taking time $\approx 0.611$. The boy wins by a clear margin. "So, the boy escapes quite easily."

*One caution about the printed text — and a lesson about the phrase "easily shown".* Tao's actual sentence is: "In fact, if $X$ is a quarter-length or less from $M$, then it is easily shown that the teacher is just too far away to catch the boy. So, the boy escapes quite easily." He does not do the computation. Whenever a text says "it is easily shown", that is the exact spot to stop reading and do it yourself — it is where errors hide, and here there is one.

Page 82 states the condition as "if $X$ is a quarter-length or less from $M$". Taken literally with $\lvert OM\rvert = 0.5$ that would mean $d \ge 1/4$, which is the losing range — at $d=0.3$, for example, the teacher's clockwise route is $2.5-1.2 = 1.3 < 1.5$ and he catches the boy. The computation above, and the position of $Y$ in the accompanying figure, both indicate the intended condition is that $X$ be a quarter-length or less from **$O$** (equivalently, at least a quarter-length from $M$). The strategy and the conclusion are unaffected; only the printed inequality is off.

**Transferable lesson.** Three moves make this problem, and all three are exportable well beyond geometry. (i) *Choose which answer to prove* based on the cost of the proof, not on which feels more likely. (ii) *A threat is a resource.* Committing to a course of action forces your adversary into a known position; the value is in the forcing, not in the execution. (iii) *A move that fails is not necessarily a bad move.* The edge-dash lost by a hair at $t=0$ and won comfortably at $t=1/6$, because the configuration had changed in between. When an idea fails narrowly, ask what would have to be different for it to succeed — then engineer that difference.

### Exercise 5.2 (\*) — Same pool, but the teacher runs **six** times as fast as the boy swims. Show the boy cannot escape.

**Tao's hint** (p. 82): "Draw an imaginary square of sidelength $1/6$ unit centred at $O$. Once the boy leaves that square, the teacher gains the upper hand."

**The technique on display.** `[find the invariant region / critical threshold]` and `[a maintainable invariant as the pursuer's strategy]`. The forcing argument of Problem 5.4 is turned around: instead of the boy forcing the teacher, we look for a condition the *teacher* can maintain forever, plus a region inside which the boy is harmless.

*Sketch, reconstructed from the hint (not spelled out in the book).* The teacher's plan is to keep himself on the ray from the centre $O$ through the boy — i.e. to match the boy's *angular* position. Consider the shrunken square of side $1/6$, concentric with and parallel to the pool: it is the pool scaled by $1/6$. For each direction, the point where that direction's ray meets the small square is exactly $1/6$ of the way out to the corresponding point on the pool wall. So if the boy is at or beyond the small square, the pool-wall point on his ray sweeps around at most 6 times as fast as the boy's own angular motion demands — precisely the teacher's speed advantage. Hence, once the boy is outside the small square, the teacher can keep himself on the boy's ray permanently, and the boy can never reach a stretch of wall that the teacher is not already standing on. Inside the small square the boy can out-turn the teacher, but he is not near any wall, so it gains him nothing; and to reach a wall he must leave the square. Therefore he cannot escape.

**Transferable lesson.** In pursuit and in adversarial reasoning generally, look for a *maintainable relation* between the two agents (here: "same angle from the centre") and a *threshold* at which the pursuer can begin maintaining it. This is the mirror image of the monovariant: instead of a quantity that must decrease, you want a relation that can be preserved forever.

### Exercise 5.3 (\*\*) — Now make the pool circular. At the **same** speed ratio 3 the boy escapes easily (swim to the point diametrically opposite the teacher). What is the **minimum** teacher speed needed to catch the boy?

**Tao's exact wording** (p. 82): "Suppose the pool was circular instead of square. Now it is clear that the boy can escape (just head for the point opposite the teacher). But what if the teacher was faster? More precisely, what is the minimum speed of the teacher needed to catch the boy? This needs a fair whack of creativity (or knowledge of calculus of variations) for finding a lower bound (i.e. designing escape strategies for the boy) and for calculating an upper bound (which needs a perfect set of moves for the teacher). (One could also ask the same problem for the square pool, but that is even trickier than the circular problem.)"

**First, why is the escape "clear"?** `[put the two competing constants side by side]` The speeds are still the ones from Problem 5.4 — teacher $3\times$ the boy — and Tao does not restate them, so it is on you to redo the one-line comparison. Let the pool have radius $r$. The boy swims straight out along the diameter away from the teacher: distance $r$, so time $r$ at speed 1. The teacher must run halfway round the rim to meet him: distance $\pi r$, so time $\pi r / 3 \approx 1.047\,r$ at speed 3. Since $\pi > 3$, the boy wins — by about 4.7%. That is the entire content of "it is clear". Now compare with the square, using the same "how much further does the teacher have to run than the boy has to swim?" ratio:

| Pool | Boy's swim to the antipodal exit | Teacher's run to that exit | Ratio | Speed 3 enough for the teacher? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Square, side 1 | $\sqrt2/2 \approx 0.707$ | $2$ | $\approx 2.83$ | yes ($3 > 2.83$) — boy caught |
| Circle, radius $r$ | $r$ | $\pi r$ | $\pi \approx 3.14$ | no ($3 < 3.14$) — boy escapes |

**Rounding off the pool flipped the answer purely by moving one constant from $2.83$ to $3.14$**, with the fixed speed $3$ sitting between them. Problems in this family are decided by which of two innocuous constants is bigger, and *finding the pair to compare* is most of the solution. It is also a good demonstration that "the teacher is 3 times faster" was not an arbitrary choice by the problem setter — it is the value tuned to sit just on the interesting side of $2.83$ for the square (hence the exact tie the boy has to out-think in Problem 5.4) and just on the losing side of $\pi$ for the circle.

**The technique on display.** `[two-sided bounds for an optimal constant]`. To *evaluate* a threshold — as opposed to deciding a yes/no question — you must attack it from both sides, and the two sides require completely different work:

- **Lower bound on the critical speed** = an *escape strategy* for the boy that works for every teacher speed below some value. This is a construction: exhibit a strategy and verify it.
- **Upper bound on the critical speed** = a *capture strategy* for the teacher, valid against every boy strategy. This is a universal statement, and much harder — exactly the asymmetry Tao exploited in Problem 5.4 when he chose to prove the boy escapes.

Tao also flags the *style* of the answer: an optimal continuous strategy is a calculus-of-variations problem (find the best *path*, not the best *point*), which is why "a fair whack of creativity" is the alternative to machinery.

**Why "calculus of variations" and not just calculus.** Calculus 1 optimizes over *numbers*: you have a function $f(x)$ and you set $f'(x)=0$. Here the unknown is not a number but a whole *path* — the curve the boy swims as a function of time — and the thing you are optimizing is a functional of that curve. That is the subject called calculus of variations. Tao's parenthetical "(or knowledge of calculus of variations)" is therefore a warning about the *type* of the unknown, and his alternative — "a fair whack of creativity" — means: guess the shape of the optimal path from the geometry and then verify it, rather than deriving it. This is the same guess-then-verify posture he adopted in Problem 5.4, one level up.

*Context, outside the book (so that the shape of the real answer is not a mystery).* This is the classical "man and lion in a circular pond" / "guard and swimmer" problem. The critical speed ratio is $\approx 4.6033$, the root of a transcendental equation — no nice closed form. The known optimal boy strategy is genuinely two-phase, and the phases echo Problem 5.4 exactly: (1) *spiral outwards*, staying on the largest circle on which he can still out-turn the teacher angularly — this is the analogue of the forcing phase, and it ends with the teacher's position pinned relative to his own; then (2) *dash radially* for the rim once the angular gap is maximal — the analogue of the 90° turn. The reason the constant is ugly is that the crossover radius and the dash have to be optimized against each other. And notice: the fact that a spiral-then-dash beats a straight dash is precisely why the answer is not simply $\pi$.

**Transferable lesson.** "Is it possible?" and "what is the threshold?" are different species of question. The second always costs you two proofs — one constructive, one universal — and you should expect them to use unrelated ideas.

## What this chapter teaches about problem solving in general

1. **The branch of mathematics a problem is filed under is not the branch that solves it.** Every problem here is geometric, and none is solved with a geometry theorem. Read the *objective*, not the packaging, and then choose the branch whose identities match the objective's shape. Squares of lengths → vectors. "Can this go on forever?" → monovariants. Unknown binary choices → colourings. Two agents with speeds → arithmetic and strategy.
2. **Most of the work is reformulation, and that is not a detour.** In Problem 5.1 the actual computation is four lines; the preceding three pages convert "sum of squares of distinct lengths" into "$Y = \lvert A_1A_1\rvert^2 + \cdots + \lvert A_1A_n\rvert^2$". Name the target, express it as an equation, choose canonical representatives, symmetrize, add a zero, change variable to something prettier. Each step is small, cheap, and reversible, and together they turn an unattackable question into a mechanical one.
3. **Spend your free choices deliberately.** You may put the origin anywhere, choose your units, choose which vertex to sum from, choose which of two colours a doubly-qualified rectangle gets. Tao never takes the default; he ranks the options and states why. "The most logical choice would be to put the origin at the centre of the circle. (The second best choice would be to make $A_1$ the origin.)"
4. **Dead ends are data, and Tao keeps the receipts.** Swim straight at the teacher (rejected on sight), number of points (invariant, useless), number of lines (ditto), number of intersections (can increase — with a counterexample figure), sum of inter-segment distances (uncomputable), swim to the far corner (loses by 0.04, and hands the teacher two equally good routes), swim to the edge midpoint (exact tie), the 1-D analogue of Problem 5.2 (does not generalize, but yields the one arithmetic fact the proof needs). Each rejection narrows the search: the far-corner failure says "head for edges, not corners"; the tie says "the constant 3 is critical"; the intersection-count failure says "measure segments, not intersections"; the 1-D failure says "the only arithmetic you get is that integers add to integers".
5. **A near-miss is a message.** When a strategy fails by a hair or ties exactly, the problem is telling you its constants were chosen at the boundary — and that a small extra idea will tip it. When a strategy fails by a mile, the whole idea is wrong. Distinguish the two before deciding whether to persevere.
6. **You can often *derive* the clever quantity instead of guessing it.** Tao's best paragraph in the chapter (p. 79) narrows the hunt for a monovariant to a unique candidate by three arguments: the measure must match the granularity of the move; the list of possible properties is short and enumerable; and the measure must break a symmetry the problem does not possess ("why clockwise and not counter-clockwise?"). Symmetry rules *out* candidates as powerfully as it proves theorems.
7. **Choose which statement to prove based on proof cost.** Existence needs one witness; universality needs to defeat everything. Before committing, ask which side of a dichotomy you would rather be on. "Always tackle the soft options first—you may then be able to dodge a load of hard work later on. (This is not laziness, but practicality.)"
8. **Keep intuition and rigour in separate columns, and say which is which.** Tao routinely gives the picture-argument first ("the vectors pull in all directions"), labels it hand-waving, then supplies the real proof (rotate by $360°/n$). He does the same with the Hex-style blocking argument, and he lists the leftover edge cases out loud rather than hoping nobody notices. He also grades his heuristics as he uses them — "not a strong conjecture at present", "Circumstantial evidence is a bit uncertain", "it is unlikely that…". Intuition earns you the idea; only the second column earns you the theorem.
9. **Every hypothesis is load-bearing; go find out what it bears.** "Each point has at most one line segment attached to it" is what keeps the rewrite from producing zero-length or coincident segments (p. 77); "no three of which are collinear" is what makes the triangle inequality *strict*, which is what makes the monovariant work at all; "infinitely manoeuvrable" is what licenses the boy's instantaneous 90° turn; the reason the answer asks for *squares* of lengths is that squares are polynomial and lengths are not. Tao's discipline on the first of these — "it is easy to verify but should be done nevertheless (**it could be a tricky question!**)" — is the general rule. Read the statement, list the hypotheses, and for each one ask "what breaks without this?" You will usually find you have just located the crux.
10. **Coin a word for the thing you cannot yet measure, then give the word a test.** 'Outeredginess' is not mathematics, and Tao knows it — "That is easy to say in English, but how do we say that in Mathematics?" But naming it lets him state a checkable requirement ("it should be higher when all the edges are scattered on the rim"), and the requirement is what lets him screen candidates. Precision is the *destination*; a working name for the vague idea is how you travel.
11. **Read the printed page adversarially.** This chapter alone contains a misprinted formula ($m = (n+1/2)$ on p. 71, corrected in the box on p. 72), a dropped subscript in a displayed equation (p. 72), a labelling convention that switches between figure and prose (pp. 79–82), and an "it is easily shown" whose stated condition is the wrong way round (p. 82). None of these damages the mathematics, and all of them will damage *you* if you assume the text is right and your understanding is wrong. Do the arithmetic yourself; when text and figure disagree, believe whichever the numbers confirm; and treat "clearly", "obviously" and "easily shown" as bookmarks for work you still owe.
12. **Direction is a convention; monotonicity is the content.** Tao's monovariant recipe asks for a quantity that *increases* and his answer *decreases*, because 'outeredginess' and 'total length' point opposite ways. Nothing is wrong: $Q$ strictly increasing and $-Q$ strictly decreasing are the same fact. Set the framework up whichever way reads more naturally and flip the sign at the end — but never let the quantity wobble, because "sometimes up, sometimes down" is exactly the failure that killed 'number of intersections'.
