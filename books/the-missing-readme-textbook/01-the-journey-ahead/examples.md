# Chapter 1 Examples: The Journey Ahead

### Cunningham's Law Applied to Team Documentation

New engineers are advised to write down their team's conventions, onboarding procedures, and other oral traditions — knowledge that lives only in people's heads. The draft will attract a flood of comments and corrections, and that is exactly the point: a perfect document isn't the goal, a triggered discussion is. This deliberately invokes Cunningham's law, the internet adage that the best way to get the right answer online is not to ask a question but to post the wrong answer, because people correct errors more eagerly than they answer questions. **Lesson:** Publish imperfect documentation early and let corrections flesh it out, without taking the feedback personally.

### Parkinson's Bike Shed Allegory

C. Northcote Parkinson described a committee assigned to review designs for a power plant. The committee approves the plant plans within minutes because they are too complex for anyone to meaningfully discuss. The same committee then spends forty-five minutes debating what materials to use for the bike shed next to the plant, because everyone can hold an opinion about a bike shed. The chapter warns that this pattern — trivial discussions becoming drawn out — appears constantly in technical work, including in responses to the documentation you write. **Lesson:** Expect disproportionate debate on trivial matters (bike-shedding) and don't let it discourage or derail you.

### The Journey Map: Five Stops from Newbie to Competent Engineer

The chapter frames the beginner's path as a voyage across an imaginary landscape. You begin at Peak Newb, where all newcomers start with orientation and setup. You travel down Ramp-Up River as you start coding and learning local conventions and processes. You arrive at Cape Contributor when you ship meaningful features. Shipping forces you to sail the storms of Operations Ocean, where software meets real users and on-call duty. Finally you land in the safe haven of Competence Cove, where the team trusts you to drive projects. **Lesson:** Growth from new hire to full-fledged engineer follows predictable stages, each with its own focus and skills.

### The Deliberately Tiny First Task

A new engineer should get a small starter task to learn how a code change travels from edit to production. If no one assigns one, the chapter says to look or ask for a useful but minor change — something as small as updating a code comment qualifies. The point of the exercise is to walk every step of the delivery process, not to demonstrate brilliance. **Lesson:** Use your first change to learn the path to production; the goal is to understand the steps, not to impress.

### When There's No New Hire Program: Ask for the Org Chart

Many companies run new hire orientations that explain how the company works, tour the organization, introduce leadership, and connect you with new hires from other departments who become future colleagues. The chapter offers a fallback for companies without one: ask your manager to explain the org chart — who is in charge of what and who reports to whom — plus the different departments and how they relate, and take notes during the conversation. **Lesson:** If structured orientation doesn't exist, manufacture it yourself by interviewing your manager about the organization.

### Creating the Onboarding Process That Doesn't Exist

Some companies have an engineer-specific onboarding process covering system access, development environment setup, and checking out and building code. If no such process exists, the chapter reframes the gap as an opportunity: write down each step as you figure it out, and your setup notes become the onboarding process for the next hire. **Lesson:** A missing process is a chance for a brand-new engineer to make a visible, lasting contribution from day one.
