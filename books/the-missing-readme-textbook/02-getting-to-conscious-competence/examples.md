# Chapter 2 Examples: Getting to Conscious Competence

### Broadwell's Four Stages of Competence
Martin M. Broadwell, in *Teaching for Learning*, defines four stages of skill: unconscious incompetence (you can't do the task and don't know it), conscious incompetence (you can't do it but know it), conscious competence (you can do it with effort), and unconscious competence (you do it effortlessly). The chapter uses this model as its frame: every engineer starts a new job in one of the first two stages, because even a hypothetical know-everything engineer must still learn company-specific processes and rules. The new engineer's goal is to reach the third stage — conscious competence — as quickly as possible. **Lesson:** Starting out incompetent is universal and expected; competence is a staged progression you can deliberately accelerate.

### Facebook's Six-Week Boot Camp
Front-loaded learning is so valuable that some companies design an explicit learning curriculum for new hires. Facebook famously puts new engineers through a six-week "boot camp" before they settle into a team. **Lesson:** Spending your first months learning rather than shipping is a recognized investment, not slacking — major companies institutionalize it.

### The Day Chris Deleted All of the Code
At one of Chris's first internships, a senior engineer showed him how to check his finished changes into CVS, the company's revision control system. Chris followed the instructions, blindly running through branching, tagging, and merging steps, then went home. The next morning everyone was somber: he had corrupted the entire CVS repository, losing all of the company's code, and the team had spent the night recovering most of it (everything except Chris's commits and a few others). His manager pulled him aside and told him not to worry — Chris had done the right thing by working with a senior engineer, and mistakes happen; every engineer has a version of this story. **Lesson:** Do your best to understand what you're doing, but accept that serious mistakes happen to everyone — teams maintain safety nets, so learn the lesson and move on instead of beating yourself up.

### A Unit Test vs. a High-Traffic Database Index
The chapter calibrates "appropriate caution" with a comparison: you can afford to be fast and loose when writing a unit test, but you must be slow and careful when altering indexes on a high-traffic database. Same engineer, different stakes, different speed. **Lesson:** Match your level of caution to the potential impact of the change.

### Discovering a Hidden Call Path
The chapter's illustration of why nonproduction experiments permit invasive techniques: suppose you know a method is invoked but can't determine how it's reached. Outside production you can simply throw an exception, print a stack trace, or attach a debugger to expose the call path — tactics you'd never use on live systems. **Lesson:** Because experiments run outside production, they are safe, and you can use aggressive tricks to answer questions documentation and coworkers can't.

### The Distinctive Startup Print Statement
A silly but surprisingly useful experimentation trick: add a distinctive print statement at the very beginning of your program's execution. If you don't see it, you're running the original binary, not your modified version. This saves hours spent chasing "mysterious" behavior that turns out to be the unmodified program running all along. **Lesson:** Before debugging confusing behavior, verify that the code executing is actually the code you changed.

### Ron Jeffries: "Code Never Lies. Comments Sometimes Do."
The chapter cites Ron Jeffries's line to justify reading source code rather than relying only on design documents and comments: code doesn't always match the design doc, and comments can be wrong while code, by definition, does exactly what it does. **Lesson:** When documentation and behavior conflict, the code is the ground truth — read it.

### Dmitriy and the Decompiled Java Application
Early in his career, Dmitriy was handed a legacy Java application and told to "figure it out" — he was the only person on the team even moderately comfortable with Java. The code was a minefield: variables named a, b, c (with the same value named differently across functions), no source control history, no tests, original developer long gone. He coped by blocking out distractions, reading carefully, renaming variables, tracing logic, sketching on paper, and experimenting — and he grew to admire whoever had kept this complexity in their head. When he voiced that admiration over lunch, a colleague stared at him: the original source was lost, and Dmitriy had been working on decompiler output — no human ever wrote code that way. **Lesson:** Reading unfamiliar code demands going slowly, reading for comprehension, and never trusting variable names — and careful technique can carry you through even absurdly hostile codebases.

### Crashing the Academic Party
Dmitriy and his colleague Peter Alvaro were struggling to make their data warehouse perform and suspected distributing aggregation across a cluster of commodity servers was the answer. While researching, Peter found Google's recently released MapReduce paper — Google was doing exactly what they proposed. Hungry for people to discuss the papers with, Dmitriy discovered that UC Berkeley's database group hosted lunches technically open to the public, and the pair became regulars (politely waiting until students had their fill of the free pizza), eventually even contributing to conversations. Their learning shifted into high gear: Peter enrolled in Berkeley's PhD program and is now a professor at UC Santa Cruz, Dmitriy also went to grad school, and the expensive data warehouse was replaced by Hadoop two years after they left. **Lesson:** If you stop learning at work, seek outside communities — university programs open to the public can expand your circle and expose you to ideas ahead of industry (grad school optional).

### The #sw-helping-sw Channel
Dmitriy's company runs a group chat channel called #sw-helping-sw where engineers post questions for anyone to answer. It illustrates multicast, asynchronous question-asking in practice: many potential responders, answers visible to all, and the discussion discoverable by anyone who hits the same problem later. **Lesson:** Ask questions in shared group channels rather than private messages so anyone can answer and everyone can benefit.

### Pankaj's Flaky Test Question: Poor vs. Good
The chapter contrasts two versions of the same ask. The poor version: "Hey Alice, any idea why testKeyValues is failing in TestKVStore? It really slows down our builds to rerun this." — it gives Alice almost nothing to work with, vaguely sounds like he's blaming her, and reads as lazy. The good version adds context (the test is in the DistKV repo; Shaun referred him to Alice), symptoms (fails roughly every third run, seemingly at random), what he ruled out (it fails in isolation, so it's not test interaction; Shaun couldn't reproduce it in a loop on his machine), a hypothesis (looks like a race condition), impact and urgency (not affecting production, but the flapping test costs 20–30 minutes per occurrence), and attached logs plus environment settings. Alice gets a real starting point — and remembers that Pankaj is thorough, which builds his credibility. **Lesson:** Showing your work in a question — context, symptoms, what you tried, hypothesis, impact, supporting data — gets you better help and builds your reputation; the extra writing effort is worth it.

### Headphones, Lounge Spaces, and "Don't Interrupt" Conventions
Offices signal availability differently: headphones, ear plugs, or earmuffs universally mean "don't interrupt," but lounge spaces are ambiguous — some people work away from their desks precisely so they won't be found, while other offices treat anyone in a shared space as fair game. The chapter also notes that walking up to someone forces a response: even a quick "I'm busy" has already broken their focus. **Lesson:** Learn your company's specific do-not-disturb conventions, and default to asynchronous channels because physical interruption always costs the other person focus.

### The 1978 Clance and Imes Impostor Study
Impostor syndrome was first described in a 1978 study by Drs. Pauline Rose Clance and Suzanne Ament Imes, "The Impostor Phenomenon in High Achieving Women: Dynamics and Therapeutic Intervention." It found that despite outstanding academic and professional accomplishments, sufferers persist in believing they are not bright and have fooled anyone who thinks otherwise — numerous achievements that should serve as objective evidence do nothing to dent the impostor belief. The authors connect this to engineers who disbelieve praise even while being promoted, insisting they got lucky or that the bar was too lax. **Lesson:** Impostor feelings are a documented, common phenomenon that objective success alone won't cure — it must be consciously recognized and countered.

### Reframing the Daria Thought
The chapter demonstrates reframing negative self-talk with a one-line rewrite: "I had to bug Daria to help with a race condition" becomes "I reached out to Daria, and now I know how to resolve race conditions!" Same event, but the second framing records growth instead of shame. **Lesson:** Deliberately rewording negative thoughts into statements of learning breaks the impostor-syndrome cycle and builds confidence.

### The Overconfident Engineer (Dunning–Kruger in Action)
The chapter sketches the Dunning–Kruger engineer: storming around critiquing the company's technical stack, complaining about code quality, and belittling design, certain their ideas are right, with a default mode of pushing back on or disregarding all feedback. Rejecting every suggestion is the giant red flag — complete confidence is the sign of a blind spot, because the unconsciously incompetent can't accurately evaluate their own performance or anyone else's. **Lesson:** Total certainty signals a blind spot; counter it by cultivating curiosity, asking why decisions were made, and thinking in trade-offs rather than right and wrong.
