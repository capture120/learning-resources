# Chapter 14 Examples: Navigating Your Career

### Valve's Handbook for New Employees defines T-shaped people
The "T-shaped" concept comes from Valve's famous *Handbook for New Employees* (valvesoftware.com/en/publications), which describes T-shaped people as both generalists — highly skilled at a broad set of valuable things, the top of the T — and experts who are among the best in their field within one narrow discipline, the vertical leg. The chapter adopts this as the model for engineers: work effectively in most areas, be an expert in at least one.
**Lesson:** Combine broad competence across specialties with deep expertise in one rather than choosing pure generalism or pure specialization.

### Camille Fournier's *The Manager's Path*
Career ladders typically split into management and individual contributor tracks at the staff level, and managing people is a wholly different skillset from engineering — advancing does not require it. For anyone considering the management track, the recommended preview is Camille Fournier's book *The Manager's Path* (O'Reilly Media, 2017).
**Lesson:** Management is a career change rather than a promotion, so study what it really entails before choosing that track.

### The teammate who doesn't know what a monad is
A quick scenario about judging colleagues: just because a teammate doesn't know what a monad is doesn't mean they aren't good at something else — everyone has their own mix of expertise and gaps. The same grace applies inward: when you find yourself the novice in a conversation, you still have other areas of comparative strength.
**Lesson:** Judge teammates and yourself by overall strengths, not by one missing piece of knowledge.

### Product teams vs. infrastructure teams
A contrast in team composition: product development teams are likely to have varied areas of depth among teammates, while infrastructure teams are more likely to share one common expertise. Either way, a good team has a solid mix of T-shaped people.
**Lesson:** Healthy teams blend breadth and depth, and the right mix depends on the kind of team.

### Specialists arrive as the company grows
A scenario of company scaling: as a company grows it increasingly hires specialists for each area, which shrinks the territory available to generalists and pushes everyone already there to specialize too. A T-shaped engineer adapts gracefully by leaning more on their specialty — and because some incoming specialists lack breadth, the T-shaped veteran can help them understand their surroundings and become effective.
**Lesson:** Breadth plus depth lets you survive specialization waves and makes you a guide for narrow specialists.

### Engineering programs to join — or create
Concrete examples of engineering programs: hiring, interviewing, brown bags, conferences, meetups, reading groups, open source projects, and apprenticeship and mentoring programs. You can participate by attending or by leading — and if your company has no well-organized programs, create one by pitching management and recruiting passionate teammates. The reward is relationships, visibility across the organization, new skills, and influence on company culture.
**Lesson:** Engineering programs are a career lever — participate, lead, or build them yourself.

### Probing for real feedback instead of reassurance
Two model questions for promotion self-assessment that dig past vague praise. First: "If you compare my design docs to those by some Level 3 engineers, are there differences that are apparent?" Second, after hearing "you write good tests": "Which tests that I wrote do you find good? What's an example of less-good tests? Who at our company writes *great* tests? How is what I do different from what they do?"
**Lesson:** Ask comparative, specific feedback questions so you uncover actual gaps rather than collecting reassurance.

### Team shuffling leaves you with nothing to show
A scenario explaining why promotion feedback might not match your self-image: a reorganization shuffled your team and left you without a completed project to point to, so reviewers have an incomplete view of your work or don't perceive it as valuable. Alternatively, your self-evaluation may simply be off and need recalibration. Either way, the fix is a candid conversation with your manager.
**Lesson:** Disagreeable feedback often stems from incomplete views of your work — investigate the source instead of dismissing it.

### The promising but unfinished project
A common promotion disappointment: an engineer expects credit for an impressive project that isn't done, but managers want results, not potential. Related wrong expectations: technical skills alone aren't sufficient (you must work well with others, contribute to team goals, and help the organization), and promotion isn't a function of time — one year in the job or five, impact is what matters. Once you perform at the next level, expect three to six months sustaining it before the promotion lands, to prove you consistently meet the bar.
**Lesson:** Promotions reward finished, impactful work sustained over time — not promise, raw skill, or tenure.

### Starting the promotion conversation at the halfway point
Two timings for raising promotion with your manager, contrasted. Start around the halfway point — before you think you're ready — and you both get time to align and close gaps. Wait until you're convinced you already deserve the promotion, and if your manager disagrees, the discussion becomes a conflict to resolve instead of a plan to build.
**Lesson:** Raise promotion early so the conversation is collaborative planning rather than dispute resolution.

### Tanya Reilly's "glue work" and the junior engineer trap
Tanya Reilly's talk and blog post on glue work (noidea.dog/glue) addresses a career-ladder blind spot. Staff-level work explicitly requires glue work — coordination, process improvements, documentation, communication — but at senior level and below, ladders often measure pure coding ability. Junior engineers who take on this essential noncoding work have no Git commits attached to it: they spend less time coding, their promotions get delayed, or they get pushed toward a different role such as project management. Reilly's advice: stop doing glue work, even if it hurts the team in the short term, if your manager doesn't see it as a path to promotion. It's a tough pill and may seem unfair, but the onus for making things fair is on management, not you.
**Lesson:** Don't let uncredited glue work quietly derail a junior engineering career; valuing it correctly is management's responsibility.

### Short stints as a hiring red flag
How hiring managers read a résumé full of brief jobs: they worry the candidate will leave as soon as the going gets tough and the initial "honeymoon" period is over. Constant switching also robs engineers of seeing their decisions play out over the long term, stunting the intuition senior roles depend on.
**Lesson:** Frequent job changes damage both your reputation with hiring managers and the long-horizon judgment that makes engineers senior.

### The grass-is-greener illusion (FOMO)
From the outside, other companies appear to have newer technology, cooler problems, and none of your current company's complications. This is an illusion — all companies have problems. The antidotes to fear of missing out: attend meetups and conferences (employers will often pay for career development or even schooling), do open source or side projects if your schedule allows, or stay at the same company and switch teams.
**Lesson:** FOMO is not a reason to quit; get fresh exposure through cheaper channels than a job change.

### Good reasons to leave after a short stint
Legitimate early exits, listed concretely: a company or team that's a bad fit (better to get out quickly than let a bad situation drag on); an exceptional opportunity, since those don't arrive on a convenient schedule; and the value of exposure to different technology stacks, coworkers, and engineering organizations.
**Lesson:** Bad fit, rare opportunity, and genuine growth justify leaving early — boredom and FOMO don't.

### Catching up to market salary by switching
Engineering salaries have been growing rapidly, and a company's raises may not keep pace with the market — sadly, it's often easier to catch up to market rates by switching jobs. The counterweight: stick with your job if you're still properly compensated, growing, and learning, because there is great value in seeing teams, companies, and software evolve over time.
**Lesson:** A pay gap against the market is a valid reason to move, but don't leave a job where you're paid fairly, growing, and learning.

### The longtime engineer as company historian
An illustration of job ossification: engineers with long tenure naturally become historians who educate others about how things work, who knows what, and why things were done the way they were. That knowledge is genuinely valuable — it's even part of being a staff engineer — but it becomes a trap when your value comes more from past work than current contributions. Changing companies and landing in a new environment can kickstart growth again.
**Lesson:** Leave when your worth rests on remembering the past instead of contributing in the present.

### Sleeping bags under desks and the "Need for Sleep" study
Engineering culture sometimes glorifies 14-hour workdays and sleeping bags under desks, but marathon coding sessions and lack of sleep damage your code, your health, and your relationships. The cited evidence: "Need for Sleep: The Impact of a Night of Sleep Deprivation on Novice Developers' Performance" (*IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering*, 2020) found that a single night of sleep deprivation leads to a 50 percent reduction in the quality of implementations. Occasional long hours happen, but they must not become a habit or, worse, an identity — the debt comes due.
**Lesson:** Overwork is self-defeating — one sleepless night halves the quality of what you build.

### Vacation cadences and sabbaticals
Even with healthy hours, the monthly grind can burn you out, and engineers structure recovery differently: some take one large vacation at the end of the year, while others take quarterly vacations to stave off fatigue. Most companies cap the vacation days you can accrue, so unused days are wasted. Some companies also offer sabbaticals — typically one-to-three-month extended breaks — to explore and refresh.
**Lesson:** Find a rest cadence that works for you and actually use your vacation; recovery is part of a marathon career.

### Industries software engineering can serve
The closing illustration of the career's reach: software engineers can contribute to any industry — from science to farming, health, entertainment, or even space exploration — and their work can improve billions of lives. The parting advice: work with people you like on problems you're passionate about, and you can accomplish great things.
**Lesson:** The career's breadth is enormous; choose your people and problems well, and the impact follows.
