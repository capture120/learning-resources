# Chapter 6: Managing Multiple Teams — Examples and Anecdotes

## The Rent the Runway engineering director ladder
**Situation:** Fournier needed to define where multi-team management begins on a career ladder, and wrote the engineering director description for her organization at Rent the Runway.
**What happened / what was done:** The ladder deliberately states that directors are not generally expected to write code day to day, while still demanding a strong technical background: debugging and triaging critical systems, doing code reviews, asking the business and product questions during architecture work, refining engineering standards and processes, owning recruiting and headcount planning, growing the next generation of leaders, and balancing product work against technical debt. It also calls out cross-functional collaboration and communication in both directions as core to the role.
**Lesson:** The director role stays technical, but the technical work shifts from writing code to guarding the organization's technical competence, processes, and goal-setting.

## Ten years to fluency before management
**Situation:** Fournier reflects on when it's safe to step away from full-time coding without losing the ability to engage technically.
**What happened / what was done:** She spent roughly ten years, including undergraduate and graduate study, reaching deep fluency in programming before moving into management. Her test for others: after a brief ramp-up with standard tools and frameworks, could you productively contribute to a good codebase in at least one language?
**Lesson:** Deep knowledge atrophies but language fluency endures; mastering programming before management is what makes it possible to stay credibly hands-on later, and to debug team issues by feel.

## Ask the CTO: I miss code!
**Situation:** A manager of two complex teams finds management is forcing them back from technical work, misses coding terribly, and wonders if this means they shouldn't be a manager.
**What happened / what was done:** The advice: nearly everyone who leaves a heavily hands-on role questions the move at first. Check whether you've absorbed the tech industry's belief that management isn't a real job — it is, and it's your job now. Coding offers constant quick wins (passing tests, compiling features); management offers few obvious quick wins to a newcomer, so nostalgia is natural, like missing school in your first full-time job.
**Lesson:** Missing code is a normal transition symptom, not a verdict. Becoming a great manager requires giving up some technical focus; the question is whether you'll consciously accept that tradeoff.

## Email's red dot and the urgency trap
**Situation:** Managers of multiple teams lose their days to things that feel urgent, like email and chat.
**What happened / what was done:** Fournier observes that the red notification dot makes email feel urgent when it almost never is — it's one of the worst vehicles for genuinely time-sensitive information. Likewise, chat is almost as bad as email for conveying truly urgent information, at least on a collocated team; moving communication into chat systems like Slack and HipChat hasn't eliminated communication; the words just flow elsewhere, and the constant trickle can be even more distracting. People also treat calendar meetings as "obvious" places to be, mistaking obviousness for urgency.
**Lesson:** Urgency is felt more sharply than importance. Time management at this level means noticing that substitution and deliberately protecting important-but-not-urgent work.

## Reading the room in boring meetings
**Situation:** A multi-team manager is tempted to drop out of internal meetings where she isn't clearly needed.
**What happened / what was done:** Fournier warns against overusing that strategy at this level. During meetings, she looks around at the team: if half are asleep, staring into space, or buried in phones and laptops, the meeting is wasting their time. Attendance is partly to observe dynamics and morale — happy teams look energized, unhappy ones drained.
**Lesson:** Internal meetings are a sensor. Skipping them too aggressively costs you the early clues to team problems, including the clue that there are too many boring meetings.

## Cate Huston: the hardest, shortest lesson of becoming a manager (guest contribution)
**Situation:** Huston, as a manager, keeps a running mental list of what her team needs — people to hire, someone with potential who needs coaching, things product or design hasn't delivered, broken process.
**What happened / what was done:** Occasionally the state of things says "we need another engineer for the next month, and that engineer is me." But more often the team needs a manager: hiring, coaching, chasing other teams, fixing process. She accepts that if the team needs a manager more than an engineer, being that manager means she cannot be that engineer — and that if she's going to be bad at one, it must not be management, because being a bad manager is a choice inflicted on other people. At the end of a day with no code written and nothing measurable done, she tells herself she was as good a manager as she knew how to be, and that has to be enough.
**Lesson:** You can't escape into engineering when the team's real need is management; sucking at engineering hurts you, but sucking at management hurts everyone who reports to you.

## Losing her voice, and the director who forgot to eat
**Situation:** The first months of managing multiple teams, even on reasonable hours, leave new managers drained — no energy after work for dinner decisions or hobbies.
**What happened / what was done:** Fournier repeatedly lost her voice in her first few months managing multiple teams because she wasn't used to talking so much every day. A friend who had just become a director of engineering had to have an assistant order her lunch, because she would forget to eat and had no energy to choose food once she noticed she was hungry.
**Lesson:** The exhaustion of this transition is normal and nearly universal; the only way out is through. If you don't feel a little overwhelmed, you're probably missing something that needs attention.

## Plate spinning
**Situation:** Fournier needs a model for what attention management feels like from this level onward.
**What happened / what was done:** She compares the job to plate spinning: plates atop poles, each needing a touch before it slows and falls. The plates are people and projects; the skill is judging how much attention each needs and when. Newcomers will drop plates by neglecting them, and should approach the craft with a student's mind.
**Lesson:** Multi-team management is continuous triage of attention. The instinct for when to touch which plate is learned through practice, especially by staying present in 1-1s and meetings where warning signs first appear.

## Delegating performance reviews and hiring estimates as training
**Situation:** Complex, infrequent tasks like performance reviews and hiring plans belong to the manager alone, but rising leaders need to learn them.
**What happened / what was done:** Fournier suggests having a tech lead sit with you to write the performance review for an intern, or asking a senior engineer to estimate how many new people a project will need next year. She advises getting help from your own boss on these tasks until you're comfortable, then pulling rising leaders in to watch and try.
**Lesson:** Complex-infrequent work is a deliberate training vehicle: keep ownership, but use each occurrence to teach the next generation of managers.

## Ask the CTO: warning signs before a surprise resignation
**Situation:** A leader has twice been blindsided — teams struggling unexpectedly and a person quitting without warning — and asks what signals to watch for.
**What happened / what was done:** The distilled signals: the normally chatty, engaged person who suddenly leaves early, goes quiet in meetings, and vanishes from chat (personal crisis or job hunting, especially after being passed over in a promotion or reorg — talk honestly before the resignation); the tech lead who says everything is fine but skips 1-1s and gives vague updates (often hiding slow progress or out-of-scope work — get a project plan in place early); meetings where only the product manager and tech lead speak (disengaged team with no say in decisions); a project list that changes weekly with customer whims (no goals beyond pleasing customers); and a small team whose engineers profess ignorance of systems they don't touch (identity bound to their silo, resistant to change for the wider business).
**Lesson:** Surprises are rarely truly sudden; experienced managers learn a catalog of behavioral tells and act on them early.

## The engineer who wants a new programming language
**Situation:** An engineer pushes to use a new language for a project — one the team doesn't currently use — armed with great arguments for why it's the perfect tool.
**What happened / what was done:** The manager could just say no with reasons, but finds herself repeating the same no over and over: we need more people who know the language, we need to understand what running it in production means, we need logging standards, we need a testing story. Fournier's move: once you're repeating yourself, convert the repeated reasons into a policy — the hard requirements that must be met to get a yes, plus guidelines for thinking about the decision.
**Lesson:** Repeated identical nos are the raw material of policy. A policy tells the team the cost of getting to yes in advance, instead of making the manager the recurring bottleneck.

## Ask the CTO: my tech lead isn't managing
**Situation:** A tech lead was supposed to oversee a junior engineer on a project rewriting an app from Objective-C to Swift. The manager discovers the junior engineer still has no project plan and never answered the design review feedback, and wants the tech lead to handle it without stepping in personally.
**What happened / what was done:** The advice: start by asking the tech lead why these things haven't happened. The answer is usually a mix of two causes. She's busy with her own work and forgot to follow up — remind her that mentoring and oversight must be scheduled in alongside her code. And she may not know how to push a junior engineer who won't commit to a schedule — ask what she's tried and suggest approaches. New tech leads often feel they lack authority and get flustered when requests go unanswered. The right fix is coaching the tech lead to ask for reports herself, even though it's slower than the manager stepping in.
**Lesson:** When delegation fails, resist doing the work yourself; building the delegate's skills and visible authority teaches the team to respect her and teaches her to lead independently.

## Why don't you release more frequently?
**Situation:** A manager evaluates an engineering team that doesn't release continuously or daily.
**What happened / what was done:** Fournier walks through the interrogation: how long does the release process take, what has gone wrong recently, how often are releases delayed or rolled back and at what cost, who decides code is production-ready. Teams with infrequent releases show predictable cracks — long release processes, engineers who don't own quality and throw code over the wall to QA (adding communication delays), slow rollbacks, and release-driven incidents. When managers object that the roadmap leaves no time, she counters with tests: are engineers challenged and growing, is product excited about the pace, is most time spent writing new code? She also notes that even a product like a database that can't release frequently still pushes complete artifacts to beta environments, which can be measured for frequency and stability the same way.
**Lesson:** Release frequency is a leading indicator of engineering health; pushing on it uncovers the real technical work (automation, feature toggles, backward compatibility, small changes) that the manager must champion even without implementing it.

## Tests and the skill of breaking work down
**Situation:** Some engineers, including senior ones, resist breaking work into small chunks and check in code rarely, sometimes going off for weeks to write code alone.
**What happened / what was done:** Fournier observes that engineers who don't write tests often have a harder time breaking down their work, and that learning test-driven development — even if they never practice it daily — improves the skill. She acknowledges the discomfort of telling veterans their style needs updating, and notes the exception: research teams legitimately work differently.
**Lesson:** Frequent check-ins are a teachable habit tied to testing skills; it's legitimate for a manager to expect work in progress to be pushed regularly on a product team, despite the conflict-avoidance instinct.

## On-call as burnout machine versus firefighting balance
**Situation:** A company has developers support the code they write, and the manager is tempted to remove herself from the on-call rotation.
**What happened / what was done:** Fournier describes the failure mode where incident management is purely reactive: engineers go on call, drown in problems, accomplish nothing but firefighting, and hand the job to "the next poor sap" on the rotation, hating the job a bit more each round. The fix is leadership carving out time to design more stable systems and fix recurring incidents. She also describes the opposite failure: chasing defect-free software with weeks of manual QA, slow exhaustive code reviews, rare releases, and drawn-out planning, which leaves developers idle without reliably reducing risk. As for the manager: if the team runs its own incident management, she should move into escalation support — handling fewer incidents but staying reachable.
**Lesson:** Tune quality investment to the product's actual needs (features-first for a young product, stability-first for mission-critical systems), and never let either incident response or incident prevention stop developers from doing what they do best every day.

## Diana's clique: us versus them (Good manager, bad manager)
**Situation:** Diana joins a midsize startup to run a long-neglected mobile team, having been told the team is a mess.
**What happened / what was done:** Her first move is to rapidly hire several people who worked for her at her previous big company. They aren't a culture fit for the startup, and the team becomes a clique of developers who see themselves as better than the rest of the organization. The technology improves, but the team clashes constantly with product, and the apps don't evolve quickly. After a year, Diana gets fed up and quits — and her imported team soon follows, leaving the company right back where it started.
**Lesson:** Identity built on the team's superiority is a shallow binding: fragile to the leader's departure, resistant to outside ideas, prone to empire building, and inflexible to organizational change. Fixing a team by importing your old loyalists can recreate the original problem with interest.

## Neil's rebuild: the team player (Good manager, bad manager)
**Situation:** Neil also joins a startup in a chaotic state and can see the team needs to change.
**What happened / what was done:** He moves cautiously on firing, and has every new hire vetted by someone with long tenure at the company so the existing culture stays represented. He works closely with his product peers and proposes a path forward built on cross-functional collaboration. He sets clear goals and communicates them to his team. Progress starts slowly, but over time the whole organization grows stronger, and both the technology and the product improve dramatically.
**Lesson:** Durable teams are bound by shared company purpose, not tribal identity. Understand and build on the company's existing strengths and culture rather than against them; the payoff is slower but compounds.

## Larry Wall's virtues, redirected
**Situation:** Fournier looks for a way to carry the classic engineering virtues — Larry Wall's "laziness, impatience, and hubris" from *Programming Perl* — into leadership.
**What happened / what was done:** She channels two of them at processes rather than people: impatience to relentlessly ask why something feels inefficient, what value it delivers, and whether it can be simpler and faster; laziness to insist that "faster" means the same value in less total time, not longer hours. Her illustration: a team that works a 60-hour week to deliver in one week what would have taken a week and a half hasn't worked faster — it has given the company free time.
**Lesson:** Impatience and laziness aimed at process, never at individuals, are the engine of focus. Brute-force hours are a sign that a culture has stopped thinking.

## Hiding the midnight email
**Situation:** A newish manager genuinely needs extra hours for a while to keep up, but late-night and weekend emails train the team to overwork: even when no response is expected, people see the timestamps and conclude that constant work is what's valued.
**What happened / what was done:** Fournier's prescriptions: queue weekend and overnight emails to send during the next workday, set chat status to away in off hours, take vacations without answering email, and keep asking of your own work the same questions you ask the team — can I do this faster, do I need to do this at all, what value am I providing? Her summary of the philosophy: we focus so we can go home, and we encourage going home because it forces us to constantly focus; that is how great teams scale.
**Lesson:** A leader's visible work habits are policy. Model going home, because preventing the team's burnout matters as much as preventing your own.
