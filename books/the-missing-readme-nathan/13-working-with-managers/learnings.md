# Chapter 13: Working with Managers

A working relationship with your manager helps you grow your career, reduces stress, and even helps you ship reliable software. The relationship runs on mutual understanding: you must understand what your manager needs so you can help them, and your manager must understand your needs so they can help you. The chapter explains what managers actually do, demystifies common management processes (1:1s, PPPs, OKRs, performance reviews), teaches you to "manage up," and tells you what to do when management is failing you.

## Core Concepts

### What Managers Do

Managers seem to live in meetings, and it's not obvious what they're doing. Engineering managers work on **people, product, and process**: they build teams, coach and grow engineers, and manage interpersonal dynamics; they plan and coordinate product development; and they iterate on team processes to keep them effective. They may weigh in on technical matters like code reviews and architecture, but good engineering managers rarely write code.

Managers "manage" in three directions:

- **Up** — through relationships and communication with higher-level executives and directors. Managers are the conduit between rank-and-file engineers and the executives making business decisions. Upward management is how a team gets resources (money and engineers) and how its work gets recognized, appreciated, and heard.
- **Sideways** — with peer managers. A manager really has two teams: the individuals they manage and their pool of peer managers. Peer managers keep teams aligned on shared goals through relationship maintenance, clear communication, and collaborative planning.
- **Down** — by tracking the progress of ongoing projects, setting expectations and giving feedback, providing visibility into relative priorities, hiring (and, if necessary, firing), and maintaining team morale.

Understanding this helps you interpret your manager's behavior and know what to expect from them.

### Communication, Goals, and Growth Processes

Managers create processes to keep teams and individuals running smoothly. The processes that maintain your relationship with your manager fall into two groups: **1:1s and PPP status reports** handle communication and updates; **OKRs and performance reviews** handle goals and growth. These processes only pay off when you know what they are and how to use them.

### One-on-Ones (1:1s)

A 1:1 is a dedicated weekly or biweekly meeting between you and your manager to discuss critical topics, address big-picture concerns, and build a productive long-term relationship. They are widely practiced but often run badly as status check-ins or troubleshooting sessions.

Two rules matter most:

1. **You set the agenda** and should do most of the talking. Share a bullet-point agenda with your manager before the meeting. Keep a running 1:1 document containing past agendas and notes; share it with your manager and update it before and after each meeting. Your manager can add items, but their agenda takes a back seat to yours.
2. **1:1s are not for status updates.** Status belongs in written updates (see PPPs). These two rules alone separate hours of wasted time from genuinely important conversations.

Useful topic prompts when you need agenda material:

- **Big picture:** What questions do you have about the company's direction? About organizational changes?
- **Feedback:** What could we be doing better? What do you think of the team's planning process? What is your biggest technical concern? What do you wish you could do that you can't? What is your biggest problem? What is the company's biggest problem? What roadblocks are you or others hitting?
- **Career:** What career advice does your manager have? What can you improve? What skills do you wish you had? What are your long-term goals, and how are you tracking against them?
- **Personal:** What's new in your life? What personal issues should your manager be aware of?

1:1s create mutual understanding and connection. Some off-topic chat (your cat, the weather, your manager's sneakers) is normal and healthy — you're building a relationship deeper than an exchange of code for salary — but don't let every 1:1 become a social visit.

If you haven't been invited to 1:1s, ask whether your manager conducts them. Some managers prefer "lazy" 1:1s, leaving it to individuals to book time; in that model, no meeting on the calendar means the manager assumes there's nothing to discuss. You should have something to talk about most weeks. If your manager repeatedly cancels your 1:1s, raise it with them: managing the team — including investing time in you — is their job, and "too busy" is not an excuse. Calling out repeated cancellations is valuable feedback a manager wants and needs, not a confrontation.

You can also hold 1:1s with people other than your manager. 1:1s with senior engineers are particularly helpful when your company has no formal mentoring program, and they're a good way to get familiar with other parts of the organization. Bring an agenda to those too, so they don't degrade into status check-ins.

### PPPs (Progress, Plans, Problems)

A PPP is a common written status-update format. A status update is not meant to account for your time; it helps your manager find problems, spot areas where you need context, and connect you with the right people. Updates also surface topics for 1:1 discussion and force you to reflect on where you've been, where you're going, and what stands in the way.

Format: three sections — Progress, Plans, Problems — each with three to five bullet points, each bullet one to three sentences. Share PPPs with your manager and anyone else interested, usually via email, Slack, or a wiki, on a weekly or monthly cadence.

Keep a log of past PPPs and write each new one by rolling the previous one forward:

1. Look at last PPP's problems. Solved problems move to the new progress section; persisting problems stay in problems.
2. Look at last PPP's plans. Finished work moves to progress. Unfinished work either stays in plans (if you'll do it before the next PPP) or generates a problems entry (if something impeded it).
3. Look at your upcoming work and calendar, and add new expected work to plans.

The whole process should take less than five minutes.

### OKRs (Objectives and Key Results)

The OKR framework is how companies define goals and measure success. Companies, teams, and individuals each define goals (**objectives**) and attach metrics (**key results**) to each one. Each objective gets three to five key results — metrics that signal progress toward the objective.

Key principles:

- **OKRs cascade.** Ideally OKRs flow from the top of the company through teams to each individual: your OKRs contribute to your team's goals, and team OKRs contribute to company goals.
- **Key results are not a to-do list.** They spell out how you'll *know* something is done, not how to *do* it. There are many ways to reach an objective, and OKRs shouldn't box you into one plan. A well-formulated OKR leaves you free to choose the method at decision time, not when the OKR was written.
- **Keep them few and quarterly.** OKRs are usually defined and evaluated quarterly. Work with your manager to understand company and team objectives and derive yours from them. One to three OKRs per quarter is the sweet spot; more than five spreads you too thin.
- **Expect stretch goals.** OKRs are commonly set slightly higher than reasonable to create "reach" or "stretch" goals, meaning you should *not* complete 100 percent of them — full completion signals you're not aiming high enough. Most implementations target a 60–80 percent success rate: above 80 percent means you're not ambitious; below 60 percent means you're unrealistic or failing expectations. Multiple ambitious goals give you flexibility on what to drop midquarter and don't demand the precision a 100-percent expectation would. Critically, find out whether *your* company treats OKRs as must-have commitments or as ambitious targets with an expected failure rate.

Variants exist: some companies use qualitative goals; others drop the "O" and track only metrics — **key performance indicators (KPIs)**. Some companies set goals only at the team, department, or company level, not per individual. Whatever the framework, make sure you know what your goals are and how success is evaluated; if there are no individual goals, still talk explicitly with your manager about expectations and how they're measured.

### Performance Reviews

Managers conduct formal performance reviews on a regular cadence, usually annually or semiannually; title and compensation adjustments generally happen at review time. Reviews typically use a tool or template with prompts like: What have you done this year? What went well? What could have gone better? What do you want in your career — where do you see yourself in three to five years? Employees self-evaluate first, managers respond, the two meet to discuss, and the employee signs the document to acknowledge receiving it.

**Don't write your self-review from memory.** Memory is spotty and fixates on a few memorable projects. Keep an up-to-date record of completed work — a completed to-do list, your PPP log, or a bullet journal. Mine your issue tracker (tasks, milestones, epics, stories) and your merged pull requests and code reviews. Include nonengineering work: mentoring interns, code reviews, conducting interviews, blog posts, presentations, documentation — all of it should be recognized. Use the record to write an honest self-review.

Reviews can be stressful; treat them as an opportunity to look back at accomplishments, talk about what you want next, openly acknowledge missed opportunities, build a growth plan for the next year, and give feedback to your manager. **You should never be surprised by performance review feedback** — if you are, talk with your manager about the communication breakdown. A successful review ends with concrete actions toward your goals.

You may also participate in **360s** ("360 degrees"), where employees solicit feedback from coworkers in all directions: above (managers), below (reports), and sideways (peers), with questions like "What could I be doing better?", "What are people afraid to tell me?", and "What am I doing well?" 360s encourage honest feedback and let employees tell managers how they're doing. Take them seriously and write thoughtful notes for others.

Managers should give feedback frequently throughout the year — in 1:1s, as asides after meetings, over chat. If you're not getting enough, ask how you're doing in your next 1:1, and also ask mentors or senior engineers.

### Managing Up

Just as managers manage up to executives, you manage up by **helping your manager and making sure they help you**. You help them by giving them feedback; they help you by giving you feedback and supporting your goals. And don't settle if things aren't working — dysfunctional management can be traumatic and damage your growth.

#### Get Feedback

Reviews and 360s are holistic but too infrequent to rely on alone; you need regular feedback so you can adjust quickly. Managers don't always volunteer it, so ask:

- Use 1:1s, and send your questions beforehand — extemporaneous feedback is hard to produce on the spot.
- Ask for *specific* feedback: "What can I do to get better with testing?" beats "What can I do better?"
- Don't limit requests to technical topics; ask about communication, career growth, leadership, and learning opportunities.
- **Don't take feedback at face value.** Your manager is one perspective (an important one). Incorporate their feedback into your own perspective rather than adopting it outright: ask yourself what gaps exist in their view, how their feedback fits with yours, and what they know that you don't.
- **Give feedback on feedback.** Feedback can feel like it disappears into a black hole. Tell your manager when their advice pays off — positive results encourage more feedback — and tell them when it isn't working so they can suggest alternatives.
- **Ask for feedback as a way to give feedback.** Asking how to do something often exposes process gaps, and it lets your manager reach the conclusion themselves instead of you proposing a solution.

#### Give Feedback

Good managers want feedback from their team. They need to know what's working and what's not; every individual has a unique perspective, and feedback eliminates blind spots. Feedback can be about anything: the team, the company, behavior, projects, technical plans, even HR policies. Raise problems, but don't focus only on them — positive feedback is just as valuable, because it's hard for managers to know which of their changes had a positive effect; their work doesn't have unit tests.

Use the **Situation-Behavior-Impact (SBI) framework**:

1. **Situation** — describe the context.
2. **Behavior** — describe a specific behavior you find praiseworthy or problematic.
3. **Impact** — describe the effect of the behavior and why it matters.

SBI avoids character judgments and assumptions about intention or motivation; it sticks to facts and observable impact and steers the discussion toward mitigation and prevention. **Do not recommend a solution inside the SBI statement.** You may have solutions in mind, but start with the issue and learn more first — you may be missing information, or the problem may look different than you thought. Discuss solution ideas at the end of the conversation, after considering the other perspective.

Give feedback **privately, calmly, and frequently**. 1:1s are an excellent venue. Feedback can trigger strong emotions; keep a clear head and keep the conversation constructive. Privacy lets your manager have an honest conversation and keeps both parties from feeling attacked. Frequency eliminates surprises — don't wait for a problem to fester until it's too late. SBI works for positive feedback too.

Whether feedback goes to your manager or your peers, written or verbal, make it the kind of feedback you would want to receive. Ask yourself: what problem am I trying to solve? What outcome do I wish for — what does success look like?

#### Discuss Your Goals

Don't expect your manager to know what you want from your career. You need to clearly articulate your goals and aspirations for your manager to help you achieve them; formal reviews are a great place for these conversations.

If you don't have career goals yet, that's fine — tell your manager you want to explore, and they'll help. If you have interests beyond your immediate focus, say so, and don't limit yourself to engineering: maybe product management fascinates you, or you want to start a company. Think big and long-term. If you do know what you want, work with your manager to steer your work toward that destination — part of a manager's job is aligning your interests with the company's needs, and the more they know about your interests, the more they can steer the right opportunities your way.

Be patient after discussing goals: there are only so many opportunities available, and it's ultimately up to you to make the most of what you're given. Opportunities come in many forms — new projects, new challenges, interns to mentor, presentation opportunities, blog posts to write, training, teams to partner with. Under the right lens, everything you do is an opportunity for growth.

#### Establish Your Support Network

It can be difficult to give feedback, deal with certain situations, or even know what's normal and what's not. Trusted peer groups, both inside and outside your organization, are a good way to sanity-check things. This goes double for members of underrepresented groups: seek out communities like PyLadies and /dev/color that can talk through your situation and share their stories and experiences.

#### Take Action When Things Aren't Working

Every employee/manager relationship is unique — outcomes depend on the company, team, manager, and employee — so the only universal advice is to **be proactive if things aren't working**. Relationships and jobs have ups and downs; a short-term rough patch doesn't need drastic action. But consistent frustration, stress, or unhappiness means you should speak up.

- Use the SBI framework to talk to your manager if you're comfortable doing so. If not, go to HR, your manager's manager, or other mentors — the right channel depends on your relationship with each party. If you have nowhere else to turn, use HR.
- Understand HR's role: HR exists to maintain stability and keep the company out of legal and compliance trouble, which is *not quite the same* as making things right or fair. At minimum, talking to HR creates a record of your concern, and companies do tend to react to *patterns* of concerns.
- If you're told things will change, give your manager time — **three to six months is reasonable**. Managers need to think about feedback and implement changes; processes or even organizational structures might need rebuilding, and opportunities to demonstrate change may be infrequent. Watch the "slope" of change: are things improving? Has commitment been demonstrated through concrete actions?
- If time passes and you're still unhappy, it may be time for an internal transfer or a new job. **Internal transfers** work well when you like your coworkers and the company. **A new company** is better when problems are systemic: a bad business, poor leadership, a toxic culture. Switching teams is delicate — talk with the manager you want to transfer to first, then your current manager. Expect a transition period while you hand off work, but don't let a team change drag past three months.

Dysfunctional management is frustrating, stressful, and can stunt your career growth. Not every manager is good, and not every good manager is a good fit for you. If you've given feedback and been patient but things still aren't working, move on.

### Recommended Reading (Level Up)

A good way to understand your manager is to read the books they read. Engineering-management books explain why managers do what they do, build empathy by exposing you to their problems, and equip you to give better feedback. The chapter recommends:

- **The Manager's Path** — Camille Fournier (O'Reilly, 2017). Walks through manager phases from staff engineer to VP of engineering, how each level operates, and processes like 1:1s; also helps you chart your own career path. Start here.
- **An Elegant Puzzle** — Will Larson (Stripe Press, 2019). Insight into the problems managers face and the frameworks they use.
- **Thanks for the Feedback** — Douglas Stone and Sheila Heen (Penguin, 2014). Tools for processing review feedback "even when it is off base, unfair, poorly delivered, and, frankly, you're not in the mood"; useful in many other conversations too.
- **Managing Up** — Mary Abbajay (Wiley, 2018). Manager personas and how to deal with them, advice on tough managers, and when it's time to move on.
- **High Output Management** — Andy Grove (Vintage, 2015; written 1983). The classic engineering-management book documenting the philosophy Grove cultivated at Intel, which shaped modern engineering management. Still relevant, and your manager has probably read it, so it gives you a common reference point.

## Actionable Practices

- Expect managers to be accessible and transparent.
- Tell your manager what you need.
- Set the agenda for your 1:1s; share it as bullet points before the meeting.
- Keep 1:1 notes in a shared, living document updated before and after each meeting.
- Don't use 1:1s as a status update meeting; put status in written PPPs.
- Don't hide difficulties from your manager — status updates exist to surface problems.
- If your manager doesn't schedule 1:1s, ask how they prefer to cover 1:1 topics; in "lazy 1:1" cultures, book the time yourself.
- Speak up if your manager repeatedly cancels 1:1s; it's feedback they need, not a confrontation.
- Set up 1:1s with senior engineers and people in other parts of the organization, with an agenda.
- Write PPPs with three to five short bullets per section; roll each new PPP forward from the last in under five minutes.
- Derive your OKRs from team and company objectives; keep one to three per quarter.
- Write key results as measurable outcomes ("how I'll know"), never as to-do items ("how I'll do it") — don't get boxed in by OKRs.
- Find out whether your company treats OKRs as must-hit commitments or stretch goals with an expected 60–80 percent success rate.
- If your company sets no individual goals, still explicitly agree with your manager on expectations and how they're measured.
- Track accomplishments year-round (PPP log, completed to-do list, bullet journal, issue tracker, merged PRs) to make self-reviews easier; don't work from memory.
- Count nonengineering work in self-reviews: mentoring, interviews, code reviews, blog posts, presentations, documentation.
- Never accept being surprised by review feedback — if surprised, address the communication breakdown with your manager.
- Take 360s seriously and write thoughtful, non-superficial notes.
- Ask for specific feedback in 1:1s, sending questions ahead of time; cover communication, career, and leadership, not just technical skills.
- Don't take feedback as an attack, and don't adopt it uncritically — integrate it with your own perspective.
- Close the loop on feedback you receive: report back what worked and what didn't.
- Write actionable feedback of the sort you'd like to receive.
- Use the SBI framework (Situation, Behavior, Impact) to make feedback factual and less personal; hold solutions until the end of the conversation.
- Give feedback privately, calmly, and frequently; include positive feedback, not just problems.
- Think about long-term career goals and articulate them to your manager; if you have none yet, say you want to explore.
- Be patient after goal conversations and treat every assignment as a growth opportunity.
- Build a trusted peer support network inside and outside your company to sanity-check situations.
- When things aren't working, speak up — to your manager via SBI, or to HR, your manager's manager, or mentors.
- Give a manager three to six months to demonstrate change, watching for concrete actions; then transfer teams or change companies if nothing improves.
- Don't let an internal team transfer take longer than three months.
- Don't put up with bad management — move on if feedback and patience haven't worked.
- Read the books your manager reads to build empathy and a shared vocabulary.

## Pitfalls & Anti-patterns

- **1:1s run as status check-ins or troubleshooting sessions.** This wastes the one venue meant for big-picture, career, and relationship conversations. Status belongs in writing.
- **Letting the manager own the 1:1 agenda, or having no agenda.** Without your agenda, the meeting drifts into status or social chat; "lazy 1:1" managers will assume you have nothing to discuss.
- **Every 1:1 becoming a social visit.** Some personal talk builds the relationship, but the meeting must still do real work.
- **Tolerating repeatedly canceled 1:1s.** Investing time in reports is part of the manager's job; "too busy" is not an excuse, and a manager too busy for 1:1s may be too busy to be a manager.
- **Assuming a friendly relationship substitutes for management.** Friendship doesn't deliver career development; regular check-ins do.
- **Treating status updates as accounting for your time.** Their purpose is surfacing problems and opportunities, so hiding difficulties defeats them.
- **Writing key results as a to-do list.** It locks you into one plan when many paths reach the objective; OKRs should leave the method open until decision time.
- **Misreading stretch goals.** Hitting 100 percent of reach-goal OKRs means you aimed too low; below 60 percent means you were unrealistic or underperforming. Worse: not knowing whether your company expects 100 percent or 60–80 percent.
- **Taking on too many OKRs.** More than five per quarter spreads you too thin.
- **Writing self-reviews from memory.** Memory is spotty and fixates on memorable projects, undercounting your year — especially nonengineering contributions.
- **Being surprised at review time.** It signals a year-long feedback communication breakdown that should be addressed directly.
- **Asking vague feedback questions.** "What can I do better?" yields little; specific questions yield usable answers.
- **Taking feedback at face value or as an attack.** Either extreme wastes it; integrate it with your own view.
- **Leading feedback with character judgments, assumed motives, or prescribed solutions.** It puts people on the defensive and may be built on missing information; SBI plus solutions-last avoids this.
- **Only giving negative feedback.** Managers can't tell which changes helped without positive signal — their work has no unit tests.
- **Letting problems fester.** Infrequent feedback creates surprises; consistent frustration that goes unspoken never gets fixed.
- **Expecting your manager to guess your career goals.** They can only steer opportunities toward interests they know about.
- **Expecting HR to make things fair.** HR maintains stability and protects the company legally; talk to them to create a record, but know their role.
- **Quitting at the first rough patch — or enduring dysfunction indefinitely.** Short-term bumps don't need drastic action, and change takes three to six months; but past that, staying in bad management stunts growth and can be traumatic. Move on.
- **Letting a team transfer drag on.** Hand off responsibly, but cap the transition at three months.

## Key Terms

- **1:1 (one-on-one)** — A recurring (weekly or biweekly) dedicated meeting between you and your manager for big-picture, feedback, career, and personal topics — not status.
- **Lazy 1:1s** — A management style where the manager holds no standing 1:1s and expects reports to book time when they have something to discuss; no meeting is read as "nothing to discuss."
- **PPP (Progress, Plans, Problems)** — A written status-update format with three sections of three to five short bullets each, shared weekly or monthly.
- **OKR (Objectives and Key Results)** — A goal-setting framework: an objective (goal) with three to five key results (metrics that signal progress), usually set and evaluated quarterly, cascading from company to team to individual.
- **Key result** — A metric defining how you'll *know* an objective is met, not a task describing how to do it.
- **Stretch/reach goal** — An OKR deliberately set higher than reasonable; 60–80 percent attainment is the typical target, and 100 percent completion signals insufficient ambition.
- **KPI (key performance indicator)** — A tracked metric used by companies that drop explicit objectives and manage by metrics alone.
- **Performance review** — A formal annual or semiannual evaluation: employee self-review, manager response, discussion, sign-off; usually when title and compensation change.
- **360 (360-degree feedback)** — A review process soliciting feedback from all directions: managers above, reports below, and peers sideways.
- **SBI (Situation-Behavior-Impact)** — A feedback framework: describe the situation, the specific behavior, and its impact, avoiding character judgments and assumed motives; propose solutions only after discussion.
- **Managing up** — Helping your manager help you: giving them feedback, telling them what you need, and articulating your goals.
- **Manage up / sideways / down** — The three directions of a manager's job: executives above, peer managers beside, and their own team below.
