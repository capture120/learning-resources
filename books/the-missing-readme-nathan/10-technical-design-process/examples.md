# Chapter 10 Examples: Technical Design Process

### The design process cone (Figure 10-1)
The chapter's central figure shows design as a spiral inside an inverted cone. The spiral starts at the narrow bottom point and loops upward and outward. The horizontal axis is the amount of work done, split into independent work (left half) and team work (right half); a vertical arrow labeled "certainty and clarity" rises alongside the cone. The cone's slanted edge is labeled, from bottom to top, "teammate," "team," and "teams," showing that each loop of the spiral involves a wider circle of people. At the base you understand little and work mostly alone or with one teammate; near the top you have prototypes, a circulated document, and organization-wide awareness. **Lesson:** Design isn't linear — it's repeated alternation between solo deep work and collaboration, with certainty, clarity, and audience growing on every iteration.

### The supply-catalog feature request, refined
An initial feature request reads roughly: supply managers want catalog and page numbers shown for each item on the inventory page so reordering is easier when supplies run low; the proposal suggests hiring contract labor to scan all the catalogs and training a machine-learning model to map the scanned images to item descriptions in the database. Instead of building that, the engineer asks the product manager questions: How do supply managers place orders today? Can one item appear in multiple catalogs? How do users cope without this feature? What are the pain points of the current workaround, and which pain point hurts the business most? The answers produce a very different problem statement: supply room managers currently keep an Excel mapping from internal stock identifiers to vendor names and SKUs, and hopping between the software and Excel to place orders is slow and error prone; a single SKU can have multiple vendors, but the spreadsheet only tracks one, preventing cost comparison; their ranked priorities are data accuracy, then time to place an order, then cost minimization; and several vendors offer online catalogs, about half with online purchasing. The contract-labor scanning and ML model ideas get discarded entirely, and the online-catalog fact opens better solution paths. **Lesson:** Interrogate the problem behind a feature request — a refined, priority-ranked problem statement leads to a very different and better solution than the one the request presupposed.

### "What happens if we don't solve this problem?"
The chapter offers this as a stakeholder interview move: when scoping a problem, ask what happens if it goes unsolved, then ask whether that outcome is acceptable. The authors note you'll find that many problems don't actually need to be solved. **Lesson:** A single pointed question can kill unnecessary projects before any design work starts.

### Your problems aren't Google's
While researching, a particularly common misstep is finding a published solution to a problem that is similar but not identical to yours and copying it wholesale. The authors quip that your problems aren't Google's — possibly even if you work at Google — even when they look similar. **Lesson:** Borrow ideas from others' architectures, but think critically about fit instead of cloning solutions built for someone else's scale and constraints.

### Manager's schedule vs. maker's schedule, and the authors' focus hours
The chapter cites Paul Graham's essay "Manager's Schedule, Maker's Schedule," which explains how valuable uninterrupted time is to "makers" — people who build things, like engineers doing design work. The authors share their own habits: Chris prefers quiet after-lunch design sessions, while Dmitriy is most productive early in the morning. They advise finding your own peak-concentration window, blocking it on your calendar, and protecting it by closing chat, email, and phone notifications. **Lesson:** Deep design work needs multi-hour uninterrupted blocks scheduled at whatever time of day your concentration is strongest.

### Design spikes from Extreme Programming
To reconcile unhurried creative thinking with delivery deadlines, the chapter borrows the Extreme Programming term "spike": a time-bounded investigation. Allocating a spike as a task within a sprint gives an engineer sanctioned space for deep design thought without other sprint tasks competing for attention. **Lesson:** Time-box design exploration as an explicit, planned sprint task so creativity and predictable delivery can coexist.

### Quick-to-add infrastructure with hidden long-term costs
As an illustration of why fast changes can still need design documents, the chapter describes introducing a new piece of infrastructure — a caching layer, a network proxy, or a storage system — or a new public API or security measure. There may be a fast way to bolt these on to solve an immediate problem, but they carry long-term extension and maintenance costs that aren't obvious. Writing a design document and getting it reviewed lets concerns surface and ensures the whole team understands what's being added and why. **Lesson:** Judge the need for a design document by a change's long-term ramifications, not by how quickly it can be implemented.

### Python PEPs versus Python documentation
The chapter uses Python as the example of an organization that separates design proposals from documentation: Python Enhancement Proposals (PEPs) are the proposals, and the Python documentation describes the implemented reality. When the two are separate artifacts, you must keep the documentation up to date as proposals get implemented. The same proposals are also cited (with Kafka Improvement Proposals and Rust RFCs) as public, real-world examples of how major changes get documented in open source. **Lesson:** If proposals and docs are distinct documents, syncing them is an explicit ongoing duty — and open source proposal archives are free models for studying real design writing.

### Design docs in the code repository
The chapter offers a concrete trick for the "version control your design documents" rule: keep design documents in the same repository as the code. Code reviews then double as reviews of design comments, and documents naturally get updated as the code evolves. For teams that dislike reviewing Markdown or AsciiDoc and prefer wikis, Google Docs, or Word, the fallback rule is to preserve the full document history, including discussions. **Lesson:** Co-locating design docs with code wires document maintenance into the existing review workflow.

### "Feedler is built on Rouft" — defining nonobvious names
To show what the Current State and Context section should do, the chapter gives a sample sentence: "Feedler is our user sign-up system. It's built on top of Rouft, infrastructure that provides stateful workflow processing." Internal system names mean nothing to readers outside the team, so the document spells out what each named system does. **Lesson:** Define internal jargon and codenames explicitly so any reader in the organization can follow the design.

### The 50 percent memory reduction, tied to business need
The Motivation for Change section contrasts two versions of the same claim. Weak: "We can reduce the memory footprint by 50 percent." Strong: "By reducing the memory needs by 50 percent, we can address the most common objection to installing our software, leading to greater adoption." The chapter adds a caution against overpromising. **Lesson:** Frame technical benefits in terms of the business outcome they unlock, not as raw engineering metrics.

### Hard technical requirements: MySQL and OpenAPI
As examples of technical requirements driven by interoperability concerns or strict internal guidelines, the chapter gives: "Must support MySQL for the storage layer" and "Must provide an OpenAPI spec to work with our application gateway." Service level objectives can also be stated in the same section. **Lesson:** Technical requirements are hard constraints an acceptable solution must satisfy, usually imposed by the surrounding environment rather than the user.

### Architectural review boards versus request-for-decision (RFD)
The chapter sketches two common review patterns. Architectural reviews are formal and heavyweight: outside stakeholders such as operations and security must approve, a design document is required, and there may be multiple rounds of meetings or presentations — so they're reserved for large or risky changes. "Request for decision" (RFD — explicitly not the Internet Society's RFC) is the authors' name for a faster pattern: an engineer circulates a quick write-up of a decision needing input, and teammates whiteboard, discuss options, and decide. The term is uncommon but the pattern is widespread. Missing a required review step can derail a project at the last moment, so learn who must be informed, who signs off, and who decides. **Lesson:** Match review weight to change risk, and know your team's specific review process before you need it.

### Prototyping while waiting for approval
Within the review-process discussion, the chapter describes spending the approval-waiting period implementing prototypes and proof-of-concept "spikes." This increases confidence in the design and shortens the path to production once approved — but going beyond proof-of-concept depth is wasted effort, because design feedback will probably force code changes. **Lesson:** Use the review window to de-risk the design with throwaway code, not to build the real thing early.

### Rich Hickey's "Hammock Driven Development"
The Level Up section points to a conference talk by the author of the Clojure programming language, "Hammock Driven Development," calling it a "field report" on software design and one of the best introductions the authors have seen to the messy process of software design. **Lesson:** Deliberate away-from-keyboard thinking is a legitimate, expert-endorsed core of the design process.

### WePay's public design document practice
The chapter points to a post on WePay's engineering blog, "Effective Software Design Documents," which describes how that company's design process evolved over the years, and notes that the design document template WePay used for hundreds of internal designs is published on GitHub. **Lesson:** Real companies run sustained, template-driven design processes, and their published templates are a ready starting point for teams without one.

### Writing resources: Strunk & White, Zinsser, and Paul Graham
For engineers building writing skill, the chapter recommends *The Elements of Style* by William Strunk and E.B. White as the canonical reference for clear writing, strongly recommends William Zinsser's *On Writing Well*, and points to two Paul Graham essays on writing: "How to Write Usefully" and "Write Like You Talk." **Lesson:** Writing is a learnable craft with a small canon of standard references, and investing in it pays off across design documents, email, and reviews.
