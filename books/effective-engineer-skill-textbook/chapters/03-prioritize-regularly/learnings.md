# Chapter 3: Prioritize Regularly

There will always be more tasks than time, so choosing what to work on determines the leverage of everything else you do. Regular prioritization is itself a high-leverage activity: weeks spent on a low-impact project are barely different from not working at all. Prioritization is a skill that improves with practice, retrospection, and a routine that makes it a habit rather than an occasional event. You don't have to prioritize your leisure time — recharging is fine — but applying it to personal and professional goals significantly increases your chances of success.

## Core Principles

**Prioritization determines the leverage of all your other time.** Working on one task means not working on another, so the choice of what to do dominates how well you do it. Small wins in the right area compound (even 0.5% improvements in key growth metrics can add a million users over time), while the opportunity cost of the wrong focus can set you back months or years. This is why revisiting priorities regularly matters more than executing flawlessly on a stale plan.

**Treat your brain as a processor, not a memory bank.** Working memory holds only about 7 +/- 2 items, and research shows that effort spent remembering tasks reduces attention, impairs decision-making, and even hurts physical performance. Offloading every task to a single, easily accessible master list frees mental energy for the higher-leverage work of prioritizing and solving problems.

**Value produced is what counts, not activity.** Time and effort expended do not necessarily correlate with value created. What matters at the end of the day is products shipped, users acquired, metrics moved, or sales made — not hours worked, tasks completed, lines of code written, or meetings attended. Effort invested should be proportional to expected impact. And once you're producing results, few people will complain about declined meetings, slow email responses, or unfixed non-urgent bugs: when you get the important things right, the small things often don't matter.

**Important beats urgent.** Urgency is not importance. If you let urgent interruptions dictate your schedule, you neglect the non-urgent investments (planning, learning, tooling, infrastructure, mentoring) that raise your future effectiveness. Heavy time spent firefighting is often a symptom of underinvestment in exactly these activities.

**Deep work needs contiguous time and limited work in progress.** Engineers need blocks of half a day or more to reach flow; interruptions cost 10-23 minutes of refocusing each. Splitting attention across too many projects wastes energy on context switching, kills momentum on each project, and increases the likelihood of failure. Protect large blocks and serialize projects.

**Perfect ordering is impossible and unnecessary; continuous re-ranking is what works.** Estimating the exact leverage of every task is too hard and the estimates go stale as new tasks appear. Instead, keep asking whether something else on your list is higher-leverage than what you're doing now, and shift toward it when the answer is yes.

**The habit matters more than the mechanics.** No prioritization workflow is "best." Take the general principles, borrow starting mechanics from any system (Getting Things Done, Personal Kanban, Pomodoro, paper lists), and iterate until something fits you. What you cannot skip is the recurring habit of reviewing priorities.

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

- **Pairwise comparison instead of total ordering:** Don't sort your whole backlog by leverage. Compile a small set of important goals, pick initial tasks toward them, then repeatedly ask: "Is there something else I could be doing that's higher-leverage?" If no, continue; if yes, rethink. The goal is to continuously shift top priorities toward the highest leverage given current information, not to rank task #100 against task #101.
- **Heuristic 1 — Directly produces value:** Prioritize tasks with a direct line to output: shipping a needed feature, removing launch blockers, moving the core business metric, closing sales, resolving high-priority support issues. Defer or ignore tasks with only weak, indirect connections to value (status reports, organizing, most meetings, low-priority communications). Yishan Wong's lesson from Facebook: "activity is not necessarily production."
- **Heuristic 2 — Important and non-urgent (Covey's quadrants):** Partition activities by urgent/non-urgent and important/unimportant. Quadrant 1 = important and urgent (deadlines, outages). Quadrant 2 = important and non-urgent (career planning, relationships, professional reading, better tools and abstractions, scaling infrastructure, learning languages, mentoring). Quadrant 3 = urgent but unimportant (most email, calls, meetings). Quadrant 4 = neither. Quadrant 2 has no natural deadline, so it never wins on urgency — you must deliberately prioritize it. Prioritization itself is a Quadrant 2 activity.
- **Maker's schedule vs. manager's schedule (Paul Graham):** Managers work in one-hour blocks; makers need units of at least half a day, because an hour is barely enough to get started. Meeting placement that fragments a maker's day destroys productive capacity.
- **Flow (Csíkszentmihályi):** A state of effortless, deep concentration where people lose track of time and self — the condition under which engineers are most productive. Flow requires sustained focused attention; interruptions break it.
- **Brain-as-stage (David Rock, *Your Brain at Work*):** Working memory is a stage with room for ~7 +/- 2 "actors." The prefrontal cortex must bring all relevant actors on stage to make a decision. Too many simultaneous projects means spending mental energy swapping actors on and off rather than watching them perform.
- **Work-in-progress limits (*Personal Kanban*, Barry & Benson):** Like a juggler managing three balls easily but straining at six or seven, each person has a WIP capacity found by trial and error. Approaching capacity taxes the brain and degrades performance; "increasing work linearly increases the likelihood of failure exponentially."
- **If-then plans / implementation intentions (Gollwitzer, Halvorson):** Decide in advance "if [situation/cue], then [behavior]." The cue triggers the behavior nearly automatically, bypassing the activation-energy barrier that causes procrastination. Deciding ahead of time also matters in itself: in the moment, the short-term value of procrastinating can dominate your decision-making, whereas advance decisions weigh a task's long-term benefits. In Gollwitzer's study, this single tweak raised task completion from 32% to 71%.
- **Firefighting as symptom diagnosis:** Frequent pager alerts may signal missing automated recovery; recurring high-priority bugs may signal low test coverage; constant deadline crunches may signal poor estimation and planning. The fix is usually a Quadrant 2 investment, not more firefighting.

## Actionable Practices

- Keep one canonical, easily accessible to-do list (notebook, app, synced text file). Add every task to it immediately, including on the go, so your only memory job is "check the list." A single master list beats scattered sticky notes, sheets of paper, and emails: those are easily misplaced and make it harder for your brain to trust the list is comprehensive — and only a trusted, complete list lets the mind fully offload tasks.
- Don't build fully ranked backlogs. At Quora, estimating percentage impact (0.1%/1%/10%/100%) and time cost (hours/days/weeks/months) for hundreds of ideas was largely wasted work because new tasks kept displacing the backlog. Instead, pick a few important goals and do recurring pairwise comparisons against your current task.
- Regularly ask: "Is there something else I could be doing that's higher-leverage?"
- After shipping something valuable, find the next task that produces value, favoring those that produce the most value with the least effort. Repetition trains the skill: after enough iterations it gets easier to recognize which tasks are most valuable.
- Say no. When someone books an unnecessary meeting, assigns a small bug, or pitches a shiny prototype, they are usually not weighing the opportunity cost of your time. Explain what it would displace and discuss relative priority.
- Match effort to impact: skipping a $3 latte matters less than an hour or two finding plane tickets that save a few hundred dollars, which matters less than negotiating a few thousand dollars more salary, which may matter less than an investment portfolio earning a few extra compounding percentage points per year.
- Label each to-do 1-4 by Covey quadrant (Nimrod Hoofien's exercise) to whittle your work toward the important and non-urgent. De-prioritize Quadrants 3 and 4; treat heavy Quadrant 1 time as a signal to invest in Quadrant 2 root-cause fixes.
- Carve out time for skill development even when it slows you down at first; the productivity gains compensate later.
- Protect contiguous blocks: schedule meetings back-to-back or at the edges of the day; block calendar hours (even with a fake meeting); institute days like "No Meeting Wednesdays"; when interrupted mid-focus, offer to help before or after your breaks instead.
- Budget for interruption cost: studies found 10-15 minutes (Microsoft Research) to 23 minutes (UC Irvine) to return to focus after handling email or instant messages.
- Limit projects in flight. Find your personal WIP limit by trial and error, and serialize projects to maintain momentum. Teams also suffer when fragmented across too many tasks — shared context for design discussions and code reviews evaporates.
- Make if-then plans for tasks you're avoiding: "If it's after my 3pm meeting, then I'll investigate this long-standing bug."
- Fill schedule gaps with a prepared list: "If I only have 20 minutes before my next activity, then I will ____" — finish a code review, write interview feedback, answer email, investigate a small bug, write an isolated unit test.
- Build a routine (sample system from the author): maintain a backlog plus a "Current Priorities" list for the week with sections "This Week," "Today," and "Doing." When picking the week's tasks, favor those that directly produce value plus some longer-term investments — and, because optimizing for learning is important and non-urgent, deliberately include some tasks related to learning something new each week. Each morning (when energy is highest — prioritizing is mentally taxing), move tasks into "Today." Estimate tasks in 25-minute chunks (e.g., "[4]" = four chunks); expect roughly 10-15 productive chunks per day, with the rest lost to meetings and interruptions. Use if-then plans to schedule creative work in the morning. Block distracting sites (e.g., via /etc/hosts) and time 25-minute focused sessions with 5-minute breaks; count completed chunks to measure the day and check estimate accuracy. As new tasks and ideas come up, add them to "Today" or "This Week" if urgent, or to the backlog if not.
- Attach the daily review to an existing routine — the author spent 5-10 minutes over morning coffee reviewing to-dos, and used misses to ask: did I work on something more important, misprioritize, or procrastinate?
- Hold a 30-minute weekly planning session (Sunday afternoon or Monday morning): review what you finished, diagnose what you didn't, and scope the next week. Weekly sessions enable larger course corrections; a monthly session reviews progress and bigger changes.
- Revisit in-flight commitments when facts change: a one-month estimate becoming three months, an old feature demanding an hour a day of firefighting, or a legacy codebase eating your time all warrant re-asking whether the current project is still the best use of time.

## Key Takeaways

- Write down and review to-dos: spend mental energy prioritizing and processing tasks, not remembering them — the brain is a processor, not a memory bank.
- Work on what directly leads to value; don't try to do everything, and regularly ask whether something higher-leverage is available.
- Work on the important and non-urgent: prioritize long-term investments that increase effectiveness even without deadlines.
- Reduce context switches: protect large blocks of time for creative output and limit ongoing projects so cognitive energy isn't spent juggling.
- Make if-then plans to combat procrastination: binding an intention to a trigger significantly raises the odds of doing it.
- Make prioritization a habit: experiment to find a workflow; done regularly, focusing on and completing your highest-leverage activities gets easier.
