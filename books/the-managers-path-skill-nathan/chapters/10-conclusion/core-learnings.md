# Conclusion — Core Learnings

## What this chapter is about

The closing chapter distills the entire path — which Fournier frames as "mentor to manager to senior leader" — into one final lesson: to manage others well, you must first learn to manage yourself. She opens by stating what she hopes the book delivered: a few tricks, a few pitfalls identified to watch out for, and inspiration to meet the challenge of whatever role you are in. She ends with the practices she relies on for self-management (meditation, journaling, curiosity) and a parting mantra: get curious.

## Core principles

### Self-management is the foundation of managing others
The most important lesson Fournier says she has learned: you have to be able to manage yourself if you want to be good at managing others. The more time you spend understanding yourself — how you react, what inspires you, what drives you crazy — the better off you will be.

### Great managers master conflict by taking their ego out of it
Working through conflict well means removing your ego from the conversation. Seeing a complex situation clearly requires looking past your own interpretations and the stories you tell yourself. To deliver hard messages that people can actually hear, you must state the facts without embellishing them with your personal storyline.

### Decisiveness has a shadow side
People who seek out management roles often have strong views on how things should be. Decisiveness is a good quality, but it becomes a liability when you forget that your interpretation of a situation is just that — an interpretation, not the truth.

### Curiosity is the antidote to ego
When a situation frustrates you, ask what the other person is trying to do, what they value, and what they want and need. Look for the other side of the story. This applies beyond people: bring the same curiosity to process, technology, strategy, and business. Ask questions and be willing to have your notions proven wrong.

## Concrete practices Fournier recommends

- **Try meditation.** She calls a meditation practice essential to developing her own self-management and self-awareness, and it trains you to recognize the voice of your ego. She is explicit that it is not a cure-all, but calls it a useful exercise for practicing awareness of your own reactions, and recommends trying it for a while if you are interested. Her favorite resources: the podcasts at tarabrach.com and the writings of Pema Chödrön.
- **Keep a daily journaling habit.** She writes a page or two of free-flow thoughts every morning to clear her mind and prepare for the day, always ending with the mantra "Get curious."
- **Investigate your emotional reactions.** Notice when your reactions make it hard to see clearly what is going on around you and what needs to be said.
- **Use curiosity as a deliberate trick to get away from your ego**, not just a passive attitude.

## Pitfalls and anti-patterns

- Embellishing hard feedback with your own storyline, which prevents people from hearing what you actually need to say.
- Letting decisiveness harden into the belief that your reading of a situation is fact.
- Treating your emotional reactions as reliable signals rather than data to investigate.
- Expecting leadership growth to be easy: Fournier describes her own development as a series of difficult lessons, mistakes, and challenges, often involving frustrating interpersonal situations.

## Parting words

Stay curious, and good luck on your path.
