# Chapter 2: Mentoring — Examples and Anecdotes

## Kevin, the mentor at Sun Microsystems

**Situation:** Fournier's first serious software job was an internship at Sun Microsystems, on a team building JVM tools — the first time she had a real software project to build.

**What happened / what was done:** Her mentor was Kevin, a senior engineer and a senior technical leader in the area. Despite his seniority, he made time for her. Instead of showing her a desk and leaving her to figure things out, he discussed projects with her, sat with her at the whiteboard, and went through the code together. She always knew what she was expected to get done, and when she got stuck she could ask him for help.

**Lesson:** That summer was critical to her development: under his guidance she saw she could do real-world work and be a productive employee. Working with Kevin was one of her first major career milestones and taught her the value of mentorship. Good mentoring is active — time, whiteboards, shared code, clear expectations, and an open door for questions.

## Left alone at BigTechCo

**Situation:** Fournier's first job out of college was at a very large tech company (called "BigTechCo"), on a team in the middle of releasing a project several years in the making.

**What happened / what was done:** Her manager showed her to her office and then left her alone to figure out for herself what needed to be done. She didn't know how to ask for help and was afraid she'd be seen as a fool if she did. Unsurprisingly she got discouraged, and in that discouragement decided the best move was to leave for graduate school — which she did.

**Lesson:** Leaving a new hire to sink or swim drives talent out the door. New hires often don't know how to ask for help and fear looking stupid; without a mentor, discouragement compounds quietly until the person leaves.

## The mentored first job after graduate school

**Situation:** Fournier's first job out of graduate school, in deliberate contrast to BigTechCo.

**What happened / what was done:** Instead of being shown a desk and abandoned, she was set up with a mentor who encouraged her to ask questions. They pair programmed so she could learn the codebase and how testing worked on the project — her first taste of unit testing.

**Lesson:** She was productive within days and learned more in her first few months there than in her entire time at BigTechCo. She credits this almost entirely to having a mentor from day one. New-hire mentoring is the difference between months of floundering and days to productivity.

## The unspoken rule about Thanksgiving vacation

**Situation:** A worked example of spoken versus unspoken rules that a new-hire mentor should surface.

**What happened / what was done:** The HR handbook states a standard vacation policy — the spoken rule. The unspoken rule at an ecommerce company is that you don't take vacation the week after Thanksgiving, because that week is critical for the business. A subtler unspoken rule: roughly how long you're expected to struggle with a problem alone before asking someone for help.

**Lesson:** Companies are full of process, culture, and jargon so second-nature to veterans that they don't realize it's foreign to newcomers. Unspoken rules make it harder for new people to join, and they can also quietly make veterans worse at their own jobs. Mentoring a new hire is a chance to notice and clarify them.

## Ask the CTO: Mentoring a summer intern

**Situation:** An engineer asks: "I'm supposed to mentor a summer intern, but I have no idea where to begin. What should the intern do? How do I prepare?"

**What happened / what was done:** Fournier's answer, as CTO: preparation shouldn't take much time but is crucial. First, prepare for arrival — know the start date, and make sure the desk, computer, and system access are ready, since even big companies overlook this for interns and there's nothing worse than showing up to your big job with nowhere to sit. Second, have a clear project: specific but not urgent, relevant to the team, and sized so an entry-level engineer could finish it in about half the internship (about five weeks of work for a ten-week intern), leaving room for program activities, and leaving useful codebase knowledge if they finish early. Third, plan for the intern to present the work at the end of the program, following the team's demo format, with coaching from the mentor.

**Lesson:** An intern is still a student — expect slow progress, be pleasantly surprised by overshoot. The end-of-summer presentation gives exposure, sets the expectation that the project gets finished, and matters for the decision to make a full-time offer or to bring the intern back for another summer if she isn't graduating yet. Interns who feel the company appreciated their work are the ones most likely to come back after graduation.

## Ask the CTO: Should we hire interns?

**Situation:** A leader asks: "My company has had several inquiries about whether we hire interns. We haven't, but I'm tempted to start in order to increase our hiring pool. What should I be thinking about?"

**What happened / what was done:** Fournier's advice: internship programs are for increasing the hiring pipeline and finding strong candidates before they graduate — many companies mistakenly treat them as a way to get extra work done. Two rules. First, don't hire interns who aren't going to graduate within a year after the internship; students with two or more years left will likely explore other companies before their first full-time job, and with only a handful of slots every intern should have high potential to convert. Second, hiring interns is relatively easy compared to hiring full-time graduates because demand is lower, so use that abundance of options to push for candidates from underrepresented groups.

**Lesson:** An internship is a talent-identification and attraction tool, not cheap labor. Diversity in the internship program translates into diversity in new-grad hiring, which translates into diversity in the organization.

## Good manager, bad manager: the alpha geek

**Situation:** A recurring character study: the "alpha geek," encountered in mentoring relationships and beyond — an excellent, effective engineer driven to be the best on the team, to always have the right answer, and to solve all the hard problems, who values intelligence and technical skill above all other traits and believes those should determine who makes decisions. She typically goes into management either because she was pushed into it or because she believes the smartest person on the team should be the manager.

**What happened / what was done:** At her best, the alpha geek is inspirational if intimidating: she worked on the original version of the system ten years ago, knows the authors, can figure anything out, and can design great systems that are fun to help build. Even at her best there is an edge: she knows exactly why that thing you're trying to do won't work, and when it doesn't, believe me, she'll remind you how she told you so — if only you had listened and done things her way! And the gotcha-hunting has a root belief: the alpha geek believes every developer should know exactly what she knows, so if you don't know something, she will gleefully point out your ignorance. At her worst, she can't let anyone else get glory without claiming some, takes credit for the team's work, claims origin of all good ideas (and only foresight about the bad ones), gleefully points out ignorance, belittles mistakes, redoes teammates' work without warning, is rigid and closed to ideas she didn't originate, gets threatened by criticism of her past technical decisions, hates taking direction from anyone she doesn't respect intellectually, and is demeaning toward nontechnical roles. The habit often first surfaces when engineers become mentors: people stop coming to you for help despite your obviously strong skills. The diagnostic questions: Do you "never pull punches"? Do you eagerly hunt for gotchas and mistakes? Are you reluctant to admit someone else had a good idea or wrote good code? Do you think correctness is always worth fighting hard for?

**Lesson:** The alpha geek tries to create a culture of excellence but creates a culture of fear. Mentoring can break the habit — treating the mentee as someone to teach in the way that works best for *her* exposes where an aggressive style blocks learning — but someone unwilling to change style to help a mentee shouldn't volunteer to mentor. Alpha geeks make absolutely terrible managers unless they let go of the smartest-person-in-the-room identity; they're often better directed at technical strategy and system design, like the CTO of a technology-focused startup paired with an execution-focused VP of Engineering. Promote them to management only with great care and close observation, because an alpha geek who believes her value is knowing more than others may hoard information and make the whole team less effective.

## The disengaged formal mentoring program

**Situation:** A scenario from the advice to managers who assign mentors: companies set up mentoring programs that match junior people with senior people outside their team for career or skills growth, but give both parties little guidance beyond the fact that they've been matched.

**What happened / what was done:** Most of the time these programs yield little for either party. When the mentor is not engaged or too busy to spend time, the mentee is disappointed. When the mentee doesn't know how to ask for help or what to do with the relationship, it feels like forced socializing and a waste of both people's time.

**Lesson:** What you measure, you improve. Decide what the mentoring relationship should achieve before creating it, then find the person who can meet those goals — and don't push people into cross-team programs without structure and guidance.

## Growing future leaders through mentoring assignments

**Situation:** A pair of brief scenarios about using mentoring to develop leadership on a team.

**What happened / what was done:** A brilliant, introverted developer who never wants to formally manage is encouraged to mentor one-on-one; this builds her external perspective and her network. Conversely, an impatient young engineer is tasked with helping an intern succeed, under the manager's supervision.

**Lesson:** Leadership requires human interaction, and patience and empathy matter for anyone's career in a team-based environment. Mentoring assignments are a low-stakes way to grow both qualities — the introvert gains connections, the hothead gains humility.

## Mentee with the wrong tool for the job

**Situation:** A scenario from the mentee-side advice: someone ends up with a mentor because a third party thought they should have one, but never prepares for sessions and isn't sure what they want from the relationship.

**What happened / what was done:** Fournier's prescription: think about what you want from the relationship and come prepared, especially when the mentor is an outside volunteer who isn't being paid. If you don't have time to prepare or don't feel preparation is necessary, question whether you need the mentoring relationship at all — there are only so many people anyone can meet for coffee and so many hours in a day.

**Lesson:** You don't have to have a mentor. Maybe you need a friend, a therapist, or a coach instead. Because mentor time is usually free, it's easy to undervalue; be respectful of it, and consider paying a professional for what you actually need.
