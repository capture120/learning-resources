# The Manager's Path — Knowledge Base Overview

## What this knowledge base is

A distillation of *The Manager's Path* by Camille Fournier (O'Reilly, 2017). The book's thesis: engineering management is a technical discipline you can learn level by level, and hands-on expertise is what gives a manager credibility — so the book follows the typical career path from being managed all the way to CTO. Each chapter directory under `chapters/` holds `core-learnings.md` (the chapter's principles and actionable guidance) and `examples.md` (the book's anecdotes retold).

**How to route:** find your current level or situation in the ladder map or the routing table below, then read that chapter's `core-learnings.md` first. The book's "Good Manager, Bad Manager" dysfunctions and "Challenging Situations" appear at the level where they are most common, but apply at every level — so check adjacent chapters if a trigger almost matches.

## The career ladder map

The chapters follow one engineer's progression. Each step is a new job, not a bigger version of the last one.

| Stage | You are... | Chapter |
|---|---|---|
| Being managed | An individual contributor with a manager | `chapters/01-management-101/` |
| Mentoring | An engineer guiding an intern or new hire (first taste of management) | `chapters/02-mentoring/` |
| Tech lead | A senior engineer responsible for a team's output, still coding at least 30% | `chapters/03-tech-lead/` |
| Managing people | A first-time manager of individual engineers | `chapters/04-managing-people/` |
| Managing a team | An engineering lead owning a whole team's health and delivery | `chapters/05-managing-a-team/` |
| Managing multiple teams | A director no longer writing production code | `chapters/06-managing-multiple-teams/` |
| Managing managers | A leader whose reports are themselves managers | `chapters/07-managing-managers/` |
| Senior leadership | A CTO, VP of Engineering, or executive | `chapters/08-the-big-leagues/` |
| Culture building | A senior leader shaping structure, values, and process (any growth stage) | `chapters/09-bootstrapping-culture/` |
| Self-management | Anyone, at any level | `chapters/10-conclusion/` |

## Routing table

### 1. Management 101 — `chapters/01-management-101/`
What to expect from a good manager and how to be a good report.
Consult when:
- 1-1s feel like status meetings, are boring, or keep getting canceled
- You've only ever been left alone by managers and wonder if that's normal or good
- You want a promotion or raise but have never actually raised it with your manager
- You only get feedback at review time, or blunt critical feedback just caught you off guard
- You're evaluating a job offer and deciding how much the prospective manager matters
- You resent your manager — or every manager you've ever had
- Career uncertainty: the job you wanted stopped being enjoyable and you don't know what's next
- You're a new graduate with CTO ambitions choosing what to optimize for
- You're getting senior and personal feedback is drying up, or you're told to bring solutions not problems
- You expect your company to hand you training, career direction, or work-life balance

### 2. Mentoring — `chapters/02-mentoring/`
Mentoring interns and new hires as the first, low-risk practice ground for management skills.
Consult when:
- You've been assigned an intern or new hire and need to prepare (project sizing, milestones, check-in cadence, onboarding docs)
- You're a manager picking who mentors someone, and deciding how to recognize that work
- A mentee seems lost but never asks questions, or asks for help without trying first
- A formal mentoring program feels vague, unstructured, or like forced socializing
- You want to decline a mentoring request without burning the relationship
- You're weighing whether to promote a brilliant but abrasive engineer into management (the alpha geek)
- People avoid asking you for help despite your strong technical skills
- Your company is debating an internship program or which students to hire
- You're unsure whether you'd like management and want a low-risk way to test the skills

### 3. Tech Lead — `chapters/03-tech-lead/`
The first leadership step: responsibility for team output, influence without authority, project management beyond agile.
Consult when:
- You're deciding whether to take a tech lead role, or who on a team should get it (not automatically the best coder)
- You just became tech lead and feel overwhelmed, resentful, or expected to keep the same code output (the "Stone of Triumph")
- You're planning a multi-sprint or infrastructure project agile alone can't handle: breakdown, unknowns, premortem, launch and rollback plans, mid-flight requirement changes
- You need to sell technical debt or unglamorous work to teammates and managers without formal authority
- You must explain technical motivation to executives or non-technical stakeholders
- You or a report keeps imposing process and tools to fix what are really communication problems (the process czar)
- You're losing coding time to meetings and need to balance maker versus manager schedules
- You're choosing between the management track and the senior individual contributor track

### 4. Managing People — `chapters/04-managing-people/`
Day-to-day mechanics of managing individual engineers for the first time.
Consult when:
- You just got your first direct reports and don't know what to actually do with them
- A new hire or internal transfer is starting: 30/60/90-day plans, onboarding docs, explicit expectations — also the mechanism for detecting a mishire early
- Your 1-1 cadence or style is off: too frequent, status-only, or devolved into venting (five 1-1 styles)
- You can't stop reviewing every line of code or attending every standup, or a delegated engineer suddenly disengaged (micromanagement versus delegation)
- Performance review season: writing fair 360 summaries, forcing concrete examples, delivering ratings people will push back on
- A report wants a promotion: building packets, stretch projects, how the promotion game is actually played
- You believe in someone's "potential" despite years of weak results (potential is shown by actions, not credentials)
- An employee is underperforming: early documented feedback, performance improvement plans, coaching out

### 5. Managing a Team — `chapters/05-managing-a-team/`
The jump from individuals to a whole team: dysfunction debugging, decisions, conflict, planning.
Consult when:
- You were promoted over a former peer who also wanted the job
- The team isn't shipping: missed deliverables, painful or infrequent releases
- Team cohesion destroyers: the brilliant jerk, the secretive noncommunicator, the openly disrespectful employee, the drama-stirrer
- The team is overworked or burned out from crunch or unstable production
- You avoid conflict, put hard decisions to votes, or reviews keep surprising people (kind versus nice)
- Your team clashes with product, design, or another engineering team
- You're debating how much company drama (layoffs, politics) to share — shield, but don't parent
- You're a new manager wondering whether and how to stay technical
- Quarterly planning: ~10 productive weeks per engineer per quarter, 20% sustaining engineering, doubling off-the-cuff estimates, saying no near deadlines

### 6. Managing Multiple Teams — `chapters/06-managing-multiple-teams/`
The director level: no more production code, time management, delegation, team health at a distance.
Consult when:
- You just started managing two or more teams and feel exhausted or hostage to meetings
- You miss coding and wonder how to stay technical without writing production code (reviews, debugging, pairing, creative time)
- Your calendar is full but nothing important gets done (importance versus urgency)
- You're deciding what to delegate, keep, or use to train rising leaders (frequency-by-complexity matrix)
- A previously engaged person went quiet, or a tech lead gives vague updates and skips 1-1s (quit warning signs)
- You need to say no to your boss, peers, or an engineer lobbying for a new language ("yes, and", policies, help-me-say-yes, quick no)
- Assessing team health: release frequency, check-in frequency, incident frequency; on-call burnout versus over-engineering for stability
- Your team acts superior to the rest of the company, or you're tempted to import loyalists (cliques versus purpose-based identity; your first team is your peers)
- You email at midnight and the team is matching your overwork (model going home)

### 7. Managing Managers — `chapters/07-managing-managers/`
Leading through a layer of abstraction: skip-levels, coaching managers, hiring managers, debugging organizations.
Consult when:
- You're newly managing managers and keep getting blindsided by attrition or blown projects
- Your open-door policy surfaces nothing (it never does — run skip-levels and ferret problems out)
- A manager blames product churn, an errant tech lead, or firefighting for poor team results (they're still accountable)
- A manager everyone loves never fixes anything or hides problems (the people-pleaser)
- A first-time manager you promoted is overworking, micromanaging, or skipping 1-1s
- You're hiring a manager: role-played 1-1s, team-debugging questions, culture screen over domain expertise, reference checks
- You inherited functions outside your expertise (operations, QA, design)
- A team feels slow or demotivated and you need to diagnose why (debug it like a system outage)
- Leadership keeps asking "why is it taking so long?", or roadmap churn keeps squeezing out technical debt (share estimates aggressively, hold the 20% sustaining allocation)
- You're worried about losing technical relevance and becoming a message-relaying go-between

### 8. The Big Leagues — `chapters/08-the-big-leagues/`
Senior leadership: what CTOs and VPs of Engineering actually do, strategy, bad news, executive peers, your echo.
Consult when:
- You're becoming (or interviewing to be) a CTO or VP of Engineering and want to know what the job actually is — or choosing between the two paths
- You're a CTO asked to hand your reports to VPs (giving up management means giving up strategic power)
- Priorities changed abruptly and you must move the org: kill in-flight work explicitly, repeat the message three times
- You're writing a technology strategy or presenting to a board for the first time
- You must announce layoffs, a disbanded team, or an unpopular reorg (through individuals and prepared managers, never mass message)
- You now report to a nontechnical CEO or boss
- You clash with executive peers, or want to tell your team you opposed a leadership decision (first team, cone of silence)
- Your offhand comments redirect projects, or you still socialize like one of the team (the Echo)
- People fear you, hide mistakes, or won't ask questions (correcting a culture of fear)
- You need engineering-wide standards for excellence and production readiness without making every decision yourself (True North)

### 9. Bootstrapping Culture — `chapters/09-bootstrapping-culture/`
Deliberately creating structure, values, career ladders, team organization, and process as an org grows.
Consult when:
- You're a new head of engineering deciding which processes to introduce first without killing speed (structure should evolve from real failures, scaled to headcount, age, infrastructure, and risk)
- Your "flat, no-process" team feels political and decisions come from an invisible inner circle (the Tyranny of Structurelessness)
- People quit over no growth path, or pay tracks negotiation skill: writing a career ladder with the team, salary bands, breakpoint levels, split management/technical tracks
- Interviewers reject candidates on "culture fit" vibes like the airport test (use written values instead; apply them in hiring, praise, and reviews)
- A teammate keeps violating a stated value and you need language to coach the friction
- You're considering cross-functional pods or squads and worried about Conway's Law tradeoffs and the 20% engineering-time reserve
- Code review has become style-nitpicking, postmortems devolve into blame (run learning reviews), or someone built a system in an unsanctioned stack (architecture review)
- The same class of outage keeps recurring and you need to decide what process failure it reveals

### 10. Conclusion — `chapters/10-conclusion/`
The meta-lesson: manage yourself first.
Consult when:
- You're stuck in recurring interpersonal conflict and certain the other person is the problem
- You're about to deliver hard feedback and notice you're wrapping the facts in your own storyline
- Your decisiveness is hardening into certainty that your interpretation is fact
- You feel reactive or emotionally drained and want concrete self-awareness practices (meditation, daily journaling, "get curious")
- You want the book's single closing framework after any role-specific chapter

## Cross-cutting themes

- **The job changes at every level.** Each step is a new entry-level job, not an extension of the last. Time allocation shifts: coding at least 30% (Ch 3), small features only (Ch 5), no production code (Ch 6), Grove's four tasks (Ch 8). Deepest: `03-tech-lead`, `05-managing-a-team`, `06-managing-multiple-teams`, `08-the-big-leagues`.
- **One-on-ones.** As a report (Ch 1), running them in five styles (Ch 4), keeping them at scale to read dynamics (Ch 6), skip-levels through the management layer (Ch 7). Deepest: `04-managing-people`, `07-managing-managers`.
- **Feedback with no surprises.** Fast beats convenient (Ch 1), continuous feedback feeding reviews and documented early for underperformers (Ch 4), stating facts without your own storyline (Ch 10). Deepest: `04-managing-people`.
- **Delegation versus abdication and micromanagement.** Trust and control as the core issue (Ch 4), the frequency-by-complexity matrix and training rising leaders (Ch 6), coaching control-freak new managers (Ch 7). Deepest: `06-managing-multiple-teams`.
- **Staying technical.** Credibility requires it at every level, but the form changes: code (Ch 3, 5), reviews and pairing (Ch 6), informed oversight, not go-between (Ch 7), True North instincts earned early (Ch 8). Deepest: `05-managing-a-team`, `06-managing-multiple-teams`.
- **Saying no, estimates, and cutting scope.** Premortems and plan discipline (Ch 3), 10 productive weeks and doubling estimates (Ch 5), the five no strategies (Ch 6), aggressive estimate sharing (Ch 7), killing work on priority changes (Ch 8). Deepest: `05-managing-a-team`, `06-managing-multiple-teams`.
- **Toxic people and psychological safety.** The alpha geek (Ch 2), the brilliant jerk and drama (Ch 5), cultures of fear at the top (Ch 8), values that name the friction (Ch 9). Deepest: `05-managing-a-team`.
- **Sustaining engineering at ~20%.** Budget it or feature work slows: Ch 5, Ch 7, Ch 9.
- **Debugging teams like systems.** Hypothesis, data, observation, relentless why: dysfunction playbook (Ch 5), organizational debugging (Ch 7). Deepest: `07-managing-managers`.
- **Manage yourself first.** Ego out of conflict, curiosity over certainty (Ch 10); the Echo and role modeling (Ch 8); self-advocacy and owning your own career (Ch 1). Deepest: `10-conclusion`.
