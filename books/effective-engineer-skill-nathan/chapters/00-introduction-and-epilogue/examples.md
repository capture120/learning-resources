# Introduction & Epilogue: Examples and Stories

### Bret Taylor as product manager at Google (Foreword)
Bret Taylor's first job out of Stanford's CS program was as a product manager at Google, where he helped create Google Maps. He observed that large companies judge product managers less on product success than on managing stakeholders — engaging PR early enough, integrating with the CEO's pet project, winning over a competing executive before a big review. This bureaucratic reward structure trains engineers and product managers coming out of large companies to care about low-leverage activities, which is why many struggle with the concept of leverage. The most successful engineers he worked with saw past these idiosyncrasies and found the one or two things that would really impact their product's success.
**Illustrates:** Organizations can reward low-leverage work; effective engineers identify the few things that truly drive impact.

### Paul Buchheit at FriendFeed (Foreword)
Paul Buchheit, creator of Gmail and co-founder of FriendFeed with Taylor, Jim Norris, and Sanjeev Singh in mid-2007, taught Taylor more about leverage than anyone. When Taylor asked "How should we do this?" about a hard technical problem, Buchheit would counter with "Why do we need to do it at all?" — challenging assumptions so the team could work around impossible problems instead of solving them. It sometimes looked like laziness, but he was almost always right: unless a project would make or break the young company, spending precious engineering resources on it was not justified. This convinced Taylor that engineering is much more about leverage than programming ability.
**Illustrates:** Question whether work needs to be done at all; leverage matters more than programming ability.

### Bret Taylor as Facebook CTO and at Quip (Foreword)
After Facebook acquired FriendFeed, Taylor became Facebook's CTO and spent as much time canceling projects as creating them. At Quip, the company he founded with Kevin Gibbs in 2012, the conviction that effective engineering does not correlate with hours of effort led them to proudly adopt a nine-to-five culture, unheard of at Silicon Valley companies. Taylor argues the endless-hours culture is unnecessary, abused by ineffective managers, unsustainable for people with families, and creates a homogeneous, immature atmosphere.
**Illustrates:** Working smart over working hard; eliminating low-leverage projects is itself high-leverage work.

### Edmond Lau's early startup years (Introduction)
At his first startups, Lau's team almost never worked under 60 hours a week, with months of 70–80 hour weeks — working through lunch, after dinner, until midnight, and even during family holidays. He assumed that as underdogs, more hours meant more value and better odds of success. Several experiences broke that assumption: a custom analytics module he spent two weeks building that the customer never used; content-quality tools the team spent months tweaking and perfecting that never got the desired user adoption; weekly traffic spikes followed by hours manually spinning servers up and down; and a text message during a hike up the Mauna Loa volcano in Hawaii asking him to fix the customer analytics reporting infrastructure that had gone down. He later concluded that overwork decreases productivity, causes burnout, and can make output negative when fatigued engineers' mistakes must be repaired. In the epilogue he sorts these wasted hours into three categories: building products and features customers didn't use, maintaining infrastructure that software could automate, and being stuck on tasks where he was the bottleneck.
**Illustrates:** Hours worked do not equal impact; not all effort translates into value.

### Lau's career-long search for effectiveness (Introduction)
Over more than a decade as a software engineer at Microsoft, Google, Ooyala, Quora, and Quip, Lau kept asking what makes an effective engineer. He screened thousands of resumes, interviewed over five hundred candidates, debated them in hiring committees, built onboarding and mentoring programs, and trained dozens of new hires — always circling the question of whether an individual would effectively get things done. He also interviewed dozens of engineering leaders and read shelves of productivity, team-building, psychology, business, and self-help books, adapting their lessons to engineering. The result is the leverage framework the book presents.
**Illustrates:** Effectiveness can be studied, distilled into a framework, and taught.

### The efficient-but-ineffective engineer (Introduction)
Lau gives two hypothetical failures of efficiency without value: an engineer who efficiently builds infrastructure that scales to millions of requests for an internal tool that at most a hundred people will use, and an engineer who builds a feature only 0.1% of users adopt when other features could reach 10% adoption (excusable only if that 0.1% generates disproportionately more business value).
**Illustrates:** Effectiveness requires choosing which results to deliver, not just delivering them efficiently.

### Mike Krieger at Instagram (Introduction, preview)
Instagram co-founder Mike Krieger followed key engineering principles that enabled a small team of 13 to scale effectively and support a product with over 40 million users. The book covers his principles in a later chapter.
**Illustrates:** High-leverage habits let small teams produce outsized impact.

### Bobby Johnson at Facebook (Introduction, preview)
Former Facebook director Bobby Johnson fostered key habits in his infrastructure team as the social network grew past a billion users. His story appears in a later chapter alongside others from Google, Facebook, Dropbox, Box, and Etsy.
**Illustrates:** Effectiveness habits scale from individual contributors to leaders of large systems.

### Leverage in personal finances (Epilogue)
When planning finances, time spent negotiating salary and setting up investment asset allocation can be worth tens or hundreds of thousands of dollars down the line, while agonizing over a coffee habit saves only tens of dollars. Spend time in proportion to the stakes.
**Illustrates:** Leverage applies beyond engineering — allocate time by impact.

### Leverage in trips, events, and time-buying (Epilogue)
When planning trips or events, decide first on the parts that matter most — location, food, activities, invitees — before sweating small details. Similar leverage calculations apply when deciding whether to hire a virtual assistant, outsource a task to a remote team, or take Uber or Lyft instead of waiting for public transportation.
**Illustrates:** Prioritize the highest-impact parts of any plan first.

### Writing The Effective Engineer itself (Epilogue)
While writing the book as a one-person team, Lau had to consciously remind himself that an hour spent collecting feedback frequently provided higher leverage than a siloed hour spent writing and editing.
**Illustrates:** Validating ideas early beats working in isolation — the author applied his own framework.
