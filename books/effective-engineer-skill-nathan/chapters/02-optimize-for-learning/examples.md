# Chapter 2 Examples: Optimize for Learning

### Edmond Lau leaves Google

Fresh out of MIT in 2006, the author joined Google's Search Quality team and found both a corporate and intellectual playground: codelabs on Protocol Buffers, BigTable, and MapReduce, internal design docs, source code from engineers like Jeff Dean and Sanjay Ghemawat, and tech talks by Joshua Bloch and Guido van Rossum. His three-person team built and launched query suggestions on google.com in five months, demoing to Marissa Mayer and getting final approval from Larry Page and Sergey Brin. Despite the perks (free food, trips to Squaw Valley, Disneyland, and Maui), he left after two years because his learning curve had plateaued; the five-month launch turned out to be the exception, and projecting another year there felt unsatisfying. At Ooyala and Quora he found growth opportunities that would have been much harder to get at Google.
**Illustrates:** Optimize for learning when choosing where to work; leave when your learning curve plateaus.

### The author's "yes man" year

An introvert who avoided coffee meetings, parties, and networking events, the author resolved one year (like Jim Carrey's character in Yes Man) to accept every social invitation. Early conversations were awkward and some events yielded nothing, but he kept iterating: reviewing botched conversations, practicing better stories, and holding the belief that conversation is a learnable skill. He made great friends and contacts and stretched his comfort zone, and the skill later helped him interview engineers for the book.
**Illustrates:** Growth mindset — treating any ability as learnable through effort and practice.

### Carol Dweck's mindset research

Stanford psychologist Carol Dweck, in her book Mindset (written after 20 years of research), found people hold either a fixed mindset (qualities are carved in stone, so failure proves inadequacy and people stick to what validates them) or a growth mindset (abilities grow through effort, so failure is a learning opportunity). Mindset predicts whether people improve.
**Illustrates:** Adopt a growth mindset — mindset determines whether you learn or stagnate.

### Hong Kong university remedial English study

At a Hong Kong university where all classes were taught in English, Dweck offered struggling English speakers a remedial language class. Among students with a growth mindset, 73% enrolled; among fixed-mindset students, only 13% did — if you believe intelligence is fixed, trying to improve seems pointless.
**Illustrates:** Growth mindset drives people to actually take improvement opportunities.

### New York City 7th-grade math study

Two groups of 7th graders in NYC public schools were compared. Students taught that intelligence can be increased through experience and hard work improved their math grades over the year; the control group's math scores actually worsened.
**Illustrates:** Believing ability is growable measurably improves outcomes.

### Tamar Bercovici at Box

Bercovici joined Box's 30-person engineering team in 2011 with no full-time web development experience, coming from a theoretical, math-heavy PhD background at an Israeli university. Interviewers assumed she didn't enjoy coding, that her PhD provided few practical advantages, and that she couldn't ramp up quickly. Rather than retreat to theory, she studied web technologies, extracted the engineering-relevant lessons from her PhD, and practiced whiteboard interviews — landing the job and rising within two years to staff engineer and manager leading the distributed data systems team at a company serving over 200,000 businesses (Box went public in January 2015). Her advice to new hires: "own your story" — don't apologize for resume gaps; tell who you are, what skills you've built, and what you're excited to do next.
**Illustrates:** Owning your story — taking control of what's in your sphere of influence instead of letting others define you.

### Compound interest arithmetic

The chapter uses bank-account math to show why rate matters: a 5% account beats a 4% account by 49% after 40 years and 82% after 60; doubling 4% to 8% yields almost 5x higher returns after 40 years and 11x after 60. Learning compounds the same way, so small deltas in learning rate dominate long-run outcomes, and starting early (like early 401(k) contributions) extends compounding.
**Illustrates:** Invest in your rate of learning — small rate differences compound enormously.

### Stephen Cohen of Palantir on cushy jobs

In a Stanford guest lecture, Stephen Cohen — co-founder of Palantir, which powers the infrastructure of intelligence agencies like the CIA, FBI, and MI6 — argued that companies offering cushy, unchallenging 9-to-5 jobs are effectively paying you to accept a much lower intellectual growth rate. Because intelligence compounds, the cost of the missing long-term compounding is enormous — and the scary outcome is complacency and stalling.
**Illustrates:** The huge opportunity cost of unchallenging work.

### Reid Hoffman's "startup of you"

LinkedIn co-founder Reid Hoffman, in The Startup of You, suggests treating yourself like a startup: startups prioritize learning over profitability early, launch in beta, and iterate as they learn what customers want. Likewise, treat yourself as a permanent work-in-progress to invest in and iterate on daily.
**Illustrates:** Continuous self-iteration as a growth-mindset practice.

### Zappos and the 1% challenge

Pursuing growth and learning is one of Zappos's ten core values; the company grew revenue from zero to over $1.2 billion in ten years. CEO Tony Hsieh, who advocates continuous iteration in his book Delivering Happiness, and CFO Alfred Lin gave employees a standing challenge: improve just 1% per day, compounding, and you end the year 37x better — not 3.65x.
**Illustrates:** Small daily learning investments compound dramatically.

### Sheryl Sandberg's rocket ship

When Sandberg was deciding whether to join Google, CEO Eric Schmidt advised: if you're offered a seat on a rocket ship, don't ask what seat — just get on. Focusing on growth served her: she became a VP at Google, which opened the path to becoming Facebook's COO.
**Illustrates:** Fast growth (factor 1) — prioritize company growth over role details.

### Google engEDU and Facebook Bootcamp

Google invests heavily in engEDU, a program of classes, seminars, design documents, and programming guides for growing engineers and leaders. Facebook runs a six-week onboarding program called Bootcamp where new engineers learn the company's tools and focus areas while doing initial hands-on development. Smaller teams that value fast ramp-up build similar programs, as the author's team did at Quora.
**Illustrates:** Training (factor 2) — strong onboarding signals a learning-conducive environment.

### Google's search UI review bottleneck

While the author was at Google, any visible change to Google search — even experimental ones — had to go up the management chain to a weekly user interface review with Marissa Mayer, VP of Search Products and User Experience, slowing experimentation.
**Illustrates:** Pace (factor 4) — approval bureaucracy slows iteration and thus learning.

### The author's project breadth at Quora

In three years at Quora, the author worked on experimentation tools, real-time analytics frameworks, site speed, infrastructure, recommendations, spam detection, and mobile development, plus organizational work: training interviewers, creating onboarding resources, building the mentoring program, and coordinating the internship program. That diversity would have been hard to get at a larger company with specialized teams and established processes.
**Illustrates:** Autonomy (factor 6) — smaller companies offer broader surface area and ownership.

### Google's 20% time

Google pioneered "20% time," letting engineers spend the equivalent of a day per week on side projects to improve the company. Initially controversial, it produced Gmail, Google News, and AdSense — now three of Google's core offerings — and many other companies adopted similar policies.
**Illustrates:** Dedicating protected time to development pays off; carve out your own 20% time.

### Bobby Johnson on fearless engineers

Bobby Johnson, a former engineering director at Facebook, concluded after years of observation that engineering success was highly correlated with having no fear of jumping into unfamiliar code. Fear of failure makes people give up before trying, but digging into things you don't know makes you a better coder.
**Illustrates:** Jump fearlessly into code you don't know.

### Mobile trend statistics (2014)

To show how to spot high-demand skills, the chapter cites industry data: as of 2014, 15% of Internet traffic came from mobile devices, and global annual smartphone sales were triple those of consumer PCs — trends suggesting mobile development expertise would open opportunities.
**Illustrates:** Invest in skills that are in high demand by reading industry trends.

### Bill Gates's reading habit

Bill Gates reads a lot, mostly non-fiction, using books to discover how the world works. Books let you re-apply others' lessons and mistakes without starting from scratch. The author found this so high-leverage he trained himself to speed read, now reading 2-3x faster and 1-2 books per week.
**Illustrates:** Reading as a high-leverage learning investment outside work.

### Benjamin Franklin's mutual improvement club

In the eighteenth century, Franklin organized friends into a "club of mutual improvement" that met every Friday evening to discuss and debate morals, politics, and natural philosophy, giving members a structured way to better themselves. The author belongs to book clubs and reading groups in the same spirit.
**Illustrates:** Discussion groups as a structured outside-work learning habit.

### Richard Wiseman's The Luck Factor

Wiseman writes that lucky people dramatically increase their chance of lucky encounters by meeting many people; the more people they meet, the greater the odds of running into someone who positively affects their lives. This converted the author from avoiding coffee meetings to valuing a broad network.
**Illustrates:** Building a network multiplies serendipitous opportunities.

### Richard Feynman's teaching technique

Physics Nobel laureate Richard Feynman learned faster by writing to teach others: explaining ideas deepens understanding of familiar material and pinpoints details you didn't fully grasp.
**Illustrates:** Write to teach — teaching as a learning accelerator.

### Television watching statistics

The average American watches 34 hours of TV per week, and psychology studies show the average mood while watching TV is mildly depressed. The chapter contrasts this passive time with actively pursuing what you love.
**Illustrates:** Replace passive consumption with passion-fueled learning.
