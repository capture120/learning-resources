# Chapter 1 Examples: Focus on High-Leverage Activities

### Quora's onboarding and mentoring programs (Edmond Lau)

In the summer of 2012, Quora's engineering team doubled in three months to pursue its mission of building a question-and-answer platform to share and grow the world's knowledge — an "internet-scale Library of Alexandria." The 14 new hires crammed into a small Palo Alto office at 261 Hamilton Avenue; even after construction crews knocked down three sets of walls to expand the space, the team could barely fit everyone's desks. The author volunteered to own onboarding, working with cofounder Charlie Cheever, and reasoned that ramping up half the engineering team would have more impact than any code he could write personally. He researched other companies' programs, then built a mentoring program (each hire paired with a mentor for two to three months, responsible for the hire's success) and an onboarding program of 10 recurring tech talks and 10 codelabs, an idea borrowed from Google. The stakes were high because Quora deployed code to production multiple times a day. Many hires deployed their first bug fix or small feature to users by the end of their first week; that summer the team shipped a new Android app, an internal analytics system, better recommendations, and news feed improvements while code quality stayed high. Mentors met regularly to keep improving the onboarding process, and dozens of engineers kept going through the programs after the author left.
**Illustrates:** Investing in people and reusable resources is among the highest-leverage activities a team can do.

### The mentoring time-investment math

A typical engineer works between 1,880 and 2,820 hours per year. Spending one hour a day for a new hire's first month (20 hours) on mentoring is only about 1% of the hire's first-year work time, yet it can strongly influence the productivity of the other 99% — for example, by teaching a useful UNIX command, walking through debugging tools, reviewing code early to prevent bad habits, teaching prioritization, or planning starter projects that teach core abstractions.
**Illustrates:** Leverage as ROI — a small, well-placed time investment produces disproportionate impact.

### Archimedes' lever

The Greek mathematician and engineer Archimedes declared that with a place to stand and a long enough lever, he could move the world. A boulder you cannot budge alone moves easily with a powerful lever; high-leverage activities work the same way, amplifying limited time and effort into much larger impact.
**Illustrates:** The core metaphor behind leverage as the yardstick for effectiveness.

### Andrew Grove's High Output Management

Former Intel CEO Andrew Grove explains that overall leverage — value produced per unit time — can only be increased in three ways: reducing the time an activity takes, increasing the activity's output, or shifting to higher-leverage activities. The chapter converts these into three questions to ask about any task.
**Illustrates:** The three-way framework for systematically increasing leverage.

### Three worked scenarios: meetings, feature work, performance tuning

The chapter applies the three questions to concrete roles. For a one-hour team progress meeting: default to 30 minutes, circulate an agenda and goals beforehand, or replace the meeting with email and build a feature instead. For a product engineer starting a customer-facing feature: automate manual development or testing steps, prioritize tasks by launch criticality, or ask customer support about pain points to find a more valuable feature. For a performance engineer fixing web app bottlenecks: master a profiling tool to find bottlenecks faster, measure page performance together with visit frequency to fix the highest-traffic bottlenecks first, or work with product teams so speed is designed in from the start rather than patched later.
**Illustrates:** How the three leverage questions translate into everyday engineering decisions.

### Yishan Wong builds Facebook's hiring culture

Yishan Wong — an early director of engineering at Facebook and later CEO of Reddit — shared this story with the author as his proudest accomplishment at Facebook. When Wong entered a management position there in late 2006, engineers saw recruiting and interviews as distractions from real work, an attitude common at many companies. Over four years he gradually applied pressure to shift the mindset until people treated the hiring process as an art form to be mastered: he told engineers that ensuring hire quality was their job, engineers didn't skip interviews for other work, feedback was expected immediately rather than hours or days later, and he pushed recruiters to schedule candidate conversations in the first humanly possible slot (tomorrow becomes 8am; the afternoon becomes 1pm). Even rejected candidates left wanting to work there. Speed and quality in hiring became a competitive advantage — Facebook closed candidates while slower companies dilly-dallied — and once strong people joined, attracting more strong people got easier. The chapter argues Facebook could not have become a $220+ billion company with over 9,000 employees without this emphasis on hiring.
**Illustrates:** High-leverage activities are not easy wins; they can demand consistent effort over years before the outsized payoff.

### Bill Gates and philanthropic leverage points

After retiring as Microsoft's CEO, Bill Gates focused on how to invest his philanthropic billions. The $40.2 billion managed by the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation is still small against a global economy worth tens of trillions, so in a November 2013 Wired essay Gates argued that big impact requires a leverage point — a way for a dollar or an hour to benefit society a hundred or a thousand times over. For Gates, examples include funding measles and malaria vaccines costing under 25 cents each that save millions of lives.
**Illustrates:** Leverage points let limited resources produce outsized impact; engineering has its own equivalents.
