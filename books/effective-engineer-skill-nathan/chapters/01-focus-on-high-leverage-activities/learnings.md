# Chapter 1: Focus on High-Leverage Activities

This chapter (which opens Part 1, "Adopt the Right Mindsets") establishes the book's central metric: leverage, the value produced per unit of time invested. Because time is the one resource that cannot be stored, extended, or replaced, effective engineers do not try to do more by working longer hours; they deliberately choose the activities that produce the most impact for the time spent. The chapter defines leverage, gives three systematic ways to increase it, and warns that high-leverage work is not the same as easy wins.

## Core Principles

- **Leverage is the yardstick for effectiveness.** Leverage = impact produced / time invested — the return on investment for effort. Effectiveness is not about volume of work or hours logged; it is about how much value each hour generates. Effective engineers use this equation as the central, guiding metric for deciding how and where to spend time: increase the numerator (impact) while keeping the denominator (time) small.

- **Time is your most limited resource.** Unlike other resources, time cannot be stored, extended, or replaced, and there will always be more work than time available. This is true whether you code 60-70 hours a week or aim for a minimal 4-Hour-Work-Week schedule. Prioritization is therefore unavoidable, and leverage is the criterion for it.

- **A small share of activities produces most of the impact (Pareto).** For many activities, 80% of the impact comes from 20% of the work. That 20% consists of high-leverage activities — work that produces disproportionately high impact for a relatively small time investment. Like Archimedes' lever moving a boulder, high-leverage activities amplify limited time and effort into far greater impact.

- **Overall leverage can only be increased in three ways.** Drawing on Andrew Grove's *High Output Management*: reduce the time an activity takes, increase the output of the activity, or shift to a higher-leverage activity. Every improvement to effectiveness is one of these three moves; asking which one applies turns leverage from an abstraction into a daily decision tool.

- **Direct energy toward leverage points, not easy wins.** High-leverage does not mean quick or easy. Just as a lever applies small force over a long distance, many high-leverage activities (like Facebook's hiring culture) require consistent effort over months or years before the outsized payoff arrives — and the payoff can compound, as when strong hires make it easier to attract more strong hires. The author calls focusing on high-leverage activities the single most valuable lesson of his professional life.

- **Investing in people multiplies team output.** Time spent on onboarding, mentoring, and training pays off because a team's success depends more on the whole team than on any single engineer. A tiny fraction of a new hire's time (about 1% of their first year) spent on training can substantially raise the productivity of the other 99%.

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

- **Leverage (definition):** Leverage = impact produced / time invested. Equivalently, the ROI of effort. Total output is the sum of the outputs of individual activities, and each daily activity (meetings, email, bug fixes, feature work, code review, interviews) has its own leverage. Being busy does not mean producing value: web surfing or email can consume as much time as fixing an important bug but generate far less value.

- **The Pareto principle (80-20 rule) applied to engineering:** 80% of impact comes from 20% of the work; identify and prioritize that 20%.

- **Grove's three ways to increase leverage**, translated into three questions to ask about any activity:
  1. How can I complete this activity in a shorter amount of time?
  2. How can I increase the value produced by this activity?
  3. Is there something else I could spend my time on that would produce more value?

- **Leverage point (Gates's term):** a place where one dollar of funding or one hour of effort benefits society (or your goals) a hundred or a thousand times as much. Engineering has its own leverage points; each chapter of the book presents one high-leverage habit. The author cautions that the book is not a substitute for mindful reflection — the habits can shortcut your path to finding your own leverage points, but you still have to look for them yourself.

- **The mentoring math:** a typical engineer works 1,880-2,820 hours per year. One hour of mentoring per day for a new hire's first month (20 hours) is only about 1% of their first-year working time, yet it can dramatically improve the productivity of the remaining 99%.

- **Reusable resources beat one-off effort:** materials like codelabs (documents explaining why core abstractions were designed, how to use them, walking through the code, with exercises) pay even higher dividends than one-on-one time because they require little upkeep after the upfront investment.

## Actionable Practices

- For every activity you undertake, ask the three leverage questions: can I do it faster, can I make it produce more value, and should I be doing something else entirely?

- Apply the three questions to meetings: default to a half-hour meeting instead of an hour; prepare an agenda and goals and circulate them to attendees beforehand; replace the meeting with an email discussion when in-person conversation is not actually necessary, and spend the freed time building an important feature.

- Apply them to feature development: automate manual parts of development or testing to iterate faster; prioritize tasks by how critical they are for launch to maximize shipped value; talk with customer support about customers' biggest pain points to check whether a different feature would produce more value with less effort.

- Apply them to performance work: learn a profiling tool well to find bottlenecks faster; measure both page performance and visit frequency so you fix the bottlenecks affecting the most traffic first; work with product teams to design performant software from the outset, so speed is a feature during development rather than a bug fixed later.

- Invest in onboarding and mentoring: pair each new engineer with a mentor for two to three months who owns the hire's success (code review, skill roadmaps, pair programming, discussing tradeoffs and prioritization, guidance on working with teammates, a planned sequence of starter tasks). Build shared foundations with recurring tech talks and codelabs, and have mentors meet regularly to keep improving the onboarding process itself.

- Small teaching investments compound: pointing out a useful UNIX command, walking through debugging tools, reviewing code early and thoroughly to catch common errors before bad habits form, teaching prioritization, and designing good starter projects all raise long-run productivity far beyond their cost.

- Do not mistake easy wins for leverage points. Be willing to apply consistent pressure over long periods (as with building a hiring culture) when the eventual impact is disproportionate.

- Treat hiring as high-leverage work, not a distraction: attend interviews, submit feedback immediately rather than deferring it hours or days, and schedule candidate conversations in the first humanly possible time slot.

## Key Takeaways

- Use leverage to measure your effectiveness as an engineer; concentrate on the work that yields the highest return on invested time.

- Systematically raise the leverage of your time in one of three ways: complete an activity faster, increase the impact the activity produces, or move to a higher-leverage activity altogether.

- Focus effort on leverage points. Time is your scarcest asset, so identify the habits that generate disproportionately high impact per hour invested.
