# Chapter 24: How to Get Better at Strategy

This closing chapter turns the book's method on the reader: it is a template for writing your own personal strategy for developing a strategy practice, structured as exploration (where to find strategies to learn from), diagnosis (how to extract the right lessons from them), policy (how to perform and practice strategy with or without authority), and operations (mechanisms that hold you accountable). The single most important takeaway: you can always do strategy work — if your role or company seems to forbid it, lower your altitude (down to your team or even just yourself) or shift from performing strategy to practicing it; only you can stop yourself from practicing.

## Core ideas

### Learning strategy is slow; treat it as its own strategy project

Larson opens by contrasting Uncle Ben's quick path to riches in *Death of a Salesman* with strategy learning: two decades in, he is "still learning more from each project." There is no shortcut; a book can accelerate the path but not replace practice. The chapter's response is to apply the book's own framework — explore, diagnose, set policy, operate — to your personal development. Why: durable improvement comes from a deliberate, accountable practice, not from passively accumulating reading.

### Exploration: three places to find strategies to learn from

Ideally you would start by reading publicly available strategies, but unfortunately "there simply aren't many easily available" — which is why the other two sources matter so much. The three places to look:

1. **Public resources** — company engineering blogs and the resources collected in the book's Appendix; you can go a long way by searching and prompting your way through them, including via the LLM companion created for the book. But public strategies are often misleading (per Chapter 23): everyone writing in public has an agenda, and that agenda often means omitting details that make them or their company look bad. Read between the lines; don't take them literally.
2. **Private resources** — ironically, easier to find than public ones. Private recollections still contain inaccuracies, but "the incentives to massage the truth are less pronounced in private." Strategies are often **oral histories**, shared freely among peers within and across companies; once your network exists, you can usually get a company's strategy on any topic "by just asking." Sensitive strategies are an exception only until their existence becomes externally obvious — and people tend to overestimate how much information companies can keep private anyway (even job postings leak a lot).
3. **Learning communities** — ongoing learning circles you build with others (see below).

### Strategy archaeologists and building a repository

Few organizations formally collect strategies into a repository, but tenured members informally collect the stories — they are the company's **strategy archaeologists**, and consulting them teaches a great deal. You can become one yourself regardless of tenure: start building your own strategy repository. Collecting strategies attracts others to contribute theirs, and over time you can foster a documentation culture where none existed. Why this matters: building that culture requires no explicit authority — "just an ongoing show of excitement."

### Learning circles are the best mechanism for learning strategy

Larson formed a learning circle when he first became an executive and has run it for over five years; what surprised him most is how much he learned from it. Four reasons ongoing circles beat other formats:

- **Bidirectional discussion** teaches far more than one-way media like conference talks and documents.
- **Groups** let you learn from others' experiences and questions instead of guiding all learning yourself.
- **Continuity** lets you see a strategy at inception, during rollout, and after it has been in practice — the full lifecycle, not a snapshot.
- **Trust builds slowly**: people only share the full details of a problem after you've held trust about smaller things. An ongoing group makes this feasible; a transient group does not.

The trade-off: putting one of these communities together requires real commitment — but they remain the best mechanism Larson has found.

On joining: people get stuck asking how to get invited to an existing circle, but "that's almost always the wrong question." If you want to join a learning circle, **make one** — that is how Larson got invited to his.

### Diagnosis: collecting strategies is only step one

You must determine what to take away from each strategy — e.g., whether Calm's approach to resourcing engineering-driven projects is something to copy or to avoid. Method: apply the strategy evaluation rubric from Chapter 23 to each collected strategy. Splitting a strategy into its phases teaches a lot; applying the rubric to each phase teaches more. Repetition makes you faster and reveals recurring patterns across strategies.

Diagnostic questions to ask (pick the two most interesting per strategy, not all of them):

- How long did it take to discover that the initial phase could be improved, and how costly was funding that discovery?
- Why did the strategy reach its final stage and get repealed or replaced? How long did that take?
- If you had to pick one, did it fail in exploration, diagnosis, policy, or operations?
- Did the strategy outlive its primary author's tenure — repealed quickly after departure, endured, or replaced during their tenure?
- Would you repeat this strategy or avoid it? If you'd repeat it, what conditions seem necessary for success?
- How might you apply it to your current opportunities and challenges?

### Policy: implement strategy, and practice implementing strategy

The two high-level policies are **implementing strategy** and **practicing implementing strategy**. The detailed options are conditional on your company's current state (see Actionable guidance). Key rule: trying everything at once is overwhelming — pick one option per phase, and if you can't gain traction, switch approaches until something works. Diagnosis matters here too: recognize *where* things aren't working. For example, if you lack sponsorship to enforce strategy, switch to suggesting strategies instead; acknowledging the constraint is what lets you find an approach that works.

### You can always do strategy work

If you look for a reason you can't do strategy in your environment, you'll always find one (echoing Chapter 3). "Don't believe the hype: you can always do strategy work." Two escapes when your role seems to prevent it:

1. **Lower your altitude** — there is always a scale where you can perform strategy, even if it's just your team or just yourself. "Only you can forbid yourself from developing personal strategies."
2. **Practice rather than perform** — organizations can only absorb so much strategy development at a time, so they sometimes won't accept more performed strategy. But "only you can stop yourself from practicing."

### Operations: personal habits need mechanisms too

The book's refrain applies to yourself: even the best policies accomplish little without operational mechanisms that ensure they happen and, if they don't, debug why. It is tempting to skip operations for personal habits — they're easiest to neglect precisely because others rarely inquire about them — yet habits profoundly shape you long-term. The recommended mechanisms: track your strategy work, review it quarterly (ideally with peers, because "it's too easy to deceive yourself, but much harder to trick someone else"), and run a debugging session with a trusted person (ideally equally or more experienced than you) when the review shows you're not doing the work.

### "Too busy for strategy" is usually a diagnosis problem

Many companies convince themselves they're in too much of a rush to make good decisions; Larson admits getting stuck in this view himself. His current stance: at this point in his career he recognizes he has the tools to create time for strategy and "an obligation to do strategy rather than inflicting poor decisions" on the organizations he works in. There is an honest endpoint, though: if you try the escalation ladder (track → start one → practice instead) and still make no progress, accept that you don't actually view strategy as important right now, examine why, and — if comfortable with the answer — maybe return to the practice later.

### Hold the book's ideas loosely

Larson closes by invoking Hegel: "even the best ideas here are wrong in interesting ways, and will be surpassed by better ones." He'd never ask you to wholly agree with his ideas — they're his best thinking on the topic, but expect them to be surpassed by better ones.

## Actionable guidance

**Find strategies to learn from**
- Search public engineering blogs and curated resource lists, but read between the lines; assume agendas shape what's omitted.
- Ask peers in your professional network for their companies' strategies on topics you care about — most will share, especially once a strategy's existence is externally obvious.
- Mine job postings and other public artifacts for what they reveal about a company's internal strategy.
- Find and consult your organization's strategy archaeologists — tenured people who informally hold the strategy stories.
- Start your own strategy repository; let your visible excitement recruit contributors and seed a documentation culture. No authority required.
- Use conference hallway tracks and privacy-committed dinners to collect oral histories.
- If you want to be in a learning circle, create one rather than waiting for an invitation. Favor ongoing groups over transient ones so trust and lifecycle visibility can accumulate.

**Diagnose what you collect**
- Split each collected strategy into phases, then apply the Chapter 23 evaluation rubric to the whole and to each phase.
- For each strategy, pick the two most interesting diagnostic questions from the list above and dig in — don't grind through all six every time.
- Look for recurring patterns as your sample of analyzed strategies grows.

**Choose a policy matched to your company's state**
- Existing strategies failing? Debug and fix one. If you lack company-scope authority, decrease altitude until you find a scope you can work at (organization → team → self).
- No documented strategies? Document one to make it debuggable — again at whatever altitude is within reach.
- Effective strategy with low adoption? Iterate on operational mechanisms to raise adoption; many (low-noise nudges, model-document-share) require no authority.
- Effective strategies with high adoption? Build excitement for a new one: ask Staff-plus engineers and senior managers which problems they believe are important, and mine that vein.
- Uncomfortable sharing internally? Share proposals with a few trusted peers, a learning circle, or only trusted external peers.
- Pick one approach per phase. If it gains no traction, diagnose why (e.g., missing sponsorship → switch from enforcing to suggesting) and try another.

**Operate your improvement loop**
- Track every strategy you implement, refine, document, or read in a document, spreadsheet, or folder.
- Review the tracker quarterly: are you doing the expected amount of work in the expected way? If not, why not? Review with a peer or learning circle to defeat self-deception.
- If a review shows you're not doing the work, schedule one hour with someone you trust — ideally equally or more experienced — to debug what's going wrong. Commit to doing this before the next review.

**Create time when "too busy"**
- Not tracking strategy creation? Start tracking first.
- No strategy work in six months? Start with one.
- Implementation prohibitively time-consuming? Practice a strategy instead of implementing one.
- Still stuck after all three? Accept that you don't currently value the practice, reflect on why, and consciously defer rather than pretend.

## Pitfalls and misconceptions

- **Taking public strategies literally.** Public write-ups are shaped by agendas; important unflattering details get omitted. Reading them at face value teaches the wrong lessons.
- **Collecting without diagnosing.** Gathering strategies is only the first step; without applying a rubric you won't know whether a given strategy is a model to copy or a cautionary tale.
- **Waiting for an invitation to a learning circle.** "How do I get invited?" is the wrong question; make the circle.
- **Believing your role forbids strategy work.** If you hunt for a reason you can't do strategy, you will always find one. Lower altitude or switch to practicing.
- **Trying every improvement policy at once.** It's overwhelming; pick one per phase and iterate.
- **Skipping operations for personal habits.** Habits are the easiest policies to neglect because nobody else asks about them — which is exactly why they need tracking and external review.
- **Solo self-review.** It's too easy to deceive yourself; review progress with peers who are harder to trick.
- **The "too busy" trap.** Rushing past strategy means inflicting poor decisions on your organization; busyness is usually a prioritization choice, not a fixed constraint.
- **Treating the framework as final.** Even the best ideas here "are wrong in interesting ways"; expect to revise them.
