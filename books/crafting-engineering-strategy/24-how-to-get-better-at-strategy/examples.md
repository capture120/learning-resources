# Chapter 24: How to Get Better at Strategy — Examples

## Uncle Ben's jungle (Death of a Salesman)

Larson opens with Arthur Miller's 1949 play, quoting Uncle Ben: "When I was seventeen, I walked into the jungle, and when I was twenty-one I walked out. And by God I was rich." He wishes learning engineering strategy worked that way, but by all accounts it is a much slower path — two decades in, he is still learning from each project.
Illustrates: there is no four-year shortcut to strategy mastery; the chapter exists to structure a long, deliberate practice.

## Larson's own learning circle

When Larson first moved into an executive role, he formed a learning circle rather than waiting to be invited to one, and he has run it for more than five years. What surprised him most is how much he learned from it — its bidirectional discussion, group learning, continuity across a strategy's lifecycle, and slowly built trust made it the best learning mechanism he has found. When people ask how to get invited to an existing circle, his answer is that creating one is how he got invited to his.
Illustrates: make the learning circle you want to join; ongoing communities beat transient ones for strategy learning.

## Peers sharing "private" strategies freely

Larson finds privately held strategy resources easier to obtain than public ones: strategies circulate as oral histories among peers within and across companies, and once you have a network you can usually get a company's strategy on a topic just by asking. Even a close peer won't share a sensitive strategy before its existence is externally obvious, but will gladly do so afterward — and people tend to overestimate how much information companies can keep private anyway. Even reading recent job postings can expose a surprising amount about a company.
Illustrates: private channels carry less-spun strategy information than public write-ups, and corporate secrecy is weaker than people assume.

## Strategy archaeologists

Few organizations formally collect their strategies into a repository, but tenured members informally accumulate the stories — Larson calls them the company's strategy archaeologists. You can consult them to learn the institutional history, or become one yourself at any tenure by starting a strategy repository; as you collect, others get interested in contributing, and a documentation culture forms with no explicit authority needed, "just an ongoing show of excitement."
Illustrates: institutional strategy knowledge lives in people, and anyone can start capturing it without permission.

## Conference hallway tracks and private dinners

Beyond peers and archaeologists, Larson points to the "hallway track" at conferences and to organizing dinners where people share stories under a commitment to privacy as further sources of strategy oral history.
Illustrates: deliberately created low-stakes, trusted settings are where candid strategy details surface.

## Calm's Engineering-driven projects as a diagnosis exercise

As an example of why collecting strategies isn't enough, Larson cites Calm's approach to resourcing engineering-driven projects (Document 21-2 earlier in the book): you must determine whether it is something to copy or something to avoid. The prescribed method is to apply the Chapter 23 evaluation rubric to the strategy and to each of its phases.
Illustrates: every collected strategy demands an explicit verdict — model or cautionary tale — reached through structured diagnosis.

## Larson's "too busy" admission

Larson confesses he has himself gotten stuck believing he was in too much of a rush to make good decisions. At this point in his career he recognizes he has the tools to create time for strategy and an obligation to do it rather than inflict poor decisions on his organizations.
Illustrates: "too busy for strategy" afflicts even experienced practitioners, and outgrowing it is a recognized obligation, not a luxury.

## Tracking personal goals (2024 year in review)

Larson notes that tracking personal habits can feel odd but is worthwhile; he has been setting and tracking personal goals for some time now, as discussed in his 2024 year in review, and has benefited greatly.
Illustrates: the book's operations discipline (track, review, debug) applies to personal development, not just organizations.

## Hegel's worldview as the closing frame

In the final words, Larson says he would never ask readers to wholly agree with his ideas. On strategy he is certain Hegel's worldview is correct: "even the best ideas here are wrong in interesting ways, and will be surpassed by better ones."
Illustrates: hold any strategy framework, including this book's, as a provisional thesis awaiting synthesis.
