# Chapter 25: Strategy Resources — Examples

The chapter is an annotated bibliography, so its "examples" are the recommended resources themselves, especially the public case studies it offers as stand-ins for the rarely published real thing.

## Stripe: "Magnitudes of Exploration" (2019)

Larson's own essay documents a public version of Stripe's engineering strategy. He offers it as one of the few publicly available accounts of a real company's engineering strategy.
Illustrates: a genuine engineering strategy made public, partially closing the gap created by companies keeping strategies private.

## Liberty Mutual and serverless (The Value Flywheel Effect)

The book by David Anderson, with Mark McCann and Michael O'Reilly, introduces Wardley mapping by walking through Liberty Mutual's rationale for adopting serverless. It doubles as a case-study collection, also covering A Cloud Guru, Workgrid, and the BBC.
Illustrates: using Wardley maps as the exploration/mapping tool that justifies a major technical guiding policy.

## Intercom: "Run Less Software" (2018)

Rich Archbold's writeup describes a cornerstone of Intercom's engineering strategy: running less software. Larson calls it "a fantastic writeup."
Illustrates: a single principle serving as the cornerstone of a company's engineering strategy.

## Slack: "How Big Technical Changes Happen at Slack" (2020)

Keith Adams and Johnny Rodgers describe how major technical changes get made at Slack. Larson notes it is "not quite Slack's engineering strategy" but contains many components of one.
Illustrates: strategy components often surface in writing about change processes even when no document is labeled "strategy."

## Financial Times: "The Difficult Teenage Years" (2019)

Anna Shipman examines setting tech strategy at the Financial Times after a launch, where the strategy "wasn't really defined until somewhat late in the lifecycle." Larson adds that this is "extremely common, even if we don't admit it."
Illustrates: strategies often aren't defined until late in the lifecycle; this is extremely common, even if rarely admitted.

## The Phoenix Project as a mapping strategy

Gene Kim, Kevin Behr, and George Spafford's novel retells Goldratt's *The Goal*, showing how to model and resolve problems using constraint optimization. Larson previously would not have called it a strategy book, but under his evolved definition of strategy as "mapping plus guiding policies," it demonstrates a useful mapping strategy.
Illustrates: how redefining strategy to include mapping expands what counts as strategy literature.

## "Why limiting work-in-progress works"

Larson cites his own essay as an example of applying Donella Meadows-style systems thinking to model a software system, showing why work-in-progress limits help.
Illustrates: systems thinking from *Thinking in Systems* applied concretely as a mapping mechanism for software development.

## BoringTechnology.club

Dan McKinley's site offers what Larson frames as "a guiding principle that many engineering strategies include."
Illustrates: a single principle that many engineering strategies include as a guiding principle.

## GitLab's company strategy

GitLab publishes its company strategy openly. It is not an engineering strategy, but since GitLab is a technology company building technology for technologists, Larson finds it "an interesting read at a slightly higher altitude than an engineering strategy."
Illustrates: reading strategy documents one altitude above engineering still informs engineering strategy work.

## How Big Things Get Done: megaproject benchmarking

Bent Flyvbjerg and Dan Gardner study why some megaprojects fail resoundingly while others come in under budget and ahead of schedule. Larson connects it to strategy via benchmarking, which can help evaluate guiding policies within a strategy.
Illustrates: benchmarking as a way to evaluate a strategy's guiding policies.

## Enterprise Architecture as Strategy: the "IT" era

Ross, Weill, and Robertson's 2006 book traces the evolution and maturity of software within businesses back when it was called "IT," and how to decide among strategies for coupling and integration across business units.
Illustrates: strategy choices about integration and coupling across business units, viewed through an earlier era's lens.
