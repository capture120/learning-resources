# Chapter 6: Exploring — Examples

## The Grand Migration antipattern

A newly hired senior leader declares that a massive migration to a new technology stack — typically the stack from their former employer, where they felt comfortable — will solve every pressing problem in the organization. The defining feature is not the initial bad pick but the single-minded ferocity with which the leader keeps pushing the migration even after it becomes abundantly clear to others that it doesn't solve the actual problem. These leaders are highly intelligent, but they built their whole strategy on early assumptions from prior experience, and once enough weight rests on those assumptions, admitting error becomes impossible.
Illustrates: how early anchoring without exploration dooms strategies, and why failure compounds the longer the anchor goes unexamined.

## Uber service migration: gathering industry papers

When Larson worked on Uber's service migration strategy, the exploration started by collecting recent papers on Mesos, Kubernetes, and Aurora to understand the state of the industry on orchestration. The team also read industry papers including "Large-scale Cluster Management at Google with Borg" and "Mesos: A Platform for Fine-Grained Resource Sharing in the Data Center". None of the readings held complete answers, but they mapped the range of options and surfaced further references to consult.
Illustrates: step 1 of the exploration process (gather every related resource) and the "read narrowly" motion for a specific strategy.

## Calm engineering strategy: asking peers

For the engineering strategy at Calm, Larson's exploration centered on conversations with industry peers about tools they had used to focus a team with diffuse goals. Step 2 also calls for web searching, foundational-model prompting, and checking with a few current and prior colleagues about what topics and resources you might be missing.
Illustrates: step 2 of the exploration process — using searches and your network to find what you don't know you're missing.

## Monolith decomposition strategy: synthesizing into a summary

The monolith decomposition strategy condensed the exploration of a broad topic into just four readable paragraphs with links out to the underlying references.
Illustrates: step 4 — synthesizing raw exploration notes into a concise summary others can actually read.

## Texting peers mid-meeting

In a recent strategy session, a proposal came up that seemed off to Larson. He texted industry peers during the meeting and got answers back before it ended — answers that invalidated the room's assumptions about what was and wasn't possible. A disagreement that might have dragged on for weeks was resolved in minutes, and the group settled next steps on the spot.
Illustrates: the power of a broad personal network to quickly understand the breadth of possible solutions, including the practical downsides people omit in public accounts.

## Reading widely: Larson's annual reading list

Larson makes an effort to read 10 to 20 industry-relevant works each year — not necessarily new releases, just new to him — deliberately choosing topics he knows little about or arguments he initially disagrees with. Recent examples: Chris Miller's *Chip War*, Curry, Hsu, and Bergman's *Building Green Software*, Kent Beck's *Tidy First?*, and Flyvbjerg and Gardner's *How Big Things Get Done*. Each stocked away ideas that might apply to future problems.
Illustrates: the "read widely" motion — ongoing reading that broadens thinking before any specific strategy needs it.

## Reading narrowly: AI agents strategy

When Larson recently started a strategy related to AI agents, the first thing he did was read Chip Huyen's survey *AI Engineering* (O'Reilly, 2024). It didn't answer his problems directly, but it mapped the option space and pointed to further references.
Illustrates: the "read narrowly" motion — starting a new strategy with a survey of the specific topic.

## Nontext sources: Dan Na's conference talk

Larson notes that exploration reading doesn't have to be books — blog posts, essays, interview transcripts, and talks all count. He cites getting a lot out of Dan Na's talk "Pushing Through Friction".
Illustrates: broadening exploration inputs beyond books, and skimming or sampling liberally.

## The imperfect internal solution worth adopting

Sometimes an internal approach isn't ideal, but it's still the superior choice because it's already implemented and someone else maintains it. Over the long run, your strategy rides along as that owner fixes the rough edges that don't fit your needs perfectly.
Illustrates: why mining internal precedent can beat building the theoretically better external option.
