# Chapter 12: Bridging Theory and Practice — Examples

## Claire Hughes Johnson's short planning window at Stripe

In *Scaling People* (Stripe Press, 2023), Stripe executive Claire Hughes Johnson argues that planning expands to fill whatever timeline it's given, so she deliberately set annual planning to a window of just several weeks. Some teams treated that window as a hard boundary and stopped planning the moment it closed. The effective teams never stopped planning at all — they were planning before the window opened and kept planning after it ended.
Illustrates: artificial deadlines are forcing functions for drafts, not endpoints for thinking; treat the deadline as the start of refinement.

## The Security team and user-data access (Document 19-1)

A Security team had stalled on reducing access to user data and framed the stall as the result of limited executive engagement, which had left the team disengaged. When the author dug into the ergonomics of the problem with them, they realized they could significantly reduce unnecessary access to user data with no top-down support at all, simply by making the access workflows easier to use. This strategy appears as Document 19-1.
Illustrates: effective diagnosis trumps authority — strategy doesn't require executive sponsorship, and "no executive support" can be a misdiagnosis hiding levers you already control.

## The layoff with an indecisive CEO

The author had to conduct a layoff while the CEO refused to define a target cost reduction or specify how much each function (Engineering, Marketing, Sales) should contribute. Those two decisions would have made Engineering's approach obvious; without them, the work felt impossible, and the author was deeply frustrated. In hindsight he came to see missing decisions as the norm in organizations rather than the exception.
Illustrates: key decisions you can't force are routine; strategy must work around missing decisions rather than treating them as showstoppers.

## Private equity strategy that blocks on a named missing decision (Document 18-1)

The strategy for navigating private equity ownership handles a missing decision by acknowledging it explicitly and blocking only the one part of execution that depends on it. Other parts of the plan, such as changing how roles are backfilled, went ahead to address the broader cost problem.
Illustrates: acknowledge what's missing and move forward where you can — partial progress beats blocking everything or pointing fingers.

## Top-down blockchain adoption during the crypto boom

Adopting high-hype technologies, such as blockchain during the crypto boom, often arrives as top-down direction from company leadership. Engineering may disagree but is obligated to align, and the engineering leader rolling it out often doesn't believe in it either — making it hard for them to explain a "strategy" they don't understand themselves. What lands on teams is a policy with no diagnosis or strategic thinking behind it.
Illustrates: why bad strategy (bare policy) exists, and why the survival move is writing your own private strategy that treats the mandate as fixed context in its diagnosis.

## The LLM ecosystem as a pocket of chaos (Chapter 17 pointer)

The chapter points to Chapter 17's case of plotting a company's path through the rapidly evolving LLM ecosystem as one example of a chaotic environment, alongside rapid hiring or layoffs, departures of load-bearing senior leaders, and slowing core-business revenue prompting scrambles toward a new business. In such periods, strategy authors may only be able to protect capacity in two-week chunks before the next critical initiative appears, versus stable periods where a strategy might run for several years with little deviation.
Illustrates: strategies don't require stable environments, only an accurate diagnosis of how unstable the environment is.
