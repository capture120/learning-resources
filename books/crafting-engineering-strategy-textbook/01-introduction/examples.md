# Chapter 1: Introduction — Examples

## Uber's service migration under a support avalanche

Larson's "first big dose of strategy" came while managing the team responsible for Uber's service migration (covered later as Document 16-1). The team was simultaneously and desperately trying to survive an accelerating avalanche of inbound support requests.
Illustrates: strategy shows up in the middle of operational crisis, not just in executive offsites — it was happening whether or not anyone called it strategy.

## Strategy everywhere: Stripe's acquisition of Index and Calm's product-engineering focus

After Uber, Larson saw strategy at every company: Stripe's acquisition of Index (Document 22-4) and Calm's decision to focus on being a product engineering company (Document 21-1). Both strategies appear later in the book.
Illustrates: strategy is pervasive across very different companies and decision types (M&A integration, organizational identity).

## The recurring monolith-decomposition problem

Some strategy problems recurred at every company Larson joined — notably deciding how to decompose monolithic codebases (Document 20-1).
Illustrates: certain engineering strategy problems are near-universal, which is part of why a structured, reusable approach pays off.

## Anonymized sensitive strategies: private equity and customer data access

More than half the book's strategies name real companies (Stripe, Uber, Calm); the rest are anonymized because they cover sensitive topics or expose internal information better kept private — how to work with private equity ownership (Document 18-1) or how to manage access to customer data (Document 19-1). Larson worked hard to remain honest in both sorts of examples, even when omitting some details out of respect for the companies and individuals involved.
Illustrates: the book's commitment to grounding strategy in real, direct experience rather than abstraction, even when confidentiality requires anonymization.

## A good diagnosis: work-in-progress is sinking the sprints

Example diagnosis given to define Rumelt's first pillar: "high work-in-progress is preventing us from finishing any tasks, so we are increasingly behind each sprint." It names a root cause and a mechanism, not just a symptom.
Illustrates: a diagnosis is a theory of the challenge that identifies root causes.

## A real guiding policy versus a fake one

Example of a genuine guiding policy: "only hire for the most urgent team; do not spread hires across all teams" — it explicitly trades breadth for focus. Counterexample: "working harder to get it done" is not a guiding policy because it implies no tradeoff; the relevant guiding policy there might be "work folks hard and expect high attrition."
Illustrates: guiding policies are typically tradeoffs, and a policy without a tradeoff should make you suspicious.

## The executive candidate who is "strategic, not in the weeds"

Anyone who has participated in executive hiring, Larson notes, has probably interviewed candidates who described strategic thinking as a personal strength; those candidates often draw a line between directing how work should be done and being in the weeds of actually doing it. The pattern is common enough to reveal that many people view strategy as fundamentally intellectual — about how things ought to work rather than how they actually do.
Illustrates: the misconception that strategy is intellectual rather than also mechanical; effective strategy depends on the nuances of how things actually work in practice.

## Smart people failing at "simple" strategy problems

Larson's recurring doubt while writing was that the book's ideas were too obvious to write down. Each time he considered abandoning it, he encountered another case of some of the smartest people he'd ever known struggling unsuccessfully with a strategy problem others would describe as quite simple.
Illustrates: in Larson's experience, engineering strategies fail for very mundane reasons (executives assuming the strategy will roll itself out, forgetting to validate the details), not intricate errors — both avoidable with a bit of structure, so the "obvious" structure is worth writing down.
