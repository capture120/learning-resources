# Chapter 3: Who Gets to Do Strategy? — Examples

## Strategy archaeology (Vernor Vinge's "software archaeologists")

Vernor Vinge's novel *A Deepness in the Sky* (Tor Books, 1999) coined "software archaeologists": people who build new functionality by cobbling together millennia of scraps of existing software. Larson borrows the image for "take five, then synthesize," calling it strategy archaeology. By simply recording how past decisions were actually made, you make the present easier to understand and the future easier to influence — no new authority required.

Illustrates: an engineer without mandate power can do real strategy by excavating and naming the organization's implicit, already-operating strategy.

## The Karpman Drama Triangle

Larson notes that when a diagnosis seems obvious to him and yet people still disagree, the cause is sometimes that he is simply wrong. When he has been obviously wrong about things he understands well, it has usually been because he viewed the situation through the Karpman Drama Triangle, mapping everyone involved onto the roles of persecutor, rescuer, and victim. That framing distorts the diagnosis even when the underlying domain knowledge is sound.

Illustrates: a self-check for diagnosis errors — persistent disagreement with an "obvious" diagnosis may signal you've dramatized the situation rather than diagnosed it.

## Stripe's acquisition of Index: the Java stack compromise

When Stripe acquired Index (covered in the book's Document 22-4), there was internal debate over how to deal with Index's Java-based technology stack. The debate ended in a compromise nobody particularly liked: defer the decision until after the initial release launched. Despite the dissatisfaction, everyone understood the conflicting priorities the compromise had to balance.

Illustrates: a genuinely complex tradeoff, where disagreement persists but is legitimate — distinct from poor-judgment disagreement, and acceptably resolved by an unsatisfying compromise.

## Calm's product engineering strategy

Calm's strategy to focus on being a product engineering organization (Document 21-1) confronted disagreement driven by poor judgment rather than complex tradeoffs. Its diagnosis acknowledged directly that "most of our disagreements stem around adopting new technologies or rewriting existing components into new technology stacks." In that environment, the strategy document itself served as a tool to educate colleagues about the tradeoffs they were making when they pushed for new technologies or rewrites.

Illustrates: when disagreement stems from poor technical judgment or self-interest, the strategy's job shifts to making the hidden tradeoffs explicit and teaching them.

## Private equity ownership and the unknown layoff

A strategy for dealing with new private equity ownership (Documents 18-1 and 18-2) faced a missing upstream decision: a layoff seemed likely, but its size was unknown. Rather than stalling until ownership decided, the strategy accepted the ambiguity as a fact and worked around it, adopting policies to reduce cost growth by shifting the organization's seniority mix. It also explicitly recognized that once reduction targets became clear, additional actions would follow.

Illustrates: never let a missing strategy elsewhere block your own — fold the ambiguity into the diagnosis, act on what's robust now, and plan the follow-up for when clarity arrives.

## Early LLM adoption: when engineers aren't the experts

Engineering organizations are usually right to believe they know the most about engineering, but the early adoption of large language models (Document 17-1) is a counterexample: engineers didn't know much about the new technology either, so outside perspectives genuinely helped. Similarly, a product manager who used to be an engineer may carry significant relevant engineering experience.

Illustrates: people outside the engineering organization can legitimately do engineering strategy, especially in novel domains where engineering's usual expertise advantage disappears.
