# Chapter 5: Measure What You Want to Improve

Metrics are among an engineer's highest-leverage tools: they measure progress, but more importantly they *drive* it. The metric you choose (and the ones you deliberately refuse to measure) shapes what work gets prioritized and what behavior a team exhibits, so picking the right one deserves real investment. Beyond goal metrics, instrument everything operationally, internalize baseline numbers for fast estimation, and stay skeptical of data integrity — bad data is worse than no data.

## Core Principles

- **Measure your progress or you cannot improve it.** Echoing Peter Drucker ("If you can't measure it, you can't improve it"), a defined metric is the only way to know consistently whether a change achieved its objective. Without measurement you proceed on intuition alone with no way to check whether that intuition is correct. Teams routinely ship features and celebrate without any mechanism to verify the product actually got better.

- **Good metrics do four jobs.** (1) They focus you on the right things by confirming that changes achieve your objectives. (2) Visualized over time, they guard against regressions — like a regression test, but system-wide (a signup-rate dashboard catches the Internet Explorer bug you never test for). (3) They can drive forward progress, as with Box's performance ratchet. (4) They let you measure your effectiveness over time and compare the leverage of a task against alternatives, using your historical improvement rate as the yardstick.

- **The choice of metric dramatically shapes behavior.** The right metric works as a North Star aligning the team; the wrong one produces ineffective or counterproductive effort. Rewarding "bugs fixed" incentivizes less rigorous testing; measuring "hours worked" incentivizes unsustainable burnout; tracking raw click-through rate rewards short clicks on results that don't satisfy the user. Selecting what to measure is as important as measuring itself.

- **What you don't measure matters too.** Deliberately not tracking a metric can be a strategic decision. Zappos refused to measure call-center "average handle time" because it would push reps to rush customers off the phone, and that refusal enabled the customer-service culture behind its growth to $1B+ in revenue.

- **For goals, be selective; for operations, measure everything.** These complement each other: carefully choose the few core metrics you optimize, but instrument your systems as broadly as possible so you understand what's actually happening. Without instrumentation you fly blind and can only guess at what's wrong, like Twitter in its first two years or the HealthCare.gov launch. The easier it is to add a metric, the more often you will.

- **Internalized baseline numbers are a cheap substitute for measurement.** You often don't need accurate numbers to decide well — just ballpark ones. Knowing key latencies, industry benchmark rates, and your own system's numbers lets you estimate a design's performance without building it, spot anomalies, and identify underinvested areas via back-of-the-envelope math. This gives the benefits of metrics at much lower cost.

- **Be skeptical about data integrity.** Metrics get their power from settling arguments: the right metric slices through office politics, philosophical biases, and product debates, quickly resolving discussions — and the wrong metric ends debate just as decisively, with disastrous results. All data can be abused: people interpret it the way they want, confuse correlation with causality, or pick easy-but-irrelevant metrics that tell a false story. Metrics-related code tends to be less robust than feature code, for three reasons engineers underinvest: (1) under tight deadlines, metrics — whose importance only shows up after launch — get deprioritized; (2) it's much easier to test and validate a feature's interactions than to verify that a plausible-looking metric (like page views) is accurate; (3) engineers assume well-unit-tested metrics code means accurate metrics, despite possible system-level errors or wrong assumptions. Errors creep in throughout the pipeline: unmeasured code paths when there are multiple entry points, data dropped over the network, mismatched definitions/units/standards when merging data from multiple teams, bugs in processing and transformation, and dashboards that are hard to unit test — so visual inspection can't tell you whether "1,024 views" or "3.1% conversion" is right. Untrustworthy data in decision-making is *negative* leverage — the only thing worse than no data is the illusion of having the right data.

## Key Concepts & Frameworks

- **Long click vs. short click (Google).** A long click: the user clicks a search result and doesn't return to the results page, or stays on the result a long time — meaning the query was satisfied. A short click: the user bounces right back to try another result — signaling unhappiness. The long click-through rate, not raw click-through rate, is Google's best indicator of user happiness, and it proved versatile enough to power name detection and universal search ranking.

- **Performance ratchet (Box, via Sam Schillace).** A metric threshold for latency or other key indicators. Any change that would push the metric past the ratchet cannot deploy until it's optimized or another feature is improved by a counterbalancing amount. Each system-level improvement lowers the ratchet, so performance only trends in the right direction — like a mechanical ratchet allowing rotation one way only.

- **Three criteria for choosing a metric.** Pick metrics that are: (1) *impact-maximizing* — optimizing it produces the greatest and most sustainable impact for the team; (2) *actionable* — its movements can be causally traced to the team's efforts (e.g., via A/B testing); (3) *responsive yet robust* — it updates quickly enough to give feedback (weekly actives beat monthly actives as a leading indicator) but is averaged or windowed enough that external noise doesn't drown the signal (hourly/daily response-time averages beat per-minute readings).

- **Economic denominator (Jim Collins, Good to Great).** The single ratio — "profit per x" — that, if systematically increased, would have the greatest and most sustainable impact on the economic engine. For engineering: one unifying core metric (products sold, rentals booked, content generated) that lets you compare disparate projects and resolve externalities, like whether a performance team should cut a feature to improve load time.

- **Vanity metrics vs. actionable metrics (Eric Ries, The Lean Startup).** Vanity metrics are gross numbers (page views/month, total registered users, total paying customers) that can rise for reasons unrelated to the team's work quality, such as momentum from past press. Actionable metrics (signup conversion rate, percent of registered users active weekly) can be traced back to specific changes.

- **Metric pairs that incentivize different behavior:**
  - Hours worked/week vs. productivity/week — near 70-hour weeks, marginal productivity per added hour drops sharply, errors and burnout rise, and overtime is followed by equal "undertime."
  - Click-through rate vs. long click-through rate.
  - Average response time vs. 95th/99th percentile — studies by Google, Yahoo, Amazon, and Facebook show response speed matters to users. The average suits cutting aggregate compute costs via general infrastructure work; the tail percentiles force hunting worst-case behaviors and reflect power users, who have the most data and activity and are costliest to serve.
  - Bugs fixed vs. bugs outstanding — rewarding fixes invites planting easy bugs; tracking outstanding bugs doesn't.
  - Total registered users vs. weekly growth rate of registered users (new users that week / total users) — the rate shows whether growth is slowing; the gross number can rise on a one-time press bump.
  - Weekly active users vs. weekly active rate by age of cohort — the fraction of users still active in week *n* after signup reveals whether product changes hurt engagement of new cohorts, which gross active counts can mask.

- **Instrumentation mindset.** Build the software equivalent of a pilot's cockpit instruments: dashboards surfacing key health metrics with drill-down, plus flexible tools that make adding a new metric trivial — necessary because many measurement questions are exploratory and you don't know ahead of time everything you'll want to measure (Etsy's "measure anything, measure everything" with Graphite + StatsD: one line of code defines a counter or timer and auto-generates a composable time-series graph). Supporting metrics explain the story behind the core metric — to optimize signup rate, break it down by referral type and landing page; to optimize response time, decompose into database, cache, rendering, and network time.

- **Jeff Dean's latency numbers every engineer should know:** L1 cache reference 0.5 ns; branch mispredict 5 ns; L2 cache reference 7 ns; mutex lock/unlock 100 ns; main memory reference 100 ns; compress 1K bytes with Snappy 10 μs; send 2K bytes over 1 Gbps network 20 μs; read 1 MB sequentially from memory 250 μs; round trip within a datacenter 500 μs; disk seek 10 ms; read 1 MB sequentially from network 10 ms; read 1 MB sequentially from disk 30 ms; packet CA → Netherlands → CA 150 ms. Implications: memory reads are ~120x faster than disk and ~40x faster than a 1 Gbps network for 1 MB; cheap 2x compression can halve network traffic for only ~50% more latency.

## Actionable Practices

- Before starting work, ask: Is there a way to measure the progress of what I'm doing? If a task doesn't move a core metric, is it worth doing — or is a key metric missing?
- Define a metric tied to each goal (weekly active rate, response time, click-through rate, etc.) and measure every change's impact against it; don't ship and celebrate without a measurement mechanism.
- Use your historical improvement rate (e.g., 1% per week on a metric) as a bar: a task expected to beat that rate for its time investment is higher-leverage; deprioritize tasks below it.
- Set aggressive targets when you need step changes: a goal of "under 400ms" for a multi-second page forces cutting features, re-architecting, or rewriting a bottleneck in a faster language rather than chasing small wins. The magnitude of the goal changes the work you do.
- Adopt a performance ratchet: block deploys that push key indicators past a threshold unless counterbalanced, and lower the threshold after each system-level improvement.
- Choose metrics that maximize impact, are actionable (causally attributable via A/B tests), and balance responsiveness with robustness (average noisy metrics over an hour or day).
- Prefer rates and cohort measures over gross totals: weekly growth rate over registered-user count; weekly active rate by age of cohort over weekly active users.
- Use 95th/99th percentile response times when user experience is the goal; use averages when cutting aggregate server cost is the goal.
- Decide deliberately what NOT to measure when a metric would incentivize the wrong behavior (Zappos and handle time).
- Apply metric choice to personal goals too: Lau replaced "write three hours per day" with "write 1,000 words per day," which shifted him from premature editing to drafting and significantly increased his writing pace.
- Even fuzzy goals can be quantified over time — consistent bug fixing shows up in customer complaints, app-store ratings, or survey-based quality scores. Hard-to-measure does not mean not worth measuring.
- Instrument everything operationally: dashboards for signup rates, latency, error rates, top referrers, and data-store performance; correlate metric graphs with deployment times to spot bad releases quickly.
- Measure supporting metrics that decompose your core metric (signup rate by referral type and landing page; response time by database/cache/rendering/network layer; search quality via click-through rates, result counts, searches per session, time to first click).
- You don't need Google-scale systems: use open-source tools (Graphite, StatsD, InfluxDB, Ganglia, Nagios, Munin) or managed ones (New Relic, AppDynamics).
- Internalize numbers for your domain: registered/weekly/monthly actives, requests per second, data stored and written daily, server counts, endpoint throughput, traffic growth rate, average page load time, traffic distribution by product area and by browser/device/OS. Write small benchmark programs for your key building blocks; get other numbers by talking to teams in similar areas or mining historical data. The trigger for this upfront investment: when you catch yourself wondering which design is more performant, whether a number is in the right ballpark, how much better a feature could do, or whether a metric looks normal, ask whether the question is recurring — if so, spend the time to gather and internalize the relevant numbers or benchmarks.
- Use industry benchmarks to size opportunities — e.g., MailChimp's data shows emails to social networks/online communities average roughly 22% open rates and 3.9% click-through rates; compare your own numbers to spot high-leverage fixes. Typical landing-page conversion rates, invite-email acceptance rates, and daily/weekly/monthly active rates of similar products likewise highlight underinvested areas.
- Use back-of-the-envelope math from known latencies to evaluate designs before building: e.g., disk-persisted writes cap at ~100/sec (10 ms seek) while memory-cached reads of ≤1 MB objects run ≥4,000/sec, so reads outpace writes ~40x — plan to parallelize or batch writes.
- Sanity-check data like a good math student: ask "does that roughly make sense?", compare against intuition, derive the same number from a different direction, and measure implied properties to check consistency. Internalized useful numbers are the raw material for many of these sanity checks.
- Protect data integrity: log liberally (decide usefulness later, as Netflix does with Cassandra); build tools to inspect what's logged during development; write end-to-end integration tests over the analytics pipeline; examine collected data early even if analysis comes later; cross-validate metrics by computing them multiple ways; dig into off-looking numbers immediately.
- Treat measurement and analysis as part of the product development workflow, not a bolt-on — logging bugs found weeks later waste weeks of iteration.

## Key Takeaways

- Measure your progress: you can't tell which effort is well spent without measurement.
- Choose your top-level metric carefully: different metrics incentivize different behaviors, so decide which behaviors you want first.
- Instrument your systems: the more complex the system, the more instrumentation you need to avoid flying blind — and the easier instrumenting is, the more you'll do it.
- Know your numbers: memorize or keep handy the numbers that benchmark progress and enable back-of-the-envelope calculations.
- Prioritize data integrity: bad data is worse than no data, because it makes you confidently wrong.
