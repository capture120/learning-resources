# Chapter 37: Never Stop Learning

This chapter expands the tenth promise of The Oath: "I will never stop learning and improving my craft." Its single idea: a programmer's education never ends — the industry changes too fast for it to, and keeping up is the programmer's own responsibility, paid for with their own time and money if necessary.

## Learn a New Language Every Year

You should learn a new language every year; a good programmer should know a dozen languages or so. Crucially, not a dozen varieties of the same language (not just C, C++, Java, and C#) but languages from many different families.

The families he names explicitly:
- A statically typed language (Java, C#)
- A procedural language (C, Pascal)
- A logic language (Prolog)
- A stack language (Forth)
- A dynamically typed language (Ruby)
- A functional language (Clojure, Haskell)

## Breadth Beyond Languages

Languages are not enough. Know several different frameworks, several different design methodologies, and several different development processes. You need not be an expert in all of them, but expose yourself to them at significantly more than a cursory level. The list of things worth exposure is virtually endless, because the industry's rapid change is likely to continue for some time — you have to keep up with it.

## Keep Up Through Continuous Input

Keep reading books and blogs. Keep watching videos. Keep going to conferences, user groups, and training courses. Keep learning. Learning is an ongoing habit, not an event.

## Pay Attention to the Treasured Works of the Past

The books of the '60s, '70s, and '80s are wonderful sources of insight and information. Don't fall into the trap of thinking all that old stuff is out of date — not much in our industry actually goes out of date. Respect the effort and accomplishments of those who came before you, and study their advice and conclusions.

## Your Career Is Your Responsibility, Not Your Employer's

Don't fall into the trap of thinking it is your employer's job to train you. This is your career; it is your job to learn, and your job to figure out what to learn. If your employer buys books and sends you to conferences and training, take full advantage. If not, pay for them yourself.

## Put In the Time

Plan to spend time on learning every week. You owe your employer 35 to 40 hours per week; you owe your career another 10 to 20. That is what a professional does: a professional puts in the time to groom and maintain their career — 50 to 60 hours total per week, mostly at work, but a lot at home too.

## In practice

- Schedule recurring weekly time (10–20 hours) for deliberate learning outside job duties.
- Pick your next language from a family you don't know (logic, stack, functional, etc.), not another dialect of one you do.
- Aim for more-than-cursory exposure to unfamiliar frameworks, design methodologies, and development processes — expertise not required.
- Read classic computing literature; don't dismiss advice merely because it's decades old.
- Treat employer-funded training as a bonus, not a precondition: buy the book or course yourself if no one else will.
- Choose what to learn yourself; don't wait to be assigned learning.
