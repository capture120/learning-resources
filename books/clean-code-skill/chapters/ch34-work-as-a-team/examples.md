# Chapter 34: Work as a Team — Examples

This chapter contains no code examples. Its argument is carried by analogies and anecdotes, retold below.

### The fallen soccer player

Martin opens with a soccer team moving the ball downfield. A player trips and falls mid-play. His teammates step into the breach, cover the hole, and keep the ball moving down the field. The analogy anchors the chapter's promise: on a software team, when one member goes down for any reason, the others must be able to fill the hole and keep the project on track. That mutual coverage is what justifies calling the group a team.

### Sailors on a navy vessel

Each sailor on a navy vessel has a specific job, yet each also knows how to do someone else's job. The reason is battle: a sailor might go down in the heat of combat, and another must step in to keep the ship fighting. The lesson is deliberate cross-training — on a software team, every member should know enough about the others' work to take it over. Knowledge silos are a single point of failure.

### The Agile-coaching team rooms

In the early 2000s Martin owned a company that helped organizations adopt Agile development by sending instructors and coaches on-site. Before each engagement, the company told managers to rearrange office space so the teams to be coached worked together in their own team rooms. More than once, before the coaches even arrived, managers reported the teams were already much more productive — purely from working together in the same room. The anecdote demonstrates that physical co-location is itself a productivity intervention, independent of any process change.

### The post-pandemic remote reality

Martin notes he wrote the chapter's first version in early 2021, as COVID-19 waned; the pandemic left in its wake a large number of software teams that work remotely. He concedes remote work can never quite match a shared room — screens are not as good as in-person presence — but says today's collaboration tools are very good, so remote teams should use them fully: a virtual team room with everyone's face in view and audio channels as open as feasible, screen-sharing for pairing and mobbing, and at least six contiguous shared hours per day. The lesson is to recreate the team-room conditions electronically rather than abandon them.

### The windscreen effect (yelling at other drivers)

Martin asks: have you ever noticed how easy it is to yell at another driver from inside your car? Behind a windscreen, other people become fools, imbeciles, even enemies — they are easy to dehumanize. He names this the windscreen effect and observes it also happens, to a lesser degree, behind computer screens. The remedy is periodic physical reunion: he recommends the team gather in one room for a week each quarter, because it is very hard to dehumanize someone you ate lunch with and physically collaborated with two weeks ago. The anecdote anchors the rule that remote teams need recurring in-person time to stay congealed.
