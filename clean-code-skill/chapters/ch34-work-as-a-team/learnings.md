# Chapter 34: Work as a Team

A software team is only a team if its members can cover for each other. The chapter elaborates one promise: "I will continuously ensure that others can cover for me and that I can cover for them." Collaborative programming spreads knowledge so the loss of one person cannot take an entire segment of knowledge with it; shared rooms and overlapping hours keep members seeing and interacting with each other frequently.

## Cover for each other

Rule: Continuously ensure that others can cover for you and that you can cover for them. If a team member goes down for any reason, the rest must be able to step into the breach and keep the project on track. That capability is what makes a group of programmers a *team* rather than a collection of individuals — like soccer players covering for a fallen teammate, or navy sailors who each know how to do someone else's job because in battle a sailor might go down.

## Collaborative Programming

Rule: Spread knowledge through the team by working together — pair or mob. Segregation of knowledge into silos is extremely detrimental: losing one individual can mean losing an entire segment of knowledge, and siloed members lack the context to understand each other, so they often wind up talking past each other. Martin claims there is hardly any better way to improve a team's productivity than collaborative programming, because a team that knows the deep connections among the work being done is far more productive than a group of silos.

## Open/Virtual Office

Rule: Team members should see and interact with each other very frequently; the best way is to put them in a room together. Martin's Agile-coaching company told client managers to rearrange office space so the teams to be coached worked together in their own team rooms, and more than once managers reported productivity gains from the room alone. For remote teams (acknowledged as a post-pandemic reality): remote work can never be quite as productive as a shared physical room, but modern collaboration tools are very good — so use them. Create a **virtual team room**: keep everyone's face in view and everyone's audio channel as open as feasible, sustaining the illusion of a team room with everyone in it. When pairing or mobbing remotely over shared screens, keep faces and audio running so you can see each other while collaborating on code.

## Overlapping hours

Rule: Remote teams should try as hard as they can to maintain the same working hours. This gets very difficult with a huge East–West spread, so keep the number of time zones per team as small as possible, and try very hard to secure at least six contiguous hours per day when everyone can be together in the virtual team room.

## The windscreen effect

Rule: Get the team together in the same physical room several times per year — Martin recommends one week each quarter. The reasoning is the **windscreen effect**: behind a car windscreen it is easy to see other drivers as fools or enemies — to dehumanize them — and the same happens, to a lesser degree, behind computer screens. It is very hard to fall into that trap with someone you ate lunch with and physically collaborated with two weeks ago. Periodic in-person time helps the team congeal and maintain itself as a team.

## In practice

- Before going deep on a component alone, ask who else on the team could maintain it if you disappeared; pair or mob to spread that knowledge.
- Prefer pairing/mobbing over solo work followed by hand-offs; treat collaboration as a productivity tool, not overhead.
- Avoid becoming (or creating) a single point of knowledge; rotate people across areas of the code.
- If remote, run a persistent virtual team room: cameras on, audio open as feasible, screens shared while programming together.
- Keep team time zones few; protect at least six contiguous shared hours daily.
- Schedule in-person weeks roughly quarterly to counter screen-induced dehumanization.
- When friction arises with a remote colleague, recognize the windscreen effect before assuming bad intent.
