# Chapter 5: How to Lead a Team — Examples, Case Studies, and Anecdotes

All examples come from Chapter 5 of "Software Engineering at Google" (written by Brian Fitzpatrick). Each is retold in plain language with the setup, what happened, and the lesson the book draws.

## The boat without a captain

**Setup.** The chapter opens with a metaphor rather than an anecdote, but it frames everything that follows.

**The idea.** A boat without a captain is nothing more than a floating waiting room; unless someone grabs the rudder and starts the engine, it drifts with the current. A piece of software is the same: if no one pilots it, the engineers burn valuable time sitting around waiting for something to happen, or worse, writing code nobody needs.

**Lesson.** Every team needs a leader, whether or not anyone was formally appointed. If you want to steer your project rather than drift, you must learn to navigate.

## Case Study: Influencing Without Authority — Jeff Dean

**Setup.** "Influence without authority" means getting people who do not report to you (often outside your organization entirely) to do work you believe needs to be done. The book calls it one of the most powerful leadership traits you can develop.

**What happened.** Jeff Dean, a senior engineering fellow and possibly the best-known engineer inside Google, formally led only a small fraction of Google's engineering organization. Yet his influence on technical decisions and direction reached across the entire engineering organization and beyond, amplified by his writing and speaking outside the company.

**Lesson.** Formal authority and actual influence are separate things. Reputation, technical credibility, and communication can give a person far more directional power than their org-chart position suggests.

## Case Study: Influencing Without Authority — The Data Liberation Front and Google Takeout

**Setup.** The author (Brian Fitzpatrick) started a Google team called The Data Liberation Front with fewer than half a dozen engineers. Their goal: let users export their data out of Google products. There was no executive directive requiring products to participate.

**What happened.** The tiny team got more than 50 Google products to export user data through a product they launched called Google Takeout. Hundreds of engineers across the company contributed to the effort voluntarily. They achieved this by (1) identifying a strategic need for the company, (2) showing how the effort linked to Google's mission and existing priorities, and (3) building a tool that made it quick and easy for product teams to integrate with Takeout.

**Lesson.** A concrete recipe for influence without authority: tie your cause to the company's mission and priorities, then lower the cost of participation so that joining is easy.

## Apples and bananas: why management feels unproductive

**Setup.** Engineers who become managers commonly end busy days feeling "I didn't do a damned thing today," because they no longer produce visible artifacts like code or closed bugs.

**The idea.** It is like spending years counting the apples you picked each day, then switching to a job growing bananas, and lamenting every evening that you picked no apples while ignoring the flourishing banana trees beside you.

**Lesson.** Management output is real but harder to quantify and pays off over a longer timeline. Making the team happy and productive is the job; do not measure a banana-growing job in apples.

## Steve Vinter's advice: "Resist the urge to manage"

**Setup.** New managers catch a "disease": they forget the awful things their own managers did to them and start doing the same things to their reports, with symptoms like micromanaging, ignoring low performers, and hiring pushovers.

**What happened.** When the author first became a manager at Google, engineering director Steve Vinter gave him the best advice he received: "Above all, resist the urge to manage." Newly minted managers feel they must actively "manage" people because that is what managers do, and it typically ends in disaster.

**Lesson.** The cure is servant leadership: serve the team's technical and social health instead of controlling its members.

## Jerry and the 4:40 p.m. appointment

**Setup.** A new engineer named Jerry joined the author's team. Jerry's previous manager at another company demanded he be at his desk from 9:00 to 5:00 every day and assumed absence meant slacking.

**What happened.** On his first day, Jerry approached the author at 4:40 p.m. and nervously apologized for needing to leave 15 minutes early for an unmovable appointment. The author smiled and said: "Look, as long as you get your job done, I don't care what time you leave the office." Jerry stared blankly, nodded, and left. He always got his work done and never needed watching.

**Lesson.** Treat employees like adults and they live up to the trust. If employees are so uninterested in their jobs that they need babysitting to be convinced to work, the babysitting is not the problem; the real problem is who you hired or how you lead them.

## Blameless postmortems at Google

**Setup.** Google wants teams to take risks, and risk taking requires knowing that failure is survivable. Failure that affects customers is the least desirable kind, so it gets the most structure for learning.

**What happened.** After every major production failure, Google performs a postmortem: a document capturing the chain of events that led to the failure plus a set of steps to prevent recurrence. The exercise explicitly forbids finger-pointing and blame, and it is not meant to add bureaucratic checks. The goal is to focus on the core problem and fix it once and for all. The book calls the process very difficult but effective and even cathartic.

**Lesson.** Institutionalized, blameless failure analysis converts expensive failures into durable organizational learning and preserves the psychological safety that makes risk taking possible.

## "Sometimes you get to be the tooth fairy, other times you have to be the dentist"

**Setup.** Early in the author's management career at Google, it came time to hand out bonus letters. He grinned and told his manager, "I love being a manager!"

**What happened.** His manager, a long-time industry veteran, replied without missing a beat: "Sometimes you get to be the tooth fairy, other times you have to be the dentist."

**Lesson.** Management is not just handing out rewards. The hard half of the job is the painful work, above all dealing with people who are not meeting expectations, and you cannot opt out of it.

## The cost of ignoring a low performer

**Setup.** The book describes leaders who did everything right building strong teams, only to watch those teams fail to excel and eventually fall apart because of one or two low performers. Google's Site Reliability Engineering team has a motto, "Hope is not a strategy," and nowhere is hope more overused than with a low performer.

**What happened.** The typical leader grits their teeth, averts their eyes, and hopes the low performer magically improves or leaves. Neither happens. Meanwhile high performers waste time carrying the low performer, morale drains away, high performers leave, and new ones will not join. Eventually the team consists entirely of low performers because they are the only ones who cannot leave of their own volition.

**Lesson.** Deal with low performers immediately so you can help them "up or out." The book's coaching recipe: imagine helping a limping person learn to walk, then jog, then run alongside the team. Set a specific time frame (about two months) with small, incremental, measurable goals; meet weekly; make expectations for each milestone explicit. Handled early, the person often just needs encouragement or direction. Handled late, the relationship is too sour and you are too frustrated to help. Either the person acknowledges the gap and quits, or determination kicks in and they meet expectations; both outcomes beat limbo.

## Jake, Katie, and Pablo: ignoring the human element

**Setup.** Jake, an engineer, had his first child. Jake and his colleague Katie had worked together for years, both remotely and in the same office, so in the weeks after the baby arrived Jake worked from home. Productivity did not suffer and Katie was completely fine with the arrangement.

**What happened.** Their manager Pablo, who worked in a different office, discovered Jake was working from home most of the week and got upset that Jake was not in the office with Katie, despite zero impact on productivity. Jake explained that he was just as productive and that working from home was much easier on him and his wife for a few weeks. Pablo's response: "Dude, people have kids all the time. You need to go into the office." Jake, normally mild-mannered, was enraged and lost a great deal of respect for Pablo.

**Lesson.** A manager who ignores the human side of the job destroys trust for no gain. Pablo had cheap alternatives: let the arrangement continue since nothing was suffering, or negotiate one or two office days a week. A little empathy would have kept Jake happy; the absence of it caused lasting damage.

## Free office supplies and Tech Stops: trust made physical

**Setup.** An illustration of the antipattern "treat your team like children," inverted to show what trust looks like in practice.

**What happened.** Google stocks cabinets with office supplies (pens, notebooks, and so on) free for the taking, and its IT department runs self-service "Tech Stops" full of computer accessories (power supplies, cables, mice, USB drives) that anyone could easily walk off with. Because employees are trusted to check items out, they feel a responsibility to do the right thing. Outsiders react in horror, assuming Google hemorrhages money to theft.

**Lesson.** Some loss is possible, but the cost of a few pens and USB cables is far smaller than the cost of a workforce that behaves like children or wastes valuable time formally requesting cheap supplies. People tend to act the way you treat them.

## Bill Coughran and the space aliens

**Setup.** Part of the "Be a Zen master" pattern: a leader's reactions get amplified down the organization, so mediating your reactions and staying calm matters more the more people you lead.

**What happened.** Bill Coughran, an early Google VP of engineering, had mastered calm. No matter what blew up or how big the firestorm, Bill never panicked. He would fold one arm across his chest, rest his chin in his hand, and quietly ask questions about the problem, usually to a completely panicked engineer, which calmed the engineer and refocused them on solving the problem. Team members joked that if someone reported that space aliens had attacked 19 of the company's offices, Bill would reply, "Any idea why they didn't make it an even 20?"

**Lesson.** The leader is always on stage. A visibly calm leader keeps the team functional in a crisis; a visibly panicked one sends every gear below them spinning.

## The Eric Schmidt question: "Is there anything you need?"

**Setup.** Shortly after joining Google, the author had his first meeting with then-CEO Eric Schmidt. He had prepared a million defensive responses to hard questions and challenges.

**What happened.** At the end of the meeting Eric simply asked, "Is there anything you need?" The author sat dumbstruck and staring, completely unprepared. He made sure to have an answer ready the next time.

**Lesson.** "What do you need?" is a disarmingly powerful leadership question. Asked consistently (the book recommends ending every one-on-one with it), it surfaces what people need to be productive and happy, and eventually team members come prepared with their own lists.

## Three managers who remove roadblocks

**Setup.** Part of the "Remove roadblocks" pattern: teams often agree on direction but get stuck on obstacles that are nearly impossible for them yet easy for their leader.

**What happened.** Three short cases:
1. A team spent several weeks stuck on an obstacle with Google's legal department. When the team finally gave up and brought the problem to its manager, he solved it in under two hours simply because he knew the right person to contact.
2. A team could not get server resources allocated. Their manager, who kept in touch with teams across the company, got them exactly what they needed that same afternoon.
3. An engineer was stuck on an arcane piece of Java code. The manager was not a Java expert, but she connected the engineer to another engineer who knew exactly what the problem was.

**Lesson.** You do not need to know all the answers to remove roadblocks. In many cases, knowing the right person is more valuable than knowing the right answer.

## Mekka's happiness scale

**Setup.** Part of the "Track happiness" pattern, extending it beyond the office.

**What happened.** The authors' colleague Mekka opens his one-on-one meetings by asking each report to rate their happiness on a scale of 1 to 10. Reports often use the rating to discuss happiness both inside and outside work.

**Lesson.** A lightweight ritual can open the door to conversations about well-being that people would not start on their own. Sensitivity to personal situations explains productivity swings and builds the goodwill that later gets repaid during crunch times.

## Tim and the compliment sandwich

**Setup.** A "compliment sandwich" wraps criticism between two compliments to soften the blow (for example: "You're one of our smartest engineers. That said, your code is impossible to understand. But you've got great potential and a cool T-shirt collection."). The problem: most recipients hear only the compliments and miss the message that something must change. A colleague of the authors inherited an engineer named Tim from another manager who insisted Tim was impossible to work with and never responded to feedback.

**What happened.** The colleague sat in on the old manager's meetings with Tim and noticed the manager relied heavily on the compliment sandwich to avoid hurting Tim's feelings. When Tim joined the new team, the colleague instead told him plainly: the way he was interacting with the team was alienating and angering them, he needed to refine his communication skills to be effective, and the team was committed to helping him do it. No compliments, no candy-coating, but no meanness either, just the facts. Within weeks (plus a few refresher meetings), Tim's performance improved dramatically.

**Lesson.** Tim had never actually heard the criticism before; the sandwich buried it. Clear, direct, kind feedback works. Skip the compliment sandwich, but never substitute cruelty for clarity.

## Ben, Dean, and the trains

**Setup.** Ben managed an engineer called Dean who had extremely strong opinions and argued with the team about everything, from the team's mission down to the placement of a widget on a web page, always with the same vehemence, refusing to let anything slide.

**What happened.** After months of this, Ben needed Dean to understand the damage. Saying "Dean, stop being such a jerk" would have been disregarded entirely. Instead Ben crafted a metaphor: every decision is like a train coming through town. Jumping in front of a train stops it, slows everything down, and annoys the driver. A new train comes every 15 minutes; jump in front of every one and you spend all your time stopping trains, and eventually a driver angry enough will run you over. It is fine to stop *some* trains; pick only the ones that really matter. The metaphor injected humor and let Ben and Dean discuss both the team impact and the energy Dean was burning.

**Lesson.** Delivery determines whether feedback is heard or deflected. A recipient put on the defensive thinks about how to argue back, not how to change. A vivid, non-accusatory framing lets the person see the problem without needing to defend themselves.

## The truck and the ropes

**Setup.** A metaphor for the "Set clear goals" pattern.

**The idea.** Imagine the product is a big truck, and every team member holds a rope tied to its front. As they work, each pulls the truck in their own direction. If you want the truck to go north as fast as possible, everyone must pull north; people pulling every which way waste most of the collective effort.

**Lesson.** Teams without clear goals can still succeed, but they squander energy as each member pulls in a slightly different direction. A concise mission statement aligns the pulling, frees the leader from constant course correction, and lets the leader grant more autonomy.

## The gears of the org chart

**Setup.** A metaphor for why leaders must moderate their reactions.

**The idea.** Picture the organization chart as a chain of gears: the individual contributor is a tiny gear with a few teeth at one end, each manager above is a successively larger gear, and the CEO is a huge gear with hundreds of teeth. When a manager's gear makes one revolution, the individual's gear makes two or three. The CEO can make one small movement and send an employee at the end of a chain of six or seven gears spinning wildly.

**Lesson.** The higher you are, the more your smallest actions and moods are amplified below you, whether or not you intend it.

## The mother-in-law and the six children ("People Are Like Plants")

**Setup.** The author's wife is the youngest of six children. Her mother faced raising six very different kids, each needing different things.

**What happened.** Asked how she managed it, the mother-in-law said kids are like plants: some are like cacti and need little water but lots of sunshine, some are like African violets and need diffuse light and moist soil, others are like tomatoes and excel with a little fertilizer. Give all six identical water, light, and fertilizer and they get *equal* treatment, but odds are none gets what it actually *needs*.

**Lesson.** Team members are the same. The leader's job is to determine, per person, the right mix of motivation (for those in a rut) and direction (for those distracted or uncertain), give both to those adrift, and give neither to those who need neither, because unneeded motivation or direction just annoys people.

## The knife blade (mastery)

**Setup.** A metaphor within the intrinsic-motivation discussion, about the "mastery" component.

**The idea.** An employee's skills are like the blade of a knife. You can spend tens of thousands of dollars finding people with the sharpest skills, but use that knife for years without sharpening it and you end up with a dull, inefficient, sometimes useless blade.

**Lesson.** Continuous opportunities to learn and improve keep people motivated and keep the team sharp. (The book notes the flip side: sharper people are more marketable and can leave more easily, which is why tracking happiness matters.)

## The manager who forwards customer emails (purpose)

**Setup.** Autonomy and mastery cannot motivate someone doing work for no reason; people also need purpose. Many engineers are kept at arm's length from the positive effects of their products.

**What happened.** A Google manager on one of the company's smaller-impact products watches the email feedback the product receives. Whenever a customer writes about how the product helped them personally or helped their business, she immediately forwards the message to the engineering team.

**Lesson.** Making the purpose of the work visible not only motivates the team but frequently inspires engineers to think of ways to make the product even better.

## The chaos was always there

**Setup.** Part of the "Shield your team from chaos" tip. When the author first became a manager in the 1990s, he was taken aback by the sheer volume of uncertainty and organizational chaos in his company.

**What happened.** He asked another manager what had caused the company's sudden rockiness. The other manager laughed hysterically at his naivete: the chaos had always been there. The author's previous manager had shielded him and his whole team from it.

**Lesson.** Good leaders absorb organizational chaos so their teams can focus. Individual contributors on well-led teams never see how much insanity exists outside; when you become a leader, shielding your team from it becomes part of your job, while still sharing the information that genuinely matters to them.
